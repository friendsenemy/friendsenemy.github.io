Selling an Alaska House From Jail or Prison: One Statewide Corrections System, a Checkbox Power of Attorney and the Right Recording District

Alaska has no county jails: the state Department of Corrections holds people awaiting trial and people serving sentences, often in the same building, and moves them between facilities as cases change. That makes the paperwork simpler in one way and trickier in another, because the power of attorney has to be acknowledged before a notary inside a DOC facility and then recorded in the correct one of Alaska's 34 recording districts. Families in most states start by asking whether their relative is in "the jail" or "the prison," because the two are run by different governments with different rules. Alaska skips that question. The state has no counties, and its Department of Corrections operates both the pretrial lockups and the long-term institutions. The National Institute of Corrections lists Alaska as one of only six states with a unified system, alongside Connecticut, Delaware, Hawaii, Rhode Island and Vermont. Someone arrested in Anchorage, held at the Anchorage Correctional Complex, sentenced, and moved to Goose Creek or Spring Creek never leaves the same department.

For a house sale, that's mostly good news. One agency, one set of mail and visiting rules, and staff notaries in its institutions. The catch is movement. People get transferred as their cases progress, and a power of attorney that was mailed to one facility can arrive after the owner has been moved to another. This guide is built around getting one well-drafted document signed and acknowledged inside a DOC facility, then recorded in the right Alaska recording district, so the sale can go ahead without the owner. For the basics that apply everywhere, see our national guide to selling a house while incarcerated.

🏔️ One Department Holds Every Alaska Prisoner

As of the end of 2022, the NIC counted 4,778 people under Alaska state correctional jurisdiction, held in 13 state facilities, about 15 small locally operated jails, and private contract beds. The locally operated jails are small facilities in outlying communities that work under contract with the state. Anyone facing a real sentence should expect to end up in a DOC institution.

The DOC institutions a family is most likely to deal with:

  • 🏙️ Anchorage Correctional Complex, the East and West buildings that used to be called the Anchorage Jail and Cook Inlet Pre-Trial. Most people arrested in Anchorage start here.
  • 🌲 Mat-Su Pretrial Facility in Palmer and Wildwood Pretrial near Kenai, for arrests in the valley and on the peninsula.
  • 🏗️ Goose Creek Correctional Center at Point MacKenzie, the largest facility, and Spring Creek in Seward, the maximum-security prison.
  • 👩 Hiland Mountain in Eagle River, the state's facility for women.
  • 🛶 Regional centers in Fairbanks, Juneau (Lemon Creek), Ketchikan, Bethel (Yukon-Kuskokwim) and Nome (Anvil Mountain).

Two pieces of Alaska history still shape how families plan. In the 1990s and 2000s the state shipped prisoners to private prisons in Arizona and later Hudson, Colorado. By 2005 roughly a third of Alaska's prisoners were held Outside. Goose Creek opened in 2012 at a cost of about $240 million largely to bring them home. In February 2026 a Senate bill to study sending long-term prisoners out of state again had a hearing, but nothing has passed. If your relative has a long sentence, keep an eye on that debate, because a power of attorney signed now is far easier than one signed from a contract prison two thousand miles away.

Federal cases are different. Alaska has no federal prison. People charged federally are usually held in state facilities before trial, but once sentenced they are usually sent to FCI Sheridan in Oregon or elsewhere in the federal system. If that's your situation, read our guide on selling when the owner is in federal prison; the notary and mail rules there are the Bureau of Prisons', not Alaska's.

🖋️ Alaska's Power of Attorney: Boxes, a Notary and Five Business Days

Alaska's power of attorney law sits in AS 13.26.600 through 13.26.695. The execution rule in AS 13.26.600 is short: the principal signs, and the signature is acknowledged before a notary public or another person authorized to take acknowledgments. No witnesses are required. If the principal is physically unable to sign, someone else can sign the principal's name in the principal's conscious presence.

The statutory form in AS 13.26.645 works differently from most states' forms. The principal doesn't initial lines. Instead, each category of authority has a box in a "YES" column, and only the marked categories are granted. Real estate is the first one, labeled "(A) real estate transactions." An owner in custody who wants a relative to sell the house needs that box marked. The form also asks the principal to choose when it takes effect and whether later incapacity revokes it. For a sale, the usual choice is effective on signing and not affected by incapacity.

Some features help families on the outside:

  • ✅ The agent doesn't need a separate acceptance form. Under AS 13.26.605, unless the document says otherwise, a person accepts the appointment by acting as agent.
  • ⏱️ Third parties get a deadline. Under AS 13.26.615, a person presented with an acknowledged power of attorney must accept it, or ask for an agent's certification, a translation or an opinion of counsel, within five business days. A third party who refuses without a permitted reason can be ordered to accept it and made to pay fees, and can't demand a different form for the same authority.
  • 📝 The form protects people who rely on it. The statutory form includes a notice to third parties saying that those who honor a properly executed form are protected.

We still recommend having an Alaska attorney prepare the document or review a completed form before it goes into the facility. A small mistake, such as an unmarked real estate box or a missing legal description, is cheap to fix on the outside and slow to fix once the paper has gone through the mail room.

🗺️ Recording in the Right One of 34 Districts

Alaska doesn't record deeds by county. The State Recorder's Office, part of the Department of Natural Resources, runs 34 recording districts from offices in Anchorage and Fairbanks, with one statewide database. A document is recorded in the district where the land sits: Anchorage, Palmer, Kenai, Fairbanks, Juneau, Kodiak, Bethel, Cape Nome and so on. The recorder's published minimum criteria require the document to name that district, for example "Anchorage Recording District."

The same criteria say that conveyances and powers of attorney must be acknowledged before they can be recorded, citing AS 40.17.030 and 40.17.110. Recorder's staff won't notarize anything. So a power of attorney signed in a facility without a notary can't be recorded, and a title company won't insure a deed signed under an unrecorded power. Other details matter too: a two-inch top margin on the first page, a "return to" address, complete mailing addresses for the grantor and grantee on a deed, and a legal description down to lot, block and subdivision or section, township, range and meridian. The fee is $20 for the first page and $5 for each additional page.

Recording the power of attorney with the deed also gives the buyer's title insurer what it needs to see: proof that the person who signed the deed had authority to sign it.

📮 Getting a Notary Inside a DOC Facility

Because one department runs everything, the notary question has a fairly consistent answer. DOC institutions have staff who hold notary commissions. Goose Creek's prisoner handbook, for example, says the institution has designated staff with notary authority and tells prisoners to ask their housing sergeant for details. Handbooks for other facilities say much the same, though procedures and scheduling vary by building.

Here's what tends to work:

  1. 📞 Confirm the facility today. Use the DOC's inmate lookup or call the facility before mailing. Pretrial transfers between Anchorage, Palmer, Kenai and Goose Creek are common.
  2. 📬 Send a complete document. Every blank filled in, the real estate box marked, the legal description attached, and a short cover letter saying what it is and that it needs a notarial acknowledgment.
  3. ⚖️ Use the attorney channel when you can. Mail from a licensed attorney marked as privileged is opened only in the prisoner's presence, and staff may not read outgoing privileged mail. A defense lawyer or a real estate attorney can also bring a notary to a legal visit.
  4. 📤 Plan the return trip. Include a prepaid envelope addressed to the agent or attorney, and ask for the original back. The recorder wants original signatures.

⚠️ The one thing not to skip: a power of attorney signed in an Alaska facility without a notarial acknowledgment is useless for real estate. The recorder won't record it, and no title company will close on a deed signed under it. Confirm the notary step with the facility before anything is mailed, and confirm where the owner is held that same week, because a transfer can strand the papers in the wrong mail room.

💍 Married Owners: Entireties Title and the Community Property Opt-In

Under AS 34.15.110(b), a husband and wife who acquire title to Alaska real estate hold it as tenants by the entirety unless the deed expressly says otherwise or the property is held as community property under AS 34.77.100. The conveyance also has to state the marital status of the buyers. Entireties title carries survivorship, and in practice every title company in the state wants both spouses on the deed when a married couple sells a house they own together.

Alaska is also one of the few states where community property is optional. Under AS 34.77.030, spouses' property is community property only to the extent a written community property agreement or community property trust says so. Most Alaska couples never sign one, so most married homeowners hold title as entireties or in one spouse's name. If a couple did opt in, the attorney needs to see the agreement, because it can change who has to sign and how proceeds are treated.

What it means when one spouse is incarcerated:

  • 🏠 Both spouses on title, one inside. The spouse at home signs personally. The spouse inside signs a power of attorney, which can name the spouse at home as agent. An Alaska attorney can draft the deed so that one person signs in both capacities.
  • 🔑 Title in the incarcerated spouse's name alone. The spouse at home has no authority to sell without a power of attorney or court order, even after years of paying the mortgage.
  • 💔 A divorce is pending. The family court decides who gets the house. Read our guide to selling a house during a divorce before signing a listing.

🤝 Unmarried Partners: Alaska Abolished Joint Tenancy

This is the Alaska rule that surprises unmarried couples. AS 34.15.130 abolishes joint tenancy in real property, except tenancy by the entirety. Unmarried people who own land together are tenants in common, and a deed that says "joint tenants with right of survivorship" doesn't create survivorship between them. Each owner has a separate share that can be sold, mortgaged or inherited.

That has two consequences when one co-owner is incarcerated. First, the partner at home can't sell the whole house without the incarcerated owner's signature, but can sell their own share. Second, if the incarcerated owner dies, their share passes under their will or to their heirs, not automatically to the partner. A partner who assumes the house is "theirs if something happens" should talk to an attorney now.

If co-owners can't agree, the fallback is an ordinary partition lawsuit; Alaska hasn't adopted the Uniform Partition of Heirs Property Act. See whether a co-owner can sell without the others and our partition lawsuit guide. Buying out the incarcerated owner's share, with that owner signing a deed for their share from inside, is usually faster.

🏛️ No Power of Attorney: "Confinement" and the Single-Transaction Order

Sometimes the owner won't sign, can't be reached, or has a sentence and a mental-health situation that makes a power of attorney doubtful. Alaska's protective-proceedings law gives families a court route. AS 13.26.401 lets the court appoint a conservator for an adult who can't manage property effectively for listed reasons that include confinement, detention by a foreign power and disappearance. That ground alone isn't enough. The petition also has to show that property will be wasted without management, or that money is needed for the support of the person or their dependents.

For a house, a full conservatorship is often more than the family needs. AS 13.26.440 authorizes protective arrangements and single transactions, and the Alaska Court System publishes a form for it, PG-106, Petition for Authorization of a Single Transaction, filed in the Superior Court. The petition describes the proposed transaction in detail, explains why it's in the person's best interests and why they can't do it themselves, and is sworn before a notary. A sale of a vacant house that's losing value through a second Alaska winter is the kind of single transaction it was designed for.

Realistic expectations: the court's guardianship filing fee is $150 and the probate fee is $250, but notice, a hearing and attorney time make the real cost several thousand dollars, and the timeline is measured in months. If the owner can sign a power of attorney, that's almost always cheaper and faster.

💵 An Anchorage House, Priced Out Honestly

Take a three-bedroom house in east Anchorage worth about $380,000 once repaired. It needs a boiler, a roof and interior work, about $45,000. The owner is serving a four-year sentence and the house has been empty since fall.

Property tax. Alaska doesn't levy a statewide tax on homes; boroughs and cities do. The Municipality of Anchorage's 2026 mill levy for tax district 001, City/Anchorage, is 15.57 mills, meaning $15.57 per $1,000 of assessed value. On $380,000 that's about $5,917 a year, or roughly $493 a month. Eagle River's district 010 is slightly higher at 15.76 mills.

Paperwork costs. An attorney-drafted or attorney-reviewed power of attorney typically runs a few hundred dollars to about $1,000. Recording a power of attorney and deed is $20 for each first page plus $5 per additional page. A single-transaction petition or conservatorship costs much more, as described above.

The offer. Cash buyers usually pay 60 to 80 percent of repaired value, which here is $228,000 to $304,000. With $45,000 of work, a fair cash price for this house would be around $260,000 to $285,000. Listed as-is with a local agent, it might sell for $320,000 to $335,000, less roughly 5 to 6 percent commission and a few more months of carrying costs. In Anchorage, with a deep buyer pool and a house that only needs moderate work, listing will often net more than our offer. The cash sale makes sense when the repairs are heavy, nobody can manage showings, or a lien or deadline is approaching.

🛶 Off the Road System: Bush Property and a Thin Buyer Pool

A house in Bethel, Nome, Kotzebue or a smaller village is a different market. Fewer buyers, fewer lenders, appraisals that are hard to support, and repair crews that have to be flown in. Some properties are also on restricted Native allotments or townsite land with special rules, and those need an attorney's review before anyone signs a contract.

In these markets the gap between a cash offer and a retail sale is narrower, because there may be no retail buyer for months. A local buyer who already knows the community, such as a neighbor, a relative or an employer that houses staff, is often the best first call. We look at bush property, but we'll tell you honestly when someone local will pay more.

Unpaid borough or city taxes also run on Alaska's own track. Municipalities foreclose by court petition rather than by auction, followed by a redemption period of at least a year. A house that sits empty while its owner is in custody can quietly slide into that process, so ask the borough or city finance office whether the property is on a delinquency list.

📋 What To Do This Week in Alaska

  1. Look up the deed in the State Recorder's Office online index and note the recording district and exactly how title is held.
  2. Confirm which DOC facility the owner is in today, and ask that facility how notary requests are made.
  3. Check whether the couple ever signed a community property agreement or trust.
  4. Have an Alaska attorney prepare or review a statutory form power of attorney with the real estate box marked and the legal description attached.
  5. Mail it complete, with a cover letter and a prepaid return envelope, preferably through an attorney.
  6. Keep the heat on or have the house winterized, and tell the insurer it's vacant.
  7. Ask the borough or city whether taxes are delinquent, then compare an agent's estimated net with any cash offer before you sign.

✅ Bottom Line for Alaska Families

Alaska's single corrections department makes the inside part of a sale more predictable than in most states: one agency, staff notaries, and privileged attorney mail. The work is in the details. Use the statutory form with the real estate box marked, get it acknowledged, confirm the facility before mailing, and record it in the correct one of the 34 recording districts. Married owners usually hold as tenants by the entirety, so both spouses sign. Unmarried co-owners are tenants in common with no survivorship, because Alaska abolished joint tenancy. With no power of attorney, a Superior Court single-transaction order is the fallback. In Anchorage, a sound house usually belongs with an agent; an empty, winter-damaged or bush property is where a cash buyer earns its place.

❓ Frequently Asked Questions

Does Alaska have county jails?

No. Alaska has no counties, and its Department of Corrections runs a unified system that holds both people awaiting trial and people serving sentences. A few small communities operate local jails under contract with the state for short stays.

Does an Alaska power of attorney need witnesses?

No. AS 13.26.600 requires the principal to sign and to have the signature acknowledged before a notary public or another person authorized to take acknowledgments. Witnesses are not required.

How does the owner give real estate authority on the Alaska statutory form?

By marking the box beside "(A) real estate transactions" in the YES column of the form in AS 13.26.645. Categories that are not marked are not granted, so an unmarked real estate box means the agent cannot sell the house.

Can a title company refuse an Alaska power of attorney?

Only for limited reasons. Under AS 13.26.615, a third party must accept an acknowledged power of attorney or request a certification, translation or opinion of counsel within five business days, and one who refuses without a permitted reason can be ordered to accept it and pay fees.

Where is an Alaska deed or power of attorney recorded?

In the recording district where the land sits. The State Recorder's Office in the Department of Natural Resources runs 34 districts from Anchorage and Fairbanks, and the document must name the district. The fee is 20 dollars for the first page and 5 dollars for each additional page.

Can an Alaska prisoner get documents notarized?

Yes. DOC institutions have staff with notary authority; the Goose Creek handbook, for example, tells prisoners to ask their housing sergeant. Procedures vary by facility, so call ahead and send a complete document.

If my unmarried partner and I own a house as joint tenants in Alaska, do I get it if they die in prison?

Not automatically. AS 34.15.130 abolished joint tenancy in Alaska real property except tenancy by the entirety, so unmarried co-owners are tenants in common, and each share passes by will or to heirs.

Is Alaska a community property state?

Only by choice. Under AS 34.77.030, spouses' property is community property only to the extent a written community property agreement or community property trust provides. Married couples who never opted in usually hold their home as tenants by the entirety.

Can an Alaska court approve a house sale without a power of attorney?

Yes, in the right case. AS 13.26.401 allows protective proceedings for an adult who cannot manage property for reasons that include confinement, and AS 13.26.440 lets the Superior Court authorize a single transaction. The Alaska Court System's form is PG-106.

What is the property tax on a vacant Anchorage house?

Alaska has no statewide tax on homes, so the municipality sets the rate. In 2026 the Municipality of Anchorage levies 15.57 mills in tax district 001, about 5,917 dollars a year on a house assessed at 380,000 dollars.
