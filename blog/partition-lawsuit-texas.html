Partition Lawsuits in Texas: Two Judgments, Two Chances to Appeal, and How Co-Owners Avoid Both

Any Texas co-owner can ask a court to divide or sell shared property. What surprises people is how the case runs: Texas splits it into two stages, each ending in its own appealable judgment, and the law prefers dividing the land to selling it. That shapes the real choice, which is usually how to settle before you need a judge. Here's the process, the costs, and the ways around it. Or ask us, free.

A partition suit is how Texas lets one co-owner stop being a co-owner. If you share a house or land with siblings, cousins, an ex, or a business partner, and you can't agree on what to do with it, any one of you can ask a court to either physically divide it or order it sold and split the money. Nobody else's consent is required.

That much is true in every state. What makes Texas different is the shape of the lawsuit itself. Texas runs partition in two separate stages, each ending in a final judgment that can be appealed on its own, and Texas courts start from a preference for dividing the property rather than selling it. Both facts make contested cases longer and more expensive than people expect, which is why the most useful question is often not how to win a partition, but how to avoid needing one.

⏳ Why a Texas Partition Takes Longer Than You'd Think

The right to partition comes from Section 23.001 of the Texas Property Code, which lets a joint owner or claimant with a possessory interest compel a partition. The procedure is in Rules 756 through 771 of the Texas Rules of Civil Procedure. The case is filed in district court in the county where the property is located, and the parties have the right to a jury on contested facts.

Texas splits the case into two phases:

  1. The first judgment. The court determines each owner's share, settles reimbursement claims for taxes, mortgage payments and improvements, and decides whether the property can be fairly divided in kind. If it can't, the court orders a sale. This judgment is final and appealable.
  2. The second judgment. If the property is to be divided, the court appoints commissioners, who survey and allot the parcels and file a report. Parties have 30 days to object under Rule 771. The court then approves or rejects the report in a second final judgment, which can also be appealed.

Rule 781 tells Texas appellate courts to give partition appeals priority, but priority still means months. A determined holdout can appeal the first judgment, lose, then object to the commissioners' report and appeal the second. A partition that would take four to eight months if everyone cooperates can stretch past two years when one owner uses every step.

Once a suit is filed, the plaintiff's lawyer will usually record a notice of the pending case (a lis pendens) in the county records under Section 12.007 of the Property Code. From then on, nobody can sell or refinance the property without dealing with the lawsuit. That alone often brings a reluctant co-owner to the table.

🧱 Texas Would Rather Divide the Land Than Sell It

Texas courts favor partition in kind, meaning a physical division, and the owner asking for a sale carries the burden of showing that dividing the property wouldn't be fair or would substantially reduce its value. For an ordinary house on a city lot, that burden is easy to meet; you can't cut a three-bedroom house in Lubbock in half. For land, it's a real fight. A 40-acre tract in Bastrop County with one house on it may well be divided, with the house and a few acres going to one owner and bare acreage to the others.

When the pieces aren't equal, Texas uses owelty: the owner who receives the more valuable piece pays the others the difference. The Texas Constitution's homestead article specifically allows a lien for owelty of partition on a homestead, so the owner who keeps the house can usually finance that payment even if the house is their homestead. Owelty is also a tool for settling without a trial: one owner keeps the property and pays an agreed owelty amount, secured by a lien, instead of selling.

If a sale is ordered, Rule 770 allows a sheriff or constable to sell it, or the court can appoint a receiver who may sell privately or at auction. A receiver listing with a broker usually brings a better price than a courthouse-steps sale, but the receiver's fee and costs come off the top.

The key point: in a Texas partition, a sale isn't guaranteed. If your property is land rather than a single house, plan for the possibility that the court divides it, and decide early whether you'd rather own a smaller piece outright or settle for cash.

🛑 When a Texas Court Won't Partition at All

Partition is broadly available, but not always:

  • A surviving spouse's homestead. Under the Texas Constitution and Section 102.005 of the Estates Code, a deceased owner's homestead can't be partitioned among the heirs while the surviving spouse uses it as a home. If a stepparent lives in the house your parent left, no partition suit will force a sale until that right ends. Our guide to Texas heirs who can't agree explains how that plays out.
  • Owners without a present right to possession. If a parent holds a life estate and the children hold the remainder, the children can't partition against the parent; only owners with possessory interests can compel partition.
  • A signed agreement not to partition. Co-owners can waive the right in a written agreement, such as a co-tenancy agreement in an investment deal. Courts generally enforce those for a reasonable time.
  • Disputed title. Partition divides property among people who undisputedly share it. If someone claims the deed was forged or that a relative isn't really an heir, those questions get resolved first, which adds time.

🧬 Inherited Property: How Chapter 23A Changes the Case

In 2017 Texas adopted the Uniform Partition of Heirs' Property Act as Chapter 23A of the Property Code. It applies to property held as tenants in common with no binding agreement on partition, where at least one owner acquired title from a relative and related owners make up a substantial share of the owners or interests (the act uses a 20% threshold). Most family houses passed down without a will fit.

Under Chapter 23A, the court orders an appraisal. If any owner asks for a sale, the other owners get the chance to buy that owner's share at the appraised value. If no buyout happens and the court decides a sale is necessary, the property is generally listed on the open market with a broker rather than auctioned. The court can also weigh family and historical ties to the property when choosing between division and sale. For heirs who want to keep the home place, it's a real shield. For heirs who want out, it means a fair price, but not a fast one.

💰 Chapter 29: A Narrower Remedy for the Owner Paying the Taxes

Texas has a remedy most states don't. Under Chapter 29 of the Property Code, a co-owner who has paid another owner's share of property taxes for any three years in a five-year period, and hasn't been repaid more than half of it, can ask the district court for an order requiring that owner to sell their interest to the one who paid. It isn't a partition. It doesn't sell the whole house, and it requires careful records and notice. But for the sibling who has quietly paid every tax bill since the funeral, it can be a way to buy out an absent co-owner without forcing a sale of the property.

🤝 Five Ways Texas Co-Owners Avoid the Courthouse

A buyout with owelty or a refinance

Best when someone wants to keep it

One owner keeps the property and pays the others their share of an agreed value, with credits for taxes and repairs each person paid. If cash is short, the payment can be secured by an owelty lien on the property. This settles everything and avoids commissions.

Mediation before or after filing

Cheapest way to end a stalemate

Texas courts routinely send partition cases to mediation under Chapter 154 of the Civil Practice and Remedies Code. A half-day session with a mediator who handles property disputes costs a fraction of a trial. You don't have to wait to be ordered; you can propose it in your first letter.

A voluntary sale of the whole property

Highest price, if everyone signs

If the owners can agree to list, a cooperative sale almost always beats a receiver's sale. Remember that a married owner's spouse may need to join the deed if the property is their homestead; we cover that in selling a co-owned house in Texas.

A cash sale to end it quickly

Best when the property or the clock is the problem

When the house needs major repairs, someone can't afford to wait, or the owners only agree on wanting it over, a cash buyer can close in weeks. You trade some price for certainty and for skipping both the lawsuit and the repairs.

Selling your share and walking away

For when you can't fund the fight

You can sell your undivided interest without anyone's consent. The buyer steps into your shoes, including the right to file for partition, so the price reflects that risk. Our guide to selling a share of an inherited Texas house explains how those shares are valued.

💵 What a Contested Partition Does to the Money

Picture a brick house in Pleasant Grove in southeast Dallas, owned by three siblings in equal thirds. Repaired, it would sell for about $280,000. As-is, it needs about $30,000 of work, and one sibling lives there and won't leave.

Carrying costs while the case runs. For 2025, the combined property tax rate inside the City of Dallas and Dallas ISD was about $2.23 per $100 of value. Without a homestead exemption on the whole house, that's roughly $6,240 a year, or $520 a month. Insurance on an older house runs $150 to $250 a month, more if it's vacant, plus routine upkeep. Even with the sibling living there, the out-of-possession owners are usually being asked to share taxes and insurance, about $700 a month across the three.

Contested partition. Eighteen months in, the court orders a receiver sale at $245,000. Subtract about 6% in sale costs and a receiver's fee ($19,600), roughly $45,000 in combined attorney fees across the three siblings, and $12,600 in carrying costs over the period. That leaves about $167,800, or roughly $56,000 per sibling, before any reimbursement adjustments.

Settlement with a cash sale. If the siblings settle at mediation in month three and sell for cash, a realistic offer is around 60% to 80% of after-repair value, here about $196,000, or 70%. Subtract minimal closing costs, mediation and limited attorney fees (about $9,000 combined), and three months of carrying costs ($2,100). Net: about $184,900, or roughly $61,600 each.

Settlement with a listing. If the occupying sibling cooperates with showings and the house sells as-is for $245,000 after four months, net after 8% costs, the same legal costs and carrying costs is about $213,500, or $71,100 each. That's the best result, and it depends entirely on cooperation.

The lesson isn't that cash always wins. It's that the lawsuit itself costs each owner $10,000 to $15,000 here, and any settlement that avoids it starts ahead.

📋 What To Do This Week

  1. Pull the deed, any probate records, and the appraisal district account. Confirm exactly who owns what share.
  2. Check for anything that blocks partition: a surviving spouse living there, a life estate, or a written agreement among the owners.
  3. Total what each owner has paid in taxes, insurance, mortgage and repairs, with receipts. Note whether you've paid another owner's tax share for three of the last five years.
  4. Get an as-is value and an after-repair value from a local agent or appraiser.
  5. Send a written proposal offering two or three settlement options and suggesting mediation, with a deadline.
  6. If you've been served with a partition petition, calendar the answer deadline. In Texas district court it's generally the Monday after 20 days from service, so call a lawyer now.
  7. Talk to a Texas real estate attorney about whether Chapter 23A or Chapter 29 applies to your property.

✅ Bottom Line

Texas guarantees every co-owner a way out through partition, but it builds the case in two stages with two appeals, prefers splitting land over selling it, and protects surviving spouses and heirs in ways that can slow everything down. Those features make a contested partition expensive for everyone. Use the right to partition as leverage, document what you've paid, and try a buyout, mediation or a sale first. If the property needs work or someone can't wait, a cash sale can end it in weeks. If you want an honest read on your situation, ask us.

❓ Frequently Asked Questions

How does a partition lawsuit work in Texas?

Under Texas Property Code Section 23.001 and Rules 756 through 771 of the Texas Rules of Civil Procedure, a co-owner files in district court where the property is located. The court first decides each owner's share and whether the property can be divided. If it can, commissioners divide it and the court enters a second judgment; if it can't, the court orders a sale.

Can a Texas partition judgment be appealed?

Yes, and Texas partition cases produce two final judgments that can each be appealed: the first on ownership shares and partitionability, and the second approving the commissioners' division. Rule 781 gives partition appeals priority, but a contested case with appeals can still take well over a year.

Will a Texas court sell the property or divide it?

Texas favors dividing property in kind, and the owner asking for a sale must show that division would be unfair or would substantially reduce value. A single house on a city lot is almost always sold. Acreage is often divided, with owelty payments to even out unequal pieces.

What is owelty of partition in Texas?

Owelty is a payment from the co-owner who receives the more valuable portion to the others, to equalize the division. The Texas Constitution allows a lien for owelty of partition even on a homestead, so an owner who keeps the house can often finance the payment.

Can I force a partition if my stepmother lives in my late father's house?

Usually not while she uses it as her home. Under the Texas Constitution and Estates Code Section 102.005, a surviving spouse's homestead can't be partitioned among the heirs during the spouse's lifetime while they occupy it. The right generally ends if she moves out permanently or dies.

Does the Uniform Partition of Heirs' Property Act apply in Texas?

Yes. Texas adopted it in 2017 as Chapter 23A of the Property Code. For qualifying inherited property, the court orders an appraisal, lets the other owners buy out anyone requesting a sale, and generally requires an open-market sale through a broker if a sale is ordered.

I've paid my brother's share of the property taxes for years. Do I have any remedy in Texas?

Possibly. Under Chapter 29 of the Texas Property Code, an owner who paid another owner's share of property taxes for any three years in a five-year period, and was repaid less than half, can ask the district court to order that owner to sell their interest to the payer.

How much does a partition lawsuit cost in Texas?

An uncontested case may cost $5,000 to $12,000 in legal fees plus receiver and sale costs. A contested case with appeals can run $15,000 to $50,000 or more per side. Court costs and receiver fees are generally shared by ownership percentage, and each side usually pays its own lawyer.

How can I avoid a partition lawsuit in Texas?

Most Texas co-owners avoid one through a buyout (sometimes secured by an owelty lien), mediation under Chapter 154 of the Civil Practice and Remedies Code, a cooperative listing, or a cash sale. Selling your undivided share to a third party also takes you out of the dispute, though usually at a discount.

I was served with a partition petition in Texas. How long do I have to respond?

In Texas district court, an answer is generally due by 10:00 a.m. on the Monday after 20 days have passed since you were served. Missing it can lead to a default judgment, so contact a Texas real estate attorney right away, even if you hope to settle.
