Selling a Jointly Owned House in Alaska When Only One Owner Wants Out: Joint Tenancy Doesn't Exist Here

Alaska abolished joint tenancy for real estate, so unless you are married to your co-owner, you are a tenant in common no matter what your deed says, with no survivorship and a share you can sell on your own. Married owners are the opposite case: they hold the house by the entirety unless the deed says otherwise. Ask us a question about your Alaska house free and we'll tell you which box you're in and what it lets you do.

People who move to Alaska from somewhere else often bring a belief with them: if two people buy a house as "joint tenants with right of survivorship," the survivor gets the whole thing and neither can do much without the other. In Alaska that belief is wrong for anyone who isn't married. The legislature abolished joint tenancy in real property decades ago, and the statute that did it still reads the same way today. If you share a house with a sibling, a friend, a business partner or a partner you never married, the law treats the two of you as tenants in common.

That one rule answers most of the question you came here with. A tenant in common can sell, mortgage or give away their own share without asking anyone. They can also ask the Superior Court to divide or sell the house. What they cannot do is sign away the other owner's share. Married owners live under a completely different default, which we cover below, and a small number of couples have opted into Alaska's community property system, which changes things again.

So the decision you face is not "can I force this?" It is narrower and more practical: given which box you're in, which exit costs you the least, and how long can you afford to keep paying for a house you don't want?

🧊 Alaska Abolished Joint Tenancy, and Your Deed Can't Bring It Back

The statute is short. AS 34.15.130 says joint tenancy, except for interests in personal property and tenancy by the entirety, "is abolished," and that people holding an undivided interest in real property are considered tenants in common, with exceptions only for married couples and community property trusts. A companion section, AS 34.15.110(a), says that any conveyance of land to two or more people is construed to create a tenancy in common unless one of those exceptions applies.

What this means in plain terms:

  • Survivorship words don't work for unmarried owners. A deed to two friends "as joint tenants with right of survivorship" creates a tenancy in common in Alaska. When one of you dies, that person's share goes to their heirs or the people named in their will, not to you.
  • Shares don't have to be equal. A tenancy in common can be 50/50, 70/30, or split three ways. If the deed is silent, a court usually starts from equal shares, but evidence of who paid what can matter later.
  • There is nothing to "sever." In most states a joint tenant has to record a deed or take some step to break survivorship before selling a share. In Alaska, an unmarried co-owner already holds a share they can transfer.

Out-of-state title forms and online deed templates still use the joint-tenancy phrase, and you will see it on Alaska deeds recorded for unmarried buyers. The words don't change the result. If you and your partner thought the survivor would automatically own the house, that plan has a hole in it, and a lawyer should look at it while you're both alive.

⚠️ No survivorship means a death changes your co-owner. If your unmarried co-owner dies, you don't inherit their share. You may end up owning the house with their children, a probate estate, or a creditor of their estate, and any of them can sell that share or file for partition. If you want the survivor to take the house, each owner can sign and record a transfer-on-death deed naming the other, which Alaska allows under AS 13.48, but it only works if it is recorded before the death.

🪪 Three Boxes: Unmarried, Married, or Opted Into Community Property

Before you decide anything, figure out which of these describes the title. Pull the recorded deed from the recording district where the house sits. Alaska deeds that convey to a couple are supposed to recite the buyers' marital status, because AS 34.15.110(b) requires it, so the deed itself often tells you which box you're in.

🔓 What One Tenant in Common Can Do Without Anyone's Signature

If you're in the first box, you have more freedom than you might think, and also less than you might hope. Here's the honest list.

You can:

  • Sell or give your share to anyone, including an investor, a relative, or the other co-owner. The buyer steps into your shoes.
  • Borrow against your share, if you can find a lender willing to take a fractional interest. Most banks won't.
  • Live in the house. Every tenant in common has the right to possess the whole property, and the other owner can't lock you out.
  • Leave your share by will or by a transfer-on-death deed.
  • File a partition action in the Superior Court asking for division or sale.

You can't:

  • Sign a listing agreement or purchase contract that binds the whole house. A title company won't insure a sale of the full property without every owner's deed or a court order.
  • Evict the other owner because they won't pay their half of the mortgage.
  • Rent the house out and keep all the rent. Rent from a third party belongs to all owners by their shares.
  • Stop the other owner from selling their share to someone you don't like.

The last point cuts both ways. If you sell your share to an investor, your former co-owner now shares the house with that investor. That is often what finally gets a holdout to the table, because an investor has the patience and the budget to file for partition, and they don't care about family history.

💍 When Your Co-Owner Is Your Spouse

Married owners in Alaska are, in a sense, the only people who still have something like the old joint tenancy. Tenancy by the entirety gives the survivor the whole house, and neither spouse can sell the house alone. In practice, if you're married and want out, the path runs through an agreement with your spouse or through a divorce, where the court divides property and the decree usually says who keeps the house or orders it sold. Our guide to selling a house during a divorce covers the timing problems.

Two Alaska rules surprise married owners who moved here from states with a stronger entireties shield.

A spouse's creditor can reach entireties property

In several states, a house held by the entirety is protected from creditors of just one spouse. Alaska doesn't work that way. AS 09.38.100(a) says that when a debtor owns property with someone else as tenants in common or tenants by the entirety, a creditor of the debtor can obtain a levy on and sale of the debtor's interest, subject to exemptions, and the creditor or the buyer at that sale can have the property partitioned or the interest severed. The homestead exemption in AS 09.38.010 is $54,000 per living unit, split pro rata among owners who live there, which is small next to Alaska home prices.

Timing matters. In Smith v. Kofstad (2009), the Alaska Supreme Court held that merely recording a judgment against one spouse doesn't sever the entirety, and that when the debtor spouse died before the creditor levied on the home, the surviving spouse took the house free of the creditor's claim. A creditor who wants the house has to move through execution while the debtor is alive.

Community property is opt-in, and it changes who can sign

Alaska is not a community property state by default. Under AS 34.77.030, spouses' property is community property only to the extent a community property agreement or a community property trust says so. That's different from Washington, where married couples' property is community property without any agreement. Alaska couples who opt in sign a written agreement under AS 34.77.090 with a required capitalized warning at the top, or they put property in a community property trust under AS 34.77.100, which needs at least one Alaska-qualified trustee. Spouses who don't live in Alaska can use the trust route, which is why some Alaska homes owned by out-of-state couples sit in these trusts.

What matters for a sale is how the title is worded. AS 34.77.040(b) says spouses may not manage and control community property held in both names, other than in the alternative, unless they act together. Title in the form "Jane and John as community property" needs both signatures. Title that says "Jane or John" lets either spouse act. And if the deed says "survivorship community property," AS 34.77.110(e) gives the whole interest to the surviving spouse at death, outside probate. If a community property agreement covers the house, it controls even if title is only in one spouse's name, so ask to see the agreement before you rely on the deed.

⚖️ If It Goes to Court: The Superior Court and the Partition Statute

When tenants in common can't agree, any one of them can ask the court to partition. AS 09.45.260 lets one or more co-owners bring an action for a division of the property according to their rights, and for a sale if it appears that partition can't be had without great prejudice to the owners. The case belongs in the Superior Court. Alaska's district courts lack jurisdiction over actions in which title to real property is in question and over equitable actions, under AS 22.15.050.

A few features of Alaska's partition chapter matter to the decision you're making now. We keep this short, because the lawsuit itself deserves its own guide.

  • Division first, sale if division would cause great prejudice. A single-family house on a city lot usually can't be split, so most residential cases end in a sale. Acreage near Palmer or Fairbanks is a different argument.
  • Owners Outside can be served. AS 09.45.280 allows service by publication on parties who live out of state or can't be found. A co-owner who moved to Arizona and stopped answering mail can't stall a case forever.
  • A court sale is a public auction. AS 09.45.420 says the referee sells at public auction to the highest bidder, in the manner used for execution sales. Auction prices for a house that can't be inspected are often well below what a listing would bring.
  • Costs come off the top. Under AS 09.45.380, sale proceeds pay the costs of the action and the reference first, then liens in order of priority, and only then the owners.

The filing fee for a civil case not otherwise listed in the court system's fee schedule is $250. That's the smallest number in the case. Alaska also has an unusual fee rule: Civil Rule 82 generally awards the prevailing party part of its attorney fees, and for a case without a money judgment that is 20 percent of actual fees if it ends without trial and 30 percent of reasonable actual fees if it goes to trial. In a partition where both owners get something, who "prevails" can itself be argued, so ask your lawyer how a judge in your district has handled it. 🧮 Who Gets Credit for the Mortgage, the Taxes and the New Roof

Most co-owner fights are really about money already spent. When an Alaska house is sold, by agreement or by the court, these are the arguments you should be ready for:

  • Mortgage principal. An owner who paid more than their share of the loan will usually ask to be repaid from the proceeds before the split. Interest and principal can be treated differently, so keep the lender's annual statements.
  • Property tax and insurance. Paying the borough bill or the homeowner's policy protects everyone's share, which makes these strong credits.
  • Repairs versus upgrades. Replacing a failed boiler before a January freeze kept the house from losing value. A new kitchen nobody else wanted is a harder sell.
  • The owner who lives there. Living in the house usually doesn't create a debt for rent by itself, but the picture changes if the occupying owner kept the other one out. Expect the occupying owner's credits to be offset against the value of their use.

Start a ledger today: date, amount, what it paid for, who paid. Bank records from three years ago are harder to find than you'd think, and a clean spreadsheet shortens negotiations as much as it shortens a trial.

🗺️ Four Decisions Facing One Alaska Co-Owner

Assuming you're a tenant in common, or you've reached the end of a marriage and the decree leaves both names on title, these are the realistic exits, with our honest verdict on each.

🤝 Agree with your co-owner and list

Highest net, if both will sign

If the house is in decent shape and both owners will sign, a listing with a local agent almost always nets more than any other option. Anchorage, Eagle River and the Mat-Su have active buyers in spring and summer. Expect slower showings from November through March, and price accordingly if you list in winter.

🔑 One owner buys the other out

Best when someone wants to stay

Agree on an appraiser, net out the credits, and have the staying owner refinance so the departing owner comes off the loan. The departing owner signs a deed. This is often the cleanest result for siblings or ex-partners, but it fails if the staying owner can't qualify for a loan alone.

📄 Sell your share and walk away

Fastest exit, lowest price

Because Alaska makes every unmarried co-owner a tenant in common, your share is transferable today. An investor who buys it inherits the dispute and the likely partition case, so they'll pay well under half of the house's value. You trade price for being done in weeks, off the tax bill and out of the fight.

⚖️ File for partition

Reliable, slow, and it can end at auction

Your co-owner can't block a partition case. But between a contested case, referees and a court auction, the final number may be lower than either of you expected. Filing often works best as leverage: many cases settle into a buyout or a listing once a complaint is served.

💵 The Money on an Anchorage House

Take a three-bedroom house in the Anchorage Bowl that would sell for $400,000 updated. It needs a roof, a new boiler and cosmetic work, about $40,000. Two former partners own it 50/50 as tenants in common. One moved out last fall and wants out; the other wants to stay but can't refinance yet.

  • Repair and list: $400,000, less a 6 percent commission ($24,000), less $40,000 of repairs and about four months of holding costs, leaves roughly $328,000 before payoffs, or about $164,000 each. That only works if both owners fund repairs and agree on every price change.
  • Whole house to a cash buyer as-is: 60 to 80 percent of the $400,000 repaired value is $240,000 to $320,000. At 70 percent, that's $280,000, or $140,000 each before payoffs, closing in about three weeks with no repairs.
  • Selling just the half that wants out: a buyer of a disputed half interest would typically pay something like $70,000 to $100,000 in a case like this, because they're buying a lawsuit along with the share.
  • Buyout: if the staying owner could refinance at an as-is appraisal of $350,000, the departing owner's half is $175,000 less the payoff share and any credits. On paper this beats every other option for the departing owner. It just has to actually close.

What waiting costs. The Municipality of Anchorage's 2024 total rate for tax district 1 was 16.145 mills. On a $360,000 assessment, that's about $5,812 a year, or roughly $485 a month. Add a vacant-dwelling insurance policy at roughly $150 to $250 a month, heat and electricity at $250 to $450 a month in winter (you can't shut the heat off in an empty Alaska house without risking burst pipes), and snow removal and upkeep at $100 to $250. That's about $985 to $1,435 a month before any mortgage payment. A partition case that runs eighteen months can cost $18,000 to $26,000 in carrying costs alone, plus both sides' lawyers. Rates differ elsewhere in the Municipality and in the Mat-Su, Fairbanks North Star and Kenai Peninsula boroughs, so check your own bill.

🙅 When an Alaska Agent Beats a Cash Buyer

If both of you will sign, the house is in reasonable condition, and nobody needs to close before breakup season, call a listing agent, not us. In the example above, a listing nets about $48,000 more than our 70 percent offer. That's real money and you should keep it.

We make sense in narrower cases: an owner who won't sign or answer, a house that needs repairs nobody can fund, a co-owner who died and left the share to heirs you've never met, or a departing owner who wants out of the dispute now and is willing to sell just their share. If your co-owner is willing to buy you out and can get a loan, that's usually the better deal for both of you, and we'd say so.

📋 What To Do This Week

  1. Get the recorded deed from the recording district office and note the exact wording: names, marital status, and any "survivorship" or "community property" language.
  2. If you're married to your co-owner, ask whether you ever signed a community property agreement or put the house in a community property trust.
  3. If you're unmarried and expected survivorship, talk to a lawyer about transfer-on-death deeds now, while both owners are alive.
  4. Start the payment ledger: mortgage, borough tax, insurance, heating fuel, repairs, with dates and who paid.
  5. Check for judgments or liens against your co-owner, since a creditor can levy on their share.
  6. Get a broker's opinion of value both as-is and repaired, so you know what a buyout, listing or share sale is really worth.
  7. Book a consultation with an Alaska real estate attorney before you sign a deed to anyone or file anything.

✅ Bottom Line

In Alaska, marriage, not deed wording, decides most of what a co-owner can do. If you aren't married to the other owner, you're a tenant in common: you can sell your share, live in the house, and file for partition, but you can't sell the whole house alone and you have no survivorship. If you are married, the entirety default and any community property agreement mean both spouses sign, though a creditor of one spouse can still reach the house. Work out your box, build your ledger, and price each exit before anyone files.

❓ Frequently Asked Questions

Does Alaska recognize joint tenancy with right of survivorship on a house?

No, except for married couples, who hold as tenants by the entirety. AS 34.15.130 abolished joint tenancy in real property, and co-owners holding undivided interests are treated as tenants in common, even if the deed uses joint-tenancy language.

Can one co-owner sell an Alaska house without the other?

Not the whole house. An unmarried co-owner can sell their own tenant-in-common share without consent, but a sale of the full property needs every owner's signature or a Superior Court order in a partition case.

If my unmarried partner dies, do I get the house in Alaska?

Not automatically. Their share passes through their estate to their heirs or will beneficiaries. To give the survivor the house, each owner can record a transfer-on-death deed naming the other before either one dies.

How do married couples own a house in Alaska?

As tenants by the entirety, unless the deed expressly says otherwise or the house is in a community property trust. Neither spouse can sell the house alone, and the surviving spouse owns the whole house at death.

Does tenancy by the entirety protect an Alaska house from one spouse's creditors?

Not fully. AS 09.38.100 lets a creditor of one spouse levy on and sell that spouse's interest and seek partition or severance, subject to the homestead exemption. Recording a judgment alone doesn't sever the tenancy, and if the debtor spouse dies before a levy, the survivor takes the house.

Is Alaska a community property state?

Only by choice. Spouses can opt in with a written community property agreement or a community property trust under AS 34.77. Without one, Alaska divides marital property equitably in a divorce.

Which court handles a partition of an Alaska house?

The Superior Court. Alaska's district courts don't have jurisdiction over cases in which title to real property is in question or over equitable actions, and partition is governed by AS 09.45.260 and the sections that follow.

Can I be ordered to pay my co-owner's lawyer in Alaska?

Possibly. Alaska Civil Rule 82 generally awards a prevailing party part of its attorney fees, 20 or 30 percent in a case without a money judgment depending on whether it goes to trial. How that applies in a partition depends on the case, so ask an Alaska attorney.

What does it cost to hold an empty co-owned house in Anchorage?

On our example house, roughly $985 to $1,435 a month for property tax, vacant-home insurance, winter heat and upkeep, before any mortgage. Anchorage's 2024 rate in tax district 1 was 16.145 mills.

Should Alaska co-owners list the house or sell it for cash?

If both owners will sign and the house shows well, list it, since it will usually net more. A cash sale fits a house that needs major repairs, an owner who won't cooperate, or a co-owner who needs out quickly.
