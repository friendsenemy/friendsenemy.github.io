Heirs Can’t Agree on an Alaska House? The Fight Is Really Over Who Holds the Letters

Under Alaska’s probate code, the personal representative can usually sell an inherited house without a court order and without every heir’s signature, so the family fight tends to turn into a fight over who gets appointed and how to rein that person in. Here is how appointment priority, supervised administration, restraining orders, removal and partition actually work in Alaska Superior Court, what a standoff costs in Anchorage, and when listing beats a cash offer. Picture a split-level in South Anchorage after the funeral. One daughter flew home from Seattle and filed the paperwork to be appointed. Her brother has lived in the downstairs unit for six years and assumes nothing can happen without him. A second brother in Wasilla wants his third in cash before winter. Each of them is waiting for the others to "agree."

Alaska law doesn't really work by agreement. Alaska adopted the Uniform Probate Code, and under it the person the court appoints as personal representative runs the estate, house included, without asking a judge for permission at each step. So in most Alaska families the argument about selling quickly becomes an argument about something narrower and more important: who holds the letters, and what the other heirs can do if they don't trust that person. This guide is built around that question. The general mechanics of opening probate and closing a sale are covered elsewhere; here we stay on the dispute.

⚖️ In Alaska, the First Fight Is Over Who Gets Appointed

AS 13.16.065 sets the order of priority for appointment, and it applies whether the case is formal or informal:

  1. The person named in a probated will (or nominated under a power the will gives).
  2. A surviving spouse who also takes under the will.
  3. Other devisees under the will.
  4. The surviving spouse.
  5. Other heirs.
  6. Any creditor, but only once 45 days have passed since the death.

With no will and no spouse, every child sits in the same tier. The statute then adds the rule that causes most of the early friction: when two or more people share a priority, the ones who haven't renounced must concur in nominating someone or in applying together. One sibling can't simply race to the courthouse and expect the others' equal priority to vanish.

The second rule matters even more for a divided family. Objections to an appointment can only be raised in a formal proceeding before a judge, not through the informal application process. And when an heir with a substantial interest objects to someone whose priority doesn't come from a will, the court may appoint a person acceptable to heirs and devisees whose interests add up to more than half of the probable distributable value, or, if they can't agree, any suitable person. In plain terms: in an intestate estate with three equal children, any two of them together control who the court is likely to pick. If no majority forms, the judge can appoint a neutral, often a professional fiduciary or an attorney, whose fees come out of the estate.

That last option is underused. A neutral personal representative costs money, but it removes the "my sister is selling Dad's house to spite me" argument entirely, and title companies deal with professional fiduciaries every week.

🔑 What the Personal Representative Can Do Without Asking

Once appointed, an Alaska personal representative is told by AS 13.16.355 to settle the estate expeditiously and, unless supervised, to do it without court adjudication, order or direction. The list of authorized transactions in AS 13.16.410 includes acquiring or disposing of an asset, "including land in this or another state," for cash or on credit, at public or private sale, and separately the power to sell, mortgage or lease estate real property. Those powers can be limited by the will or by an order in a formal proceeding, but absent such a limit, no heir's signature is needed on the listing agreement or the deed.

Two more provisions make that power real in the marketplace:

  • 📜 Buyers are protected. Under AS 13.16.405, someone who deals with a personal representative in good faith and for value is protected as if the power had been properly exercised, and isn't required to investigate whether the sale was wise. Once a closing happens, an unhappy heir's claim is usually against the personal representative, not against the house.
  • 🧭 The duty runs to everyone. AS 13.16.350 makes the personal representative a fiduciary held to the standards Alaska applies to trustees, obligated to use these powers for the best interests of the successors. That duty is what gives the other heirs leverage. A sale at a fair, documented price meets it. A rushed sale to a friend at a discount may not.

There is one transaction the personal representative cannot quietly do: buy the house personally. AS 13.16.400 makes a sale to the personal representative, a spouse, agent or attorney, or any transaction tainted by a substantial conflict of interest, voidable by any interested person who didn't consent after fair disclosure, unless the will authorized it or the court approved it after notice. This comes up constantly, because the sibling who volunteered to be appointed is often the sibling who wants the house. It can be done. It just needs either signed consents from the other heirs after full disclosure (an appraisal, the terms, the financing) or a court order.

🛑 Four Brakes Heirs Can Pull in Alaska Superior Court

Probate cases are heard in the Alaska Superior Court, and the code gives heirs who don't trust the personal representative several distinct tools. They are not interchangeable, and picking the wrong one wastes weeks.

1. Petition for supervised administration (AS 13.16.220)

Any interested person may ask at any time for the estate to be placed under court supervision. If the will is silent, the court orders it when it finds supervision necessary under the circumstances. Once the personal representative has notice of the petition, AS 13.16.225 bars any distribution of the estate, but the representative's other powers continue unless the court restricts them. And even after supervision is ordered, AS 13.16.230 says a supervised representative keeps all ordinary powers without interim orders, except that distribution needs a prior court order. Any other restriction must be endorsed on the letters to bind people dealing in good faith.

That surprises families. Supervision slows distribution and puts a judge on the case, but it does not, by itself, stop a sale of the house.

2. Ask for a restraining order (AS 13.16.275)

If the real worry is a specific act, such as signing a purchase agreement next week, an interested person can petition for an order restraining the personal representative from that act when the representative might otherwise unreasonably jeopardize the heirs' interests. The statute requires a hearing within 10 days unless the parties agree otherwise. This is the fast tool.

3. Petition to remove (AS 13.16.295)

Cause for removal exists when removal would be in the estate's best interests, or when the representative misrepresented facts to get appointed, disregarded a court order, became unable to serve, mismanaged the estate or failed to perform a duty. After receiving notice of a removal petition, the representative may act only to account, correct maladministration or preserve the estate, unless the court orders otherwise. In practice, filing a well-documented removal petition freezes a pending sale.

4. Void a self-interested deal (AS 13.16.400)

If the representative has already sold to themselves, a relative or a business partner without disclosure and consent or court approval, any heir who didn't consent can seek to void it.

⚠️ Object before the closing, not after. Because AS 13.16.405 protects a buyer who deals with the personal representative in good faith, the time to stop a bad sale in Alaska is before the deed records. A petition for supervised administration alone won't do it. If a sale is imminent and you believe the price or buyer is wrong, talk to an Alaska attorney about a restraining order, which must be heard within 10 days, or a removal petition, and bring evidence: an appraisal or broker opinion, the buyer's relationship to the representative, and what the house was listed for.

🏚️ The Sibling Who Lives There

Occupying heirs are the most common reason an Alaska sale stalls, and the code speaks to them directly. AS 13.16.380 gives the personal representative the right to possession or control of the decedent's property, but lets real property be left with the person presumptively entitled to it "unless or until" the representative decides possession is needed for administration. Then comes the sentence that ends most of these arguments: the representative's request for delivery of property held by an heir is conclusive evidence, in a later possession suit against that heir, that possession is necessary.

So the brother downstairs can stay while the estate is being sorted out, but if the personal representative needs the house empty to sell it, a written demand followed by a possession action in Superior Court is a short legal question. It is still slow in calendar terms, and nobody wants to sue a sibling. Most families do better with a written choice offered early:

  • 🏦 Buy out the others at an independent appraisal, with a deadline to show loan approval.
  • 📅 Agree to a move-out date in exchange for a credit from the sale proceeds.
  • 🧾 Stay until closing but pay the utilities, heat and insurance in the meantime.

What about rent? Whether a co-owning heir owes the others for living in the house, or gets credit for taxes and repairs they paid, is normally sorted out when the estate is distributed or a partition case divides the money. Courts look at who paid what and whether anyone was shut out. Keep receipts on both sides, and ask an Alaska attorney how judges in your district handle these accountings before you demand back rent in a family email.

✍️ Settling It on Paper Instead of in Court

Alaska's code gives families two quiet tools that do more than most realize.

A binding family agreement. Under AS 13.16.590, competent successors may agree among themselves to change the shares they would take under the will or intestacy, in any way they set out in a written contract signed by everyone affected. The personal representative must follow it, subject to paying creditors, taxes and costs. This is how an in-family buyout is usually documented: the sibling who keeps the house takes it in distribution and pays the others an agreed amount, and the representative carries it out. It needs every affected heir's signature, but it turns a fragile handshake into something the estate is obligated to honor.

A proposal with a 30-day fuse. AS 13.16.560 starts from a preference for distributing assets in kind and lets the residue be distributed in whatever manner the representative finds most practicable, including in undivided interests or in cash. It also lets the representative mail a written proposal for distribution once probable charges are known. A distributee who doesn't object in writing within 30 days of the mailing loses the right to object based on the kind or value of the asset they receive. For an heir who has been ignoring every email, that deadline is the end of passive resistance.

Mediation works well on top of both. If a partition lawsuit is filed, Alaska Civil Rule 100 lets a party move for mediation at any time after the complaint, and the court can order it on its own when it may produce an equitable settlement. Unless the court orders otherwise, the parties split the mediator's cost equally, and each side gets one challenge to a court-appointed mediator. Many families hire a private mediator before anything is filed, for the same reason: a day of mediation costs far less than a month of motion practice.

🪓 Partition in Alaska: An Old Statute, No Heirs Property Act

When the estate has been closed, or the heirs already hold title together, the remedy is a partition action in Superior Court under AS 09.45.260 and the sections after it. Any tenant in common can sue to divide the property, and the court can order a sale if partition "cannot be had without great prejudice" to the owners. Under AS 09.45.290, if physical division would cause that prejudice, the court may order a sale; otherwise it orders the land divided and may appoint one or more referees to do it. Where a division can't come out equal, AS 09.45.590 lets the court order one owner to pay another to even things up, which matters on larger rural parcels that really can be split.

Costs, including referee fees, are paid by the owners in proportion to their interests under AS 09.45.620, but when the litigation is really between some of the owners, the court may make any or all of them bear it. A sibling who fights a partition for a year and loses can end up paying more than their share of the bill.

Alaska has not adopted the Uniform Partition of Heirs Property Act. That means no statutory appraisal-then-buyout window for relatives, and no built-in preference for an open-market listing over a court-ordered sale, both of which heirs in Georgia or California can rely on. Alaska families get those protections only by negotiating them.

There is also an in-estate version. Under AS 13.16.585, before the estate closes, the personal representative or any heir or devisee holding an undivided interest can petition for partition, which the court handles like a civil partition case, and the court may direct the representative to sell property that can't be divided without prejudice or conveniently allotted to one person. For a single house, that is usually the cleaner route, because the representative already controls the sale. For how partition works in general, see our partition lawsuit guide.

🧮 The Anchorage Numbers: What a Standoff Costs

Take a house in the Anchorage Bowl with a repaired value of $400,000, an assessed value of $380,000, and $50,000 of deferred work: a roof, a boiler near the end of its life, and dated baths. Three siblings inherit equally.

Monthly carrying cost while they argue (illustrative; use your own bills):

  • 🏛️ Property tax: the Municipality's 2026 levy for Tax District 1 (the core Anchorage area) is 15.57 mills. $380,000 × 0.01557 = about $5,917 a year, or $493 a month. Late payments carry a 10% penalty plus interest.
  • 🔥 Heat and utilities: an empty Alaska house can't go cold. Keeping gas and electric on through an Anchorage winter commonly runs $300–$600 a month, and a frozen line can cost more than a year of heat.
  • 🛡️ Vacant-home insurance: standard homeowner policies often limit coverage after a house sits empty, so budget $150–$300 a month for a vacancy policy.
  • ❄️ Snow, checks and upkeep: plowing, roof snow, weekly walk-throughs: $100–$250 a month.

That is roughly $1,050–$1,650 a month, or $12,600–$19,800 a year, before a single attorney invoice.

A cash offer, worked out. Cash buyers typically pay about 60–80% of repaired value depending on condition and complications. With $50,000 of work, a realistic offer on this house might be $260,000–$300,000; call it $280,000 (70%). Split three ways, that is about $93,300 each, in three to five weeks.

A listing, worked out. Sold as-is on the open market, the same house might bring $350,000. Subtract about 5–6% in commission (roughly $21,000) and $4,000 in closing costs and the estate nets around $325,000, or $108,300 each, after perhaps three to four months of carrying costs (another $4,000–$6,500 in total). The listing still wins by more than $13,000 per sibling.

The math flips when the dispute drags. A contested removal petition or a partition case that costs each side $20,000 and adds a year of $1,300-a-month carrying costs erases the listing's advantage and then some. That is the honest case for a cash sale: not that it pays more, but that it can end a fight that is costing more than the discount.

🧭 Pick Your Lever

🤝 Agree on the personal representative first

Best first move

Before anyone files, try to agree on who will serve, or on a neutral professional. Under AS 13.16.065, heirs holding more than half the estate's value carry real weight with the judge, so a majority that agrees now avoids a formal appointment fight later.

🏡 Let the representative list it, with guardrails

Most money if the house is sound

Ask for a written appraisal or broker opinion, a commitment to list on the open market, and copies of every offer. A representative who agrees to that has little to fear from the other heirs, and the estate usually nets the most.

🔑 Document a family buyout

When one heir wants it and can pay

Put the price, the financing deadline and the distribution terms into a written agreement signed by all affected heirs under AS 13.16.590. If the buyer is the personal representative, get full disclosure and consents or court approval so the deal can't be voided later.

⚖️ Go to the judge

When the representative is the problem

Use a restraining order for a specific bad act, removal for real mismanagement, and partition only when the estate is closed or the heirs already hold title. Each is legitimate; none is cheap.

💼 Sell for cash, whole or in part

When condition or conflict rules

A cash buyer can purchase the whole house from the representative as-is, or, after heirs hold title, buy one heir's undivided share and take over the negotiation. You give up price for speed and an end to the fight.

📋 What To Do This Week

  1. Find out whether anyone has applied to be personal representative. The Alaska Court System's clerks can tell you whether a probate case has been opened in the decedent's name.
  2. Read the will, if there is one, for a named personal representative, any limit on selling real estate, and any direction about supervised administration.
  3. If you share priority with siblings, decide together who will serve, or agree on a neutral, and put it in writing.
  4. Confirm the house is insured as vacant, heated, and that property taxes are current with the municipality or borough.
  5. Get one independent appraisal or broker opinion that every heir sees.
  6. If a relative lives there, give them a written choice: buyout by a date, move-out with a credit, or stay and cover costs until closing.
  7. If you think a bad sale is about to close, call an Alaska attorney today about a restraining order rather than waiting.

✅ Bottom Line

In Alaska, an inherited house is rarely sold by unanimous vote. It is sold by a personal representative who has broad power under AS 13.16.410 and owes every heir a fiduciary duty. That puts the real decisions at two points: who gets appointed, where heirs holding more than half the value have leverage, and what the others do if they don't trust that person, where a restraining order, removal petition or written family agreement does more than any group text. Alaska offers no heirs property buyout window, so partition is a blunt last resort. If the house is sound and the representative is acting in good faith, list it. If someone won't leave, the house needs heavy work, or the fight is costing more than the discount, ask us for a number.

❓ Frequently Asked Questions

Can an Alaska personal representative sell an inherited house without the heirs agreeing?

Usually yes. Under AS 13.16.355 and AS 13.16.410, a personal representative in an unsupervised estate can sell estate real property at public or private sale without a court order, unless the will or an order in a formal proceeding restricts that power. The representative must still act as a fiduciary in the best interests of the heirs under AS 13.16.350.

Who has priority to be appointed personal representative in Alaska?

Under AS 13.16.065, the order is: the person named in a probated will, a surviving spouse who is a devisee, other devisees, the surviving spouse, other heirs, and, 45 days after death, any creditor. People who share a priority must concur in nominating someone or applying together unless they renounce.

What happens if Alaska heirs can't agree on who should be personal representative?

Objections can only be raised in a formal proceeding before a judge. If an heir with a substantial interest objects, the court may appoint someone acceptable to heirs and devisees holding more than half of the probable distributable value, or, without that agreement, any suitable person, which can be a neutral professional.

Does supervised administration stop the sale of the house in Alaska?

Not by itself. Once a personal representative has notice of a petition for supervised administration, they cannot distribute the estate, but their other powers continue unless the court restricts them. A supervised representative keeps ordinary powers, including selling, except that distributions need a court order. Other restrictions must be endorsed on the letters.

How quickly can an Alaska court stop a personal representative from selling?

An interested person can petition for a restraining order under AS 13.16.275 if the representative might unreasonably jeopardize the heirs' interests. The statute requires the matter to be set for hearing within 10 days unless the parties agree otherwise.

How do you remove a personal representative in Alaska?

Any interested person can petition under AS 13.16.295. Cause includes removal being in the estate's best interests, misrepresentation to obtain appointment, disregarding a court order, incapacity, mismanagement, or failing to perform a duty. After notice of the petition, the representative may act only to account, correct maladministration or preserve the estate unless the court orders otherwise.

Can the personal representative buy the inherited house in Alaska?

Only carefully. Under AS 13.16.400, a sale to the personal representative or their spouse, agent or attorney, or any deal affected by a substantial conflict of interest, is voidable by an interested person who did not consent after fair disclosure, unless the will authorized it or the court approved it after notice.

Can a sibling living in the inherited house refuse to leave in Alaska?

They can stay for a while, but not indefinitely if the house must be sold. AS 13.16.380 lets the personal representative leave real property with the heir until possession is needed for administration, and the representative's request for delivery is conclusive evidence of that need in a possession suit against the heir.

Has Alaska adopted the Uniform Partition of Heirs Property Act?

No. Partition in Alaska follows AS 09.45.260 and the sections after it, under which the court divides the property or orders a sale when division would cause great prejudice. While an estate is open, AS 13.16.585 also allows partition for purposes of distribution, and the court may direct the representative to sell.

Can Alaska heirs make a binding agreement to let one sibling keep the house?

Yes. Under AS 13.16.590, competent successors can change their shares by a written contract signed by everyone affected, and the personal representative must follow it, subject to paying creditors, taxes and administration costs. It is the usual way to document an in-family buyout.
