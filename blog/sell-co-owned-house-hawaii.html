Selling a Co-Owned House in Hawaii: Who Lives There Sets the Tax Bill, and Who Lives on the Mainland Sets the Withholding

Hawaii co-ownership has a twist most mainland guides miss. When the co-owner who lives in the house moves out or passes away, the property can drop out of the owner-occupied tax class, and on some islands the rate more than doubles. When co-owners who live off-island sell, the buyer has to hold back 7.25 percent of their share of the price for the state. Add tenancy by the entirety for spouses and reciprocal beneficiaries, two recording systems and Hawaiian Home Lands leases, and the right move depends on facts that are specific to Hawaii. Here's how to sort it out. Or ask us a question, free.

Picture a family house in Hilo. Mom has passed. One son lives there and wants to stay. His two sisters live in Las Vegas and Sacramento and want their money. In most states, this is a straightforward fight about a buyout price or a sale. In Hawaii, three other things change the numbers before anyone talks price: the county's property tax class, the state's withholding on nonresident sellers, and the way the deed was written. Get those right and the family usually finds a deal. Ignore them and money disappears on the way out.

🏝️ In Hawaii, Where Each Co-Owner Lives Changes the Math

Every state cares where you live for income tax. Hawaii goes further for real estate. Each county sets its own property tax rates by class, and the residential classes split on whether an owner lives in the house. Separately, the state requires a buyer to withhold part of the price when a seller isn't a Hawaii resident. So for co-owned property, two questions run through every option:

  • 🏡 Does at least one owner live there as their home? That usually keeps the property in the lower owner-occupied tax class and keeps the county homeowner exemption alive.
  • ✈️ Do any owners live outside Hawaii? Their share of a sale is subject to HARPTA withholding unless an exemption or a state certificate applies.

Those two facts often decide whether a buyout, a sale or simply waiting is the best move, before anyone argues about what the house is worth.

🧾 The Tax Class Can Double When the Owner Who Lives There Leaves

Here are the county rates for fiscal year 2027, per $1,000 of net taxable value, from the counties' published rate schedule:

MauiOwner-Occupied: $1.65 up to $1.5 millionNon-Owner-Occupied: $6.25 up to $1 million

Each county has its own rules for which class a property lands in, and Honolulu's Residential A class applies only to certain properties, so check with the county real property assessment office. The pattern is still clear. On Maui, the non-owner-occupied rate is nearly four times the owner-occupied rate. On the Big Island, it's nearly double. When the co-owner who lived in the house moves out, or the parent who owned it dies and nobody files a new homeowner exemption claim, the next tax year's bill can jump.

For co-owners, that cuts both ways. A sibling who lives in the house and holds a share can often keep the property in the lower class, which is a real reason to let that sibling stay while a buyout is arranged. But if the plan is a sale, every year of delay in a non-owner-occupied class costs more in Hawaii than in most states.

⚠️ Don't let the exemption lapse by accident. If a co-owner moves into the house, or already lives there, ask the county about filing a homeowner exemption claim before the county's deadline. If nobody lives there, budget for the higher class while you decide.

✈️ Mainland Co-Owners and the 7.25 Percent HARPTA Withholding

Hawaii's Real Property Tax Act, known as HARPTA, requires a buyer of Hawaii real property from a nonresident to withhold 7.25 percent of the amount realized and send it to the Department of Taxation. It's a prepayment of Hawaii income tax, not the final tax. The seller files a Hawaii return and gets back anything withheld beyond the tax actually owed.

When co-owners are a mix of residents and nonresidents, the state's Form N-288 instructions say to allocate the amount realized among the sellers by their capital contribution, with spouses treated as contributing half each, and withhold on the nonresidents' portion. The withholding is credited among the nonresident sellers as they agree, or split evenly if they don't. Each nonresident gets a separate Form N-288A.

Three things soften the hit:

  • 🏠 Resident sellers aren't withheld on. A Hawaii resident co-owner certifies residency on Form N-289.
  • 📉 Inherited houses often have little gain. Heirs generally take a stepped-up tax basis at the date of death. If the house sells for about what it was worth then, there may be little or no gain, and Form N-288B lets a seller apply for a withholding certificate that reduces or eliminates the withholding when there's no gain or not enough proceeds to cover it.
  • 🧾 It comes back when you file. Without a certificate, the withheld money isn't lost, but it's tied up until your Hawaii return is processed.

Federal FIRPTA withholding is separate and applies to sellers who are foreign persons, not to U.S. citizens living on the mainland. A Japanese or Canadian co-owner can face both.

💍 Spouses and Reciprocal Beneficiaries: The Entirety Shield

Hawaii recognizes tenancy by the entirety, and under HRS § 509-2 it's available not only to spouses but also to reciprocal beneficiaries, a registered status Hawaii created for adults who can't marry each other, such as a parent and adult child or two siblings. The Hawaii Supreme Court held in Sawada v. Endo, 57 Haw. 608 (1977), that entireties property isn't subject to the claims of one spouse's separate creditors.

That has two effects for co-owners. First, neither spouse or reciprocal beneficiary can sell or encumber the property alone; it takes both signatures. Second, a creditor of only one of them generally can't force a sale. Section 509-2 also lets couples move entireties property into qualifying revocable trusts while keeping that creditor protection, and says that when a divorce decree is entered or the reciprocal beneficiary relationship ends, the protection for trust-held property ends and it's treated as held in tenancy in common.

If you're divorcing, the Family Court divides the house, not a partition case. For how Hawaii divorces usually handle a shared home, read our divorce sale guide.

📄 Joint Tenants and Tenants in Common: What One Owner Can Do Alone

Outside of spouses and reciprocal beneficiaries, Hawaii co-owners usually hold as joint tenants or tenants in common. The deed language controls, and the case notes to § 509-2 point out that the statute creates no presumption in favor of tenancy in common, so read the wording carefully.

How the deed readsWhat happens at deathWhat one owner can do alone
Tenants by the entiretySurvivor owns it allNothing; both must sign
Joint tenantsSurvivors take the deceased owner's shareConvey their own interest, which ends survivorship for that share
Tenants in commonShare passes by will or intestacySell, give or mortgage their own share
Heirs who never probatedTitle may still be in the parent's nameLittle, until an estate is opened or a small-estate process is used

One Hawaii detail cuts against joint tenants: the case notes to § 509-2 also cite a 1978 decision holding that a joint tenant's right of survivorship is subject to levy by that tenant's creditors. Unlike entireties property, a joint tenancy doesn't shield one owner's interest from that owner's debts.

🗂️ Land Court or Regular System? Check Before You Sign

Hawaii runs two recording systems: the Regular System at the Bureau of Conveyances and the Land Court, a Torrens-style registration system where title is shown on a certificate of title. Many older Oahu properties are registered in Land Court. Any deed of a co-owner's share, a trust transfer or an estate distribution has to be recorded in the right system, and a Land Court transfer can require extra steps and time. Before you sign anything, find out which system your property is in. A title company in Hawaii can tell you in a day.

🌺 Hawaiian Home Lands Leases Are a Different Animal

If the "house" is on Hawaiian Home Lands, the family doesn't own the land. The lessee holds a long-term homestead lease from the Department of Hawaiian Home Lands, and leases can generally be transferred only to qualified native Hawaiian beneficiaries with the department's approval. That means outside buyers, including us, can't buy it, and the co-ownership rules above don't work the usual way. Talk with the department and a Hawaii attorney about successorship and transfers.

⚖️ If No One Agrees: Partition in Circuit Court

When co-owners can't agree, any joint tenant or tenant in common can ask the circuit court for partition under HRS chapter 668, which can end in a division of the land or, for a single house, usually a sale. Hawaii also adopted the Uniform Partition of Heirs Property Act as HRS chapter 668A, which adds an appraisal, a buyout opportunity and a preference for an open-market sale when inherited property qualifies. Our partition lawsuit explainer covers how these cases generally work. In Hawaii, a partition case also keeps the clock running on the tax class, so the cost of a slow case is higher than it looks.

📊 Four Ways Hawaii Co-Owners Usually Go

🏡 Resident co-owner buys out the others

Often best in Hawaii

The property stays in the owner-occupied class, there's one sale instead of two, and HARPTA applies only to the mainland owners' portion. Financing at Hawaii prices is the hurdle.

🏷️ List it together

Best price for a sound house

If everyone will sign and the house shows well, Hawaii's tight inventory means a listing usually beats a cash offer. Mainland owners should apply for a withholding certificate early.

🧩 Sell just your share

Fastest for a tenant in common

Possible without the others' signatures, at a discount, because the buyer inherits the co-ownership. HARPTA applies to a nonresident's share sale too.

⚖️ Partition in circuit court

Last resort

It works when someone won't engage, but months of non-owner-occupied taxes and legal fees come out of everyone's proceeds.

💵 A Hilo House With One Sibling Home and Two on the Mainland

Three siblings inherited a plantation-style house in Hilo as tenants in common, a third each. The brother lives there. It needs a roof and termite work. Fixed up, it might sell for $520,000; as it stands, about $450,000. Their mother's date-of-death value was about $440,000, which is roughly their tax basis.

  • 🧾 Tax class. With the brother living there and a homeowner exemption on file, the Big Island homeowner rate of $5.75 per $1,000 applies, about $2,590 a year on $450,000 before the exemption reduces it. If he moves out and the property shifts to the residential class at $11.10, the bill is roughly $5,000 a year, over $400 a month.
  • ✈️ HARPTA on a $450,000 sale. The sisters' two-thirds is $300,000 of the amount realized, so the buyer withholds 7.25 percent of that, about $21,750, unless a certificate reduces it. With a stepped-up basis near $440,000, their actual gain is small, so most of that would come back, but only after they file. A Form N-288B application before closing can keep that money in their hands.
  • 🏡 Buyout by the brother at $450,000: he owes his sisters $300,000 less agreed credits. The house stays in the homeowner class. If he can finance it, this beats every other option for the family.
  • 💵 Cash sale: at 60 to 80 percent of the $520,000 repaired value, $312,000 to $416,000. For this house we'd expect around $360,000 to $390,000, closing in a few weeks. Add vacant-home insurance of $150 to $300 a month on the Big Island, utilities of $150 to $250, and yard and termite upkeep if the house sits empty while the family decides.
  • 🧩 One sister sells her third: a share buyer might pay $85,000 to $110,000, because they'd be co-owning with a brother who wants to stay.

The honest takeaway: if the brother can borrow, a buyout wins. If no one can, a listing after the roof and termite issues are disclosed will likely beat us by tens of thousands. We make sense when the repairs are bigger than the family can fund, the house is vacant and taxed at the higher rate, or the mainland siblings need a clean, certain closing.

📋 This Week's Checklist for Hawaii Co-Owners

  1. 📄 Get the deed and find out whether the property is in Land Court or the Regular System.
  2. 🏷️ Check the property's current tax class and exemption status with the county real property assessment office.
  3. ✈️ List every owner's state of residence. Nonresidents should talk to a tax preparer about Form N-288B before any contract is signed.
  4. 📉 If the house was inherited, document the date-of-death value. It sets the basis and the likely refund of any withholding.
  5. 🌺 Confirm the land isn't a Hawaiian Home Lands lease.
  6. ✉️ Put a written proposal in front of your co-owners: a buyout price, a listing plan or a cash offer, with how taxes and expenses will be credited.

✅ Bottom Line

In Hawaii, a co-ownership plan starts with residency. Keep the property in the owner-occupied class if someone lives there, plan for HARPTA if any owner lives off-island, and read the deed for entireties, joint tenancy or tenancy in common. A buyout by the owner who lives there is often the best outcome; a listing is usually second; a cash sale or a share sale wins when repairs, delay or an uncooperative co-owner would cost more than the discount.

❓ Frequently Asked Questions

Can one co-owner sell a house in Hawaii without the others?

A joint tenant or tenant in common can sell their own share without the others' signatures, but not the whole house. Spouses and reciprocal beneficiaries holding as tenants by the entirety must both sign.

What is HARPTA and does it apply to co-owners who live on the mainland?

Yes. HARPTA requires the buyer to withhold 7.25 percent of the amount realized by a nonresident seller. With mixed resident and nonresident co-owners, the amount is allocated by capital contribution and withheld on the nonresidents' portion.

Can a mainland co-owner reduce HARPTA withholding on an inherited house?

Often. Heirs usually get a stepped-up basis, so there may be little gain. Form N-288B lets a seller apply for a withholding certificate to reduce or eliminate withholding when there is no gain or not enough proceeds.

Does property tax go up if the co-owner living in a Hawaii house moves out?

It can. Each county taxes owner-occupied and non-owner-occupied homes at different rates. For fiscal 2027, Maui's non-owner-occupied rate is $6.25 per $1,000 versus $1.65 owner-occupied, and Hawaii County's is $11.10 versus $5.75.

Who can hold property as tenants by the entirety in Hawaii?

Spouses and reciprocal beneficiaries under HRS 509-2. Under Sawada v. Endo, entireties property isn't subject to the separate creditors of one spouse.

Can a creditor of one joint tenant reach a Hawaii house?

A joint tenant's interest, including the right of survivorship, can be subject to levy by that tenant's creditors. Entireties property is protected from one spouse's separate creditors; joint tenancy is not.

What is the difference between Land Court and the Regular System in Hawaii?

They're Hawaii's two recording systems. Land Court property has a registered certificate of title, and deeds and estate transfers must be recorded in the correct system, which can add steps to a co-owner sale.

Can a cash buyer purchase a house on Hawaiian Home Lands?

Generally no. Homestead leases from the Department of Hawaiian Home Lands can usually be transferred only to qualified native Hawaiian beneficiaries with the department's approval.

How much is Hawaii's conveyance tax on a co-owned house sale?

Under HRS 247-2, the rate on a property under $600,000 is 10 cents per $100, or 15 cents if the buyer of a home is ineligible for the county homeowner exemption, with higher rates on higher-value tiers.

Is a buyout better than selling a co-owned Hawaii house?

Often, when the co-owner who lives there can finance it. The property stays in the owner-occupied tax class and only the departing owners' portion is subject to HARPTA. If nobody can finance it, a listing usually nets more than a cash sale.
