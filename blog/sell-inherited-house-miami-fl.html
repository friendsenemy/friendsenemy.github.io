Inherited a Miami House? Florida's Homestead Rules Decide Who Gets to Sell It

In most states the will decides who inherits a house. In Florida, if the house was the owner's homestead and they left a spouse or a minor child, the Florida Constitution can override the will entirely. That one question, plus the property-tax reset most Miami heirs get hit with the following January, shapes everything else: who signs the deed, how fast you can sell, and whether listing or a cash sale nets more.

Most people who inherit a house in Miami start with the will. They find it, read who gets the house, and assume that's the end of the ownership question. In Florida it often isn't. The state constitution treats a person's homestead differently from every other asset they own, and those protections can quietly rewrite who owns the house the moment the owner dies.

That's why this guide starts with homestead status rather than with probate timelines or sale prices. Once you know whether the house was a protected homestead and who survived the owner, the rest of the decision gets much simpler. You'll know who has to sign, whether a court has to be involved, how much the property tax bill is about to rise, and whether a retail buyer can even get insurance on the place.

🏝️ Florida's Homestead Rule Can Override the Will

Article X, Section 4 of the Florida Constitution does two separate things to a homestead, and heirs regularly confuse them.

First, it protects the homestead from the owner's creditors. When the house passes to the owner's spouse or heirs, most of the owner's unsecured debts (credit cards, medical bills, personal loans) can't be collected against it. The mortgage and property taxes still can. This protection follows the house to the family, which is a real advantage Florida heirs have over heirs in most states.

Second, and this is the part that surprises people, it restricts who the owner can leave the homestead to. Under Article X, Section 4(c), if the owner is survived by a spouse or a minor child, the homestead can't be freely left by will. The single exception: if there is a spouse but no minor child, the owner may leave the homestead outright to the spouse. Any other gift of the homestead in that situation is ignored, and the house passes under the descent rules in Florida Statutes section 732.401 instead.

So a will that says "my house goes to my two kids from my first marriage" may simply not work if the owner had remarried and the new spouse survived. Families learn this after the funeral, usually from a title company that won't insure the sale.

The question to answer before anything else: was this the owner's permanent residence (did they claim the homestead exemption with the Miami-Dade Property Appraiser), and at death did they leave a spouse or any child under 18? If yes to both, the will may not control the house at all. Get that answered by a Florida probate attorney before you list, sign a contract, or promise anyone a share.

👨‍👩‍👧 Who Survived the Owner Changes Who Signs the Deed

Here's how the common Miami situations play out, simplified.

No spouse, no minor children

The owner could leave the house to anyone, and the will controls. If there's no will, Florida intestacy decides (usually adult children in equal shares). The house still keeps its creditor protection when it goes to family heirs. This is the cleanest version and the one where a quick sale is most realistic.

A surviving spouse, no minor children

If the will left the house to the spouse, the spouse owns it. If the will left it to anyone else, that gift fails, and section 732.401 gives the spouse a life estate with the remainder going to the owner's descendants. The spouse has a choice: within six months of the death, the spouse can elect to take an undivided one-half interest as a tenant in common instead of the life estate, with the other half going to the descendants. That election is irrevocable.

A minor child survived

The homestead can't be left by will at all. It passes by the descent rules, and the minor child ends up owning part of it. A minor can't sign a deed. Selling then usually requires a guardianship of the child's property and court approval of the sale, which adds months and legal fees. This is the Miami situation where a fast sale is least realistic, no matter who the buyer is.

Why the life estate matters for a sale

A life estate split between a surviving spouse and adult children is one of the hardest ownership setups to sell. The spouse has the right to live there for life and is responsible for ordinary taxes and upkeep; the children own the future. To sell the whole house, everyone has to sign. If they agree, the sale proceeds get divided by an actuarial value of the life estate. If they don't, nobody can force the spouse out, and there is no buyer for "the house, but someone may live in it for 25 more years" at a price anyone in the family will like.

📜 Protected Homestead Usually Skips the Personal Representative

In many states, an inherited house is sold by the executor. In Florida, protected homestead generally isn't part of the probate estate the personal representative manages. It passes directly to the people entitled to it at death. That means the owners named by the will or by section 732.401 sign the deed, not the personal representative acting alone.

Title insurers want proof that the house really was protected homestead and proof of who took it. The usual tool is a court order determining homestead status, which a probate attorney requests under Florida Probate Rule 5.405. In Miami-Dade, that petition is filed with the Probate Division of the Eleventh Judicial Circuit, often inside a summary or formal administration that's already open.

A house that was not a homestead (a rental, a second home, or a house the owner hadn't lived in for years) works the other way. It's an ordinary probate asset, exposed to creditor claims, and the personal representative can sell it. If the will grants a power of sale, the representative can usually sell without asking the court first; if it doesn't, or there is no will, the representative typically needs a court order authorizing the sale.

🧾 The Save Our Homes Reset: The Tax Bill Most Miami Heirs Don't See Coming

Florida's Save Our Homes amendment caps how much a homestead's assessed value can rise each year (3% or the change in the Consumer Price Index, whichever is lower). A Miami homeowner who bought in the 1990s may be taxed on an assessed value that's a fraction of the home's market value today.

That cap does not pass to most heirs. Under section 193.155, a change of ownership resets the assessment to full market value as of the next January 1. The major exception is a surviving spouse: a transfer to the spouse isn't treated as a change of ownership, so the cap carries over as long as the spouse keeps living there. A dependent who lived in the home may also qualify. Everyone else, typically adult children who live elsewhere, gets the full reset and loses the $50,000 homestead exemption too.

Here is what that looks like. Take a house in a City of Miami neighborhood with a market value of $560,000 that was assessed at $190,000 under the cap, with the homestead exemption. The owner's tax bill was roughly $3,200 a year. The 2025 proposed combined millage for the City of Miami was about 22.3 mills (county, school, state and city, before special districts). After the reset, the heirs are taxed on around $560,000 with no homestead exemption: about $12,500 a year, or more than $1,000 a month. In unincorporated Miami-Dade, the combined rate was closer to 16.5 mills, so the same house would run about $9,200 a year. Either way, the bill can triple or quadruple.

The reset takes effect January 1, shows up on the TRIM notice in August, and the bill arrives in November. Heirs who are still debating in October often don't feel the increase until a year after the death, when it's already locked in.

🏢 Inheriting a Miami Condo: Inspections, Reserves and the Board

A large share of inherited Miami property is condo units, and condos carry a Florida-specific issue that didn't exist a few years ago. After the 2021 Champlain Towers collapse in Surfside, Florida required milestone structural inspections for condo buildings three stories or taller once they reach 30 years old, then every 10 years, and structural integrity reserve studies (SIRS) that force associations to actually fund reserves for roofs, structure, waterproofing, plumbing and similar items. Associations that had kept dues low for decades are now catching up through big dues increases, special assessments, or loans.

For heirs, this cuts several ways:

  • The assessment follows the unit. If the association has voted a $40,000 special assessment, whoever owns the unit owes it. Estates often inherit a unit with an assessment already levied or about to be.
  • Buyers read the disclosures. Florida requires associations to give buyers financial information, including reserve funding and association debt. A building with a weak SIRS and an underfunded reserve sells at a discount, and some lenders won't finance units in it at all.
  • Board approval can slow a sale. Many Miami condo declarations give the association a right to approve buyers or a right of first refusal. Read the declaration before signing a contract with a short closing date.
  • Dues don't stop for probate. Unpaid assessments become a lien, and Florida associations can foreclose on them.

If the inherited unit is in an older coastal tower with a pending assessment, the list-versus-cash comparison changes a lot. A retail buyer using a mortgage may not be able to buy at all, and a cash buyer will subtract the assessment from the price. Get the association's estoppel certificate early so you know the real number.

⚖️ Getting Authority in the Eleventh Judicial Circuit

Even when homestead passes outside the estate, most Miami families still need some probate case, because something has to establish who the heirs are and that the house was protected. Florida gives you two main routes.

Summary administration

Available under section 735.201 when the estate subject to administration in Florida (not counting creditor-exempt property such as protected homestead) is $75,000 or less, or when the owner has been dead more than two years. There's no personal representative; the court issues an order distributing the property. For many Miami inherited-homestead cases, summary administration plus an order determining homestead is the efficient path. The clerk's filing fee is $340 for an estate of $1,000 or more (plus a $4 service charge). Uncontested cases often finish in one to three months.

Formal administration

Used when the estate is larger, a personal representative needs to sell a non-homestead house, or creditors have to be dealt with. The filing fee is $395 (plus the $4 charge). The personal representative publishes a notice to creditors, and creditors have three months from first publication to file claims. Realistic timelines in Miami-Dade run six to twelve months for an uncontested estate, longer if anyone objects.

Two Florida rules that trip up out-of-state families

  • Who can serve as personal representative. Under section 733.304, someone who doesn't live in Florida can serve only if they're related to the deceased (for example a child, parent, sibling, spouse, or certain other relatives). A non-relative friend in New York can't be appointed. Plenty of Miami families have one child up north who expected to handle everything.
  • Attorney fees follow a statutory schedule. Section 733.6171 presumes a reasonable fee for ordinary formal administration based on the estate's value: $1,500 up to $40,000, stepping up to $3,000 at $100,000, plus 3% of the value from $100,000 to $1 million. A $400,000 probate estate works out to about $12,000. Homestead determinations and contested issues can be billed as extra services. Many attorneys quote flat fees for summary administration, often $1,500-$4,000.

🔀 The Realistic Paths, Rated Honestly

Listing the house on the open market

Best for most sound houses

Miami-Dade still has deep retail demand, and a house in decent shape in Westchester, Coral Way or Miami Gardens will usually sell for more on the MLS than any investor will pay. If the roof is reasonably new, the heirs agree and nobody needs money in the next 60 days, list it. That's our honest recommendation, even though it means you won't sell to us.

Selling as-is for cash

Best when the house can't pass inspection

Florida's insurance market is the hidden factor here. Most retail buyers need a mortgage, the lender needs homeowner's insurance, and insurers routinely decline or surcharge homes with older roofs or outdated electrical and plumbing found on a four-point inspection. A 1960s concrete-block house with a 20-year-old roof can be hard to sell to a financed buyer at any price. A cash buyer doesn't need an insurer's approval to close.

Keeping it

Strong only for the surviving spouse

A surviving spouse who keeps the Save Our Homes cap and the homestead exemption may have the cheapest housing they'll ever get. Adult children who inherit with a fully reset tax bill, high wind insurance and a flood zone often find that renting it out barely breaks even.

Selling one share

A way out of a deadlock

Once heirs hold title as tenants in common, any one of them can sell their own undivided share without the others. The price is discounted because the buyer inherits the dispute. It's worth it when the alternative is a partition lawsuit; see our guide to how partition lawsuits work and our Florida guide on what to do when heirs can't agree.

💵 A Cash Offer on an Inherited Miami House, With the Math

Cash buyers generally pay about 60-80% of the after-repair value (what the house would sell for fully fixed up), minus nothing else; the discount already covers repairs, holding costs and profit. Where in that range depends on how much work the house needs and how sure the title is.

Example: a 1962 three-bedroom concrete-block house in unincorporated Westchester. Fixed up, comparable homes sell for about $600,000. It needs a new roof (around $25,000-$30,000 for a typical shingle roof in Miami-Dade), electrical panel and plumbing updates to pass a four-point inspection, plus a kitchen and baths: roughly $75,000 in total. A cash offer would likely land between $400,000 and $440,000, about 67-73% of repaired value.

Now the listing route. Listed as-is, the house might draw $500,000 from a buyer who can get insurance, or less if the roof scares off financed buyers. Subtract about 5-6% in commission ($25,000-$30,000), Florida documentary stamp tax on the deed (Miami-Dade's rate for single-family homes is 60 cents per $100, about $3,000 here), title and closing costs, and three to five months of carrying costs. The two numbers can end up surprisingly close on a house like this. On a renovated house with a new roof, listing wins clearly.

What holding it costs each month

  • Property tax after the reset: at about 16.5 mills on a $520,000 as-is value, roughly $715 a month in unincorporated Miami-Dade; about $970 a month at the City of Miami's roughly 22.3 mills.
  • Insurance: a vacant-dwelling policy with windstorm coverage in Miami-Dade commonly runs $350-$600 a month, more in a flood zone, and many standard policies stop covering a home that's been vacant for 30-60 days.
  • Utilities: $150-$250 a month to keep power, water and some air conditioning running (humidity damage starts fast in a closed-up Miami house).
  • Upkeep: lawn, pool, pest control and hurricane preparation, $200-$400 a month.

That's about $1,400-$2,200 a month. A year of indecision can cost $17,000-$26,000 before counting a hurricane season.

📋 What To Do This Week

  1. Look up the property on the Miami-Dade Property Appraiser's website. Note whether a homestead exemption was in place and the current assessed versus market value. That tells you whether a tax reset is coming and how big.
  2. Write down exactly who survived the owner: spouse (and whether married at death), every child, and the age of each. That list largely decides who owns the house under Florida law.
  3. Find the will and any deed changes. Check the Miami-Dade Clerk's official records for a life estate deed (sometimes called a Lady Bird deed) or trust deed, which can let the house skip probate entirely.
  4. Insure the house. Tell the current insurer about the death and vacancy, and get a vacant-dwelling policy if needed. A hurricane claim denied for vacancy is the worst outcome on this list.
  5. If it's a condo, request the estoppel certificate and ask the association about milestone inspection status, reserve funding and any pending assessments.
  6. See a Florida probate attorney about homestead status and whether summary administration fits. Ask for a flat fee.
  7. Get two numbers before deciding: an agent's pricing opinion for the as-is and repaired house, and a written cash offer. Compare net proceeds after costs and months, not headline prices.

✅ Bottom Line

For an inherited Miami house, start with homestead: whether it was the owner's residence and whether a spouse or minor child survived. That determines who owns it, which overrides the will more often than families expect. Then look at the Save Our Homes reset, which can triple the tax bill for heirs other than a surviving spouse, and, for condos, the building's inspection and reserve situation.

If the house is insurable, the heirs agree and there's no rush, list it; you'll likely net more. ❓ Frequently Asked Questions

Does a will control who inherits a homestead house in Florida?

Not always. Under Article X, Section 4(c) of the Florida Constitution, if the owner is survived by a spouse or a minor child, the homestead cannot be freely left by will. The only exception is leaving it outright to the spouse when there is no minor child. Otherwise the house passes under section 732.401 regardless of what the will says.

What does a surviving spouse get in a Florida homestead if the will left it to someone else?

The spouse gets a life estate, with the remainder going to the owner's descendants. Within six months of the death, the spouse can instead elect to take an undivided one-half interest as a tenant in common, with the other half going to the descendants. The election is irrevocable.

Can a personal representative in Miami sell an inherited homestead house?

Usually not alone. Protected homestead generally passes directly to the heirs or beneficiaries at death rather than becoming part of the probate estate, so the people who own it sign the deed. Title companies typically want a court order determining homestead status first.

Will property taxes go up when I inherit a house in Miami-Dade?

Usually yes. Under section 193.155, a change of ownership resets the Save Our Homes capped value to full market value on the next January 1, and the homestead exemption ends unless an heir moves in and qualifies. A surviving spouse who keeps living there is the main exception and keeps the cap.

How much is the Miami-Dade property tax rate on an inherited house?

It depends on the city. The 2025 proposed combined millage was about 22.3 mills in the City of Miami and about 16.5 mills in unincorporated Miami-Dade, before special districts. On a $500,000 taxable value that is roughly $8,200 to $11,200 a year.

What is summary administration in Florida and does it work for a house?

Summary administration under section 735.201 is a shorter court process with no personal representative. It is available when the Florida estate subject to administration, not counting creditor-exempt property like protected homestead, is $75,000 or less, or when the owner died more than two years ago. Paired with an order determining homestead, it is often enough to clear title to an inherited Miami homestead.

Can I be personal representative of a Miami estate if I live in another state?

Only if you are related to the deceased. Section 733.304 allows a nonresident to serve as personal representative only if they are a qualifying relative, such as a child, parent, sibling or spouse. A nonresident friend cannot serve.

What happens if a minor child inherits part of a Miami house?

A minor cannot sign a deed, so selling usually requires a guardianship of the child's property and court approval of the sale. That adds months and legal fees, and it is the situation where a fast sale is least realistic for any buyer.

How do the new Florida condo laws affect selling an inherited Miami condo?

Buildings three or more stories must have milestone inspections at 30 years and every 10 years after, plus structural integrity reserve studies. Many associations are raising dues or levying special assessments to catch up. Those assessments stay with the unit, buyers see the reserve disclosures, and some lenders will not finance units in underfunded buildings.

Is it better to list an inherited Miami house or sell it for cash?

If the house is insurable, especially with a newer roof, and the heirs agree and are not in a hurry, listing usually nets more. A cash sale tends to win when the roof or systems would fail a four-point inspection, a condo assessment is pending, the title involves a life estate or minor heir, or carrying costs after the tax reset are draining the estate.
