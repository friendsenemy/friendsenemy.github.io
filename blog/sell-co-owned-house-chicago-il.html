Co-Own a Chicago House With Someone Who Won't Sell? Illinois Splits the Cost of Saying No

In a lot of states, the co-owner who files a partition lawsuit carries most of the legal bill. Illinois spreads it out: the court can order every owner to pay an equitable share of the filing owner's reasonable attorney fees, unless the holdout has a real defense. That changes the negotiation for anyone stuck co-owning a Chicago house or two-flat. Here's how partition works in Cook County, how inherited shares are treated, and when a sale on the open market beats every other exit.

By Dustin Ray, Founder of Pages of Purpose LLC. Not legal advice. Illinois property and partition law and Cook County court practice change, laws differ by state, and every co-ownership is different. Talk to an attorney licensed in Illinois about the property before you file anything or sign a contract.

Co-owning property works right up until the owners want different things. One wants to sell; another wants to keep living there, or keep collecting rent, or just won't answer the phone. In Chicago this happens constantly, because so much of the city's housing is the kind people end up sharing: brick two-flats and three-flats bought by relatives together, bungalows inherited by three siblings, and houses titled to unmarried partners who later split up.

Illinois law gives every co-owner (other than spouses holding their home as tenants by the entirety) a way out through partition. What makes Illinois different is who pays for that way out, and that's where this guide starts.

💸 Illinois Can Make the Holdout Help Pay the Filer's Lawyer

Partition in Illinois is governed by Article XVII of the Code of Civil Procedure, starting at 735 ILCS 5/17-101. The cost provision, 735 ILCS 5/17-125, says the court apportions the costs of the case among the parties in interest, including a reasonable fee for the plaintiff's attorney, so that each party pays an equitable portion. The exception: if a defendant raises a good and substantial defense to the complaint, that defendant can recover costs from the plaintiff instead.

In practice, that means a co-owner who simply refuses to sell, with no real legal argument against partition, may end up paying a share of the lawyer the other owner hired to force the sale. If two siblings each own half, the holdout can be ordered to bear roughly half of the plaintiff's reasonable fees out of their share of the sale proceeds.

That has two practical effects:

  • It gives the owner who wants out real leverage. "If you make me file, you'll likely help pay for it" is a credible statement in Illinois, and a letter from an attorney saying so often restarts negotiations.
  • It cuts against the reluctant owner who stalls. Delay tactics that run up the plaintiff's bill can come back to the person using them. Owners with a legitimate dispute (about who owns what share, or credits for money paid in) are in a different position, because a substantial defense changes how costs fall.
Don't read this as "partition is cheap." Cost-sharing doesn't make the case free. Everyone's share of the fees, the commissioners or appraisal, the sale costs and months of carrying costs come out of the same pot of equity. A negotiated sale almost always leaves more money for everyone than a judgment does. The fee rule matters most as a reason for the holdout to negotiate.

🏚️ Chicago Two-Flats Make Co-Ownership Messier

A single-family house shared by co-owners has one main question: who lives there. A two-flat or three-flat adds a second one: who collects the rent, and what happens to it.

  • The co-owner living in a unit. Under the general rule in Illinois, a co-owner who occupies the property doesn't automatically owe rent to the other owners just for living there, unless they've shut the others out. That's frustrating for the owner who lives elsewhere and gets nothing from the building.
  • Rent from tenants is different. Rent collected from third-party tenants belongs to all the owners in proportion to their shares, after legitimate expenses. A co-owner who pockets the garden-unit rent can be made to account for it, and in a partition case the court can settle those accounts out of the sale proceeds.
  • Expenses cut both ways. The owner who paid the property taxes, insurance, and a new boiler can usually ask for credit for the other owners' share of those costs. Keep receipts; a lot of Chicago partition disputes turn on ten years of undocumented payments.
  • Tenants stay with the building. A tenant with a lease is part of the sale. Chicago's Residential Landlord and Tenant Ordinance applies to most rental units in the city, and buyers price tenant-occupied units differently from vacant ones.

Physically dividing a two-flat between owners, one unit each, sounds natural but rarely works legally; it generally means converting the building to condominiums, with surveys, declarations and lender issues. Most Cook County partition cases involving two-flats end in a sale.

📑 How the Title Is Held Changes Your Options

Before anything else, pull the deed from the Cook County Clerk's recordings search and read the words after the owners' names.

Tenants in common

Each owner has a separate, undivided share they can sell, mortgage or leave by will. Any owner can file for partition. This is the default when the deed doesn't say otherwise, and how most heirs end up holding inherited property.

Joint tenancy with right of survivorship

When one owner dies, the survivors take their share automatically. But a joint tenant can still sell or convey their own interest, which generally severs the joint tenancy as to that share and turns it into a tenancy in common. Joint tenants can also be sued for partition.

Tenancy by the entirety

Illinois allows this only for a married couple's homestead, and only when the deed expressly says so (765 ILCS 1005/1c). Neither spouse can sell, mortgage or lease the property alone, and partition isn't available while the marriage lasts. On divorce, it becomes a tenancy in common by operation of law unless the court orders otherwise. If you're divorcing, our divorce selling guide is the better starting point.

Illinois land trusts

Chicago has more property held in land trusts than almost anywhere. The bank or trust company holds legal title, and the beneficiaries' interests are personal property controlled by the trust agreement. Who can direct a sale, and whether one beneficiary can force it, depends on that agreement. Get a copy before assuming normal co-owner rules apply.

🧬 Inherited Shares: The Uniform Partition of Heirs Property Act

Illinois adopted the Uniform Partition of Heirs Property Act effective August 23, 2019 (755 ILCS 75). It applies to "heirs property": roughly, property held by tenants in common without a binding agreement on partition, where enough of the ownership traces to relatives (such as an inherited bungalow owned by siblings and cousins). When a partition case involves heirs property, the Act changes the process:

  • The court generally orders an appraisal to set fair market value, rather than leaving value to an auction.
  • Co-owners who didn't ask for the sale get the chance to buy out the share of the owner who did, at the appraised value, before a sale is ordered.
  • The court has to consider whether dividing the property in kind is feasible, weighing factors including the family's ties to the property.
  • If a sale is ordered, the default is an open-market sale through a real estate broker at a price at least equal to the appraisal, not a courthouse auction.

For a Chicago family, that means an heir who wants out can't simply force a fire sale, and the heirs who want to keep the house get a real chance to buy the leaving heir out at a fair price. If you're in the middle of an inheritance standoff, our guide on what to do when heirs can't agree covers the decision in more depth.

🏛️ What a Cook County Partition Case Actually Looks Like

Partition cases in Cook County are filed in the Circuit Court, Chancery Division, at the Daley Center. A typical case:

  1. Complaint and service on every owner and anyone with a recorded interest (mortgage lenders, judgment creditors). Finding and serving relatives who live out of state can take months on its own.
  2. Determining shares. The court confirms who owns what percentage and resolves claims for credits, such as one owner paying the taxes for years.
  3. Division or sale. The statute provides for commissioners to decide whether the property can be divided without prejudice to the owners. For a single house or two-flat the answer is almost always no, and the court orders a sale. For heirs property, the Uniform Act steps above apply first.
  4. Sale and distribution. The sale is confirmed by the court, liens are paid, costs and fees are apportioned, and the balance is divided by share after accounting credits.

Realistic timeline: 9-18 months uncontested to modestly contested; longer with service problems or a real fight over shares. Realistic cost: $7,500-$25,000 or more in total attorney fees on a simple case, before appraisal and sale costs, and much more if it's litigated hard. 🧾 Cook County Taxes Keep Running During the Standoff

Every month a co-owned Chicago property sits in dispute costs money, and property tax is the largest piece.

  • Property tax. Cook County assesses homes at 10% of market value, multiplied by a state equalizer (about 2.92 for tax year 2024). Effective rates in Chicago proper typically run around 1.8-2.0% of market value; many suburbs are higher. On a $450,000 two-flat, that's roughly $8,100-$9,000 a year, or $675-$750 a month. If the co-owner who lived there loses the homeowner exemption (which takes $10,000 off the equalized value), the bill rises further.
  • Tax delinquency. Bills come in two installments. If co-owners stop paying, the taxes can be offered at the Cook County annual tax sale, and a tax buyer's lien carries steep penalties that eat into everyone's equity.
  • Insurance. A two-flat with a vacant unit may need a different policy; expect $150-$300 a month, more if both units are empty.
  • Heat and utilities. A vacant unit still has to be heated through a Chicago winter or the pipes freeze. Budget $150-$400 a month in winter.
  • City compliance. Rear wooden porches, masonry and tuckpointing, and snow removal on the public sidewalk all generate city enforcement on neglected buildings.

Six months of standoff on a $450,000 two-flat easily costs $7,000-$10,000 before any repairs.

🔀 The Exits, Honestly Rated

List it together

Best price for most buildings

Chicago two-flats in neighborhoods like Logan Square, Avondale, Bridgeport and Pilsen draw owner-occupant buyers who want the rental income to help with the mortgage. If every owner agrees and the building is in decent shape, listing beats any cash offer, including ours.

Buy out the other owner

Cleanest if someone wants to stay

The owner who lives upstairs often wants to keep the building. A buyout at an agreed appraisal, with credits for taxes and repairs each side paid, avoids court entirely. It works only if the buyer can get financing; settle that before arguing about price.

Sell your share

Fast exit from a deadlock

A tenant in common can sell their share without anyone's permission. The buyer takes on the dispute, so the price for a partial interest is well below the share's proportional value. It makes sense when you need out now, when the other owners won't negotiate, or when you don't want to be the plaintiff in a family lawsuit.

File for partition

The backstop that forces a decision

In Illinois, the fee-sharing rule makes partition a more realistic threat than in many states. Often the filing alone produces a settlement. Go in knowing the full timeline and costs.

💵 Pricing a Pilsen Two-Flat, Three Ways

Cash buyers generally pay about 60-80% of after-repair value, with the low end for buildings needing heavy work. Here's a realistic example.

A 1900s brick two-flat in Pilsen, owned 50/50 by two siblings. Fully renovated, similar buildings sell for about $560,000. It needs a new rear porch system, tuckpointing, a boiler, and a gut rehab of the vacant first-floor unit: about $110,000. One sibling lives upstairs.

  • Whole building, sold for cash as-is: about $350,000-$400,000 (roughly 63-71% of repaired value), closing in three to five weeks, no commission. Each sibling nets about half, minus liens and their share of closing costs.
  • Whole building, listed as-is: perhaps $430,000-$460,000 to an owner-occupant or small investor, minus 5-6% commission, the state and Cook County transfer taxes customarily paid by the seller (75 cents per $500 combined) plus the seller's share of Chicago's city transfer tax, and three to four months of carrying costs. Net around $395,000-$425,000. If both siblings cooperate, listing nets more.
  • One sibling's half share, sold alone: because the buyer inherits an occupied building and a co-owner dispute, a half interest typically sells for well under half the building's value. Expect something like $110,000-$150,000 here. Worse money, but it ends that sibling's involvement in weeks.

The honest takeaway: if the two owners can cooperate even briefly, selling the whole building is clearly better for both. A partial-interest sale is for when that cooperation is not going to happen.

📋 What To Do This Week

  1. Pull the deed from the Cook County Clerk's recordings search and note exactly how title is held. If it's in a land trust, request the trust agreement.
  2. Gather payment records for property taxes, insurance, repairs and the mortgage over the years. Credits for those payments often decide who gets what.
  3. If there are tenants, get the leases and a rent ledger. Rent from tenants belongs to all owners by share.
  4. Check the tax status on the Cook County Treasurer's site, including whether any taxes have been sold.
  5. Get two numbers: an agent's pricing opinion and a written cash offer, so the conversation with the other owner is about real figures.
  6. Send a written proposal to the other owners: list together, a buyout at appraisal, or a deadline after which you'll pursue your options.
  7. Talk to an Illinois real estate attorney about partition, the Heirs Property Act if the property was inherited, and how fees would likely be apportioned.

✅ Bottom Line

Any Illinois co-owner except a spouse holding the home in tenancy by the entirety can force a sale through partition, and Illinois lets the court spread the filing owner's reasonable attorney fees across every owner who has no real defense. Inherited property gets extra protection under the Heirs Property Act, which favors appraisals, buyouts and broker sales over auctions. In a Chicago two-flat, rent and expense credits often matter as much as the sale price.

If the owners can agree, even reluctantly, list the building; the market will pay more than we will. If they can't, or you need out before a lawsuit would finish, ask us. We'll tell you honestly whether buying the whole property, buying your share, or neither is the right move.

❓ Frequently Asked Questions

Can a co-owner force the sale of a house in Illinois?

Yes, in most cases. Any co-owner holding as a tenant in common or joint tenant can file a partition action under 735 ILCS 5/17-101 and following. The main exception is a married couple's homestead held as tenants by the entirety, which can't be partitioned while the marriage lasts.

Who pays the attorney fees in an Illinois partition case?

Under 735 ILCS 5/17-125, the court apportions costs among the parties, including a reasonable fee for the plaintiff's attorney, so each pays an equitable portion. A defendant who raises a good and substantial defense can instead recover their costs from the plaintiff.

Does the Uniform Partition of Heirs Property Act apply in Illinois?

Yes. Illinois adopted it effective August 23, 2019, at 755 ILCS 75. For qualifying inherited property, it provides for an appraisal, a right for the other co-owners to buy out the share of the owner seeking partition, consideration of division in kind, and a default open-market sale through a broker if a sale is ordered.

Can one owner of a Chicago two-flat be split off with their own unit?

Rarely. Giving each owner a unit generally means converting the building to condominiums, which takes surveys, a declaration and lender cooperation. Most Cook County partition cases involving two-flats end in a sale of the whole building.

My co-owner lives in the property. Do they owe me rent?

Generally not just for living there, unless they have shut you out of the property. Rent they collect from third-party tenants is different and is shared by all owners according to their interests, after legitimate expenses, and it can be accounted for in a partition case.

Can I sell my share of a jointly owned house in Illinois without the other owner?

If you hold as a tenant in common, yes. A joint tenant can also convey their interest, which generally severs the joint tenancy as to that share. Spouses holding a homestead as tenants by the entirety cannot sell alone.

How long does a partition lawsuit take in Cook County?

Commonly 9 to 18 months for an uncontested or modestly contested case filed in the Chancery Division, and longer when owners are hard to serve or contest their shares.

What happens to property held in an Illinois land trust when beneficiaries disagree?

The trustee holds legal title, and the beneficiaries' rights, including who can direct a sale, are set by the trust agreement. Read that agreement before assuming the normal co-owner and partition rules apply.

How much are property taxes on a co-owned Chicago house?

Cook County assesses homes at 10% of market value, multiplied by a state equalizer. Effective rates in Chicago typically run about 1.8 to 2.0% of market value, so a $450,000 property pays roughly $8,100 to $9,000 a year, more if a homeowner exemption is lost.

Should co-owners list a Chicago property or sell it for cash?

If all owners agree and the property is in reasonable shape, listing usually nets the most. A cash sale of the whole property tends to make sense when it needs heavy work or carrying costs are mounting, and selling a single share makes sense when the other owners won't cooperate and you need out without filing suit.
