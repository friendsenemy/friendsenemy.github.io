In Pennsylvania, a Partition Can Become an Auction Where Only the Owners Bid

Pennsylvania runs partition through court rules rather than a statute. If owners reject the plan, the house can go to a private sale by open bidding confined to the parties. Whoever has the money ready has the advantage. And who ends up buying decides whether a transfer tax of 2% or more applies.

By Dustin Ray, Founder of Pages of Purpose LLC. Not legal advice — laws differ by state, and Pennsylvania counties handle partition cases differently in practice. Talk to an attorney licensed in Pennsylvania, where the property is, before you rely on any of this.

Here's something that surprises people: Pennsylvania doesn't have a typical partition statute. It runs this process through its Rules of Civil Procedure, Rules 1551 to 1574. They produce a result that's different from what most co-owners picture. You may imagine a sheriff's sale to strangers. In many Pennsylvania cases, the deciding moment is smaller and more personal: you, your co-owner, and a round of bidding between the two of you.

That has a practical consequence. If you want to keep the house, the question is how much cash you can raise and how fast. If you want out, your co-owner's ability to bid sets a floor under what you'll get. Either way, knowing that early beats being surprised at a hearing.

🔨 The Private Sale: Bidding Against Your Own Co-Owner

Pennsylvania's Superior Court has called partition "a possessory action" and an "absolute right" that comes with a tenancy in common, in cases such as Lombardo v. DeMarco and Bernstein v. Sherman. So your co-owner can't simply refuse. How it ends is what matters. Pennsylvania courts describe a two-step process. First the court decides partition is due, then it works out how:

  1. A complaint in the county where the property sits, naming every co-tenant.
  2. An order directing partition. Rule 1557 covers it. Since the right is absolute, this step is usually not the fight.
  3. A master or hearing officer, commonly appointed under Rule 1558(b), who looks at value and how to divide the property, usually with an appraisal.
  4. A proposed plan. Rule 1560 addresses dividing the property into shares. A single house generally can't be divided, so the plan may allot it to one owner, with owelty, an equalizing payment, paid to the others.
  5. Accept or reject. Under Rule 1566(b)(1), parties have 20 days to accept or reject the proposed allocation.
  6. A private sale. If the allotment is rejected, Rule 1566(c) sends it to a "private sale by open bidding confined to the parties," on at least 20 days' notice.
  7. Public sale if required. Under Rule 1570, the court decides whether a sale is required and whether private or public will yield the better price.

Bernstein confirms that rejecting a master's recommendation and insisting on a private sale is allowed under the rules. There, a master's plan on eight Philadelphia parcels was thrown over in favor of a private sale. So a plan you dislike isn't the end.

⚠️ The owner with financing wins. In a sale confined to the parties, only the parties bid. If your co-owner shows up with a loan commitment and you have only a hope of one, the bidding ends where they want it to end. If you mean to keep the house, get pre-approved before the hearing, not after. If you want to sell, remember a low bid by the only bidder is a risk, so ask your lawyer whether a public sale would bring a better price under Rule 1570.

A bidding example

Two sisters each own half of a house in Upper Darby with a $255,000 appraisal. One bids; the other can raise. Suppose the bidding ends at $240,000 with the sister who lives there. She pays her sister half, $120,000, less adjustments, and takes the deed. If she had stopped at $200,000 because that's all her lender would give, the other sister could top it, but only if she wants to own the house. If no one else can bid, the appraisal doesn't automatically protect the seller, which is why the choice of a private or public sale is worth arguing about.

🧾 Transfer Tax: A Sibling Can Skip It. A Friend Can't.

Pennsylvania charges a 1% state realty transfer tax, and local taxes usually add at least another 1%. In Philadelphia the bite is much bigger. The city's website lists 3.578% city plus 1% state for 4.578% total, though the same page and other sources also show a 3.278% city rate, so confirm the current figure before you budget.

What makes this a planning point is the family exclusion in 61 Pa. Code § 91.193. It excludes transfers between spouses; between parents and children and other lineal relatives; between siblings ("children of the same parent"); between an individual and a sibling's spouse; and between divorced people for real estate acquired before or during the marriage. Philadelphia has its own family exemption and requires documentation.

Two traps. The regulation has a one-year rule: if the family member who received the transfer resells within a year, it's taxed as if the original owner made the transfer. So a sibling can't take your share tax-free and flip it next month without a bill. Also, an estate isn't itself a family member, which matters if the house is still in a deceased parent's estate. Only inherited property gets the partition exclusion: under § 91.193(b)(5), a division in kind for no or nominal consideration of property passed by will or intestacy is excluded, but tax is due on the excess if someone takes more than their share.

🧮 Occupancy, Taxes and the Credit Fight

Rule 1570(a)(5) is the one to know if a co-owner lives there. The court's findings must address "the credit which should be allowed or the charge which should be made" for use and occupancy, taxes, rents or other amounts paid. In other words, the rules put those items on the table. A co-owner who paid the real estate taxes for years will ask for credit. The owner who has been out will ask for a charge for occupancy. How they're decided depends on the facts, and a Pennsylvania lawyer can tell you how that's playing out in your county.

Encumbrances are part of the findings too, in Rule 1570(a)(4). Unpaid taxes, a mortgage, or a lien against one owner all come out of the process. In Philadelphia in particular, rowhouses with back taxes or an old deceased owner on the deed are a common problem. Bring a title search before you bring a theory.

If you rely on the master's conferences to prove credits, get a record. In Bernstein the appeals court declined to review improvement and credit arguments because the master's conferences hadn't been transcribed.

🏚️ Inherited Shares and the Pennsylvania Inheritance Tax

Many co-owned Pennsylvania houses got that way through a parent's death. The state's inheritance tax runs by relationship: no tax for a surviving spouse, 4.5% for children and other lineal heirs, 12% for siblings, and 15% for most others, including nieces, nephews and friends. The return is generally due nine months after death, with a 5% discount for paying within three months. If it was never filed on the house, expect a title company to ask about it. That's a cost to sort out before splitting proceeds. Our guide to heirs who can't agree and our guide on what happens to a house with no will cover the family side.

A bill to adopt the Uniform Partition of Heirs Property Act, HB 1498, passed the Pennsylvania House in June 2025, 181–22, and a Senate companion was filed. We could not confirm that it became law. If it has, inherited houses could get a separate buyout process, so ask your attorney for the current status.

👩‍⚖️ What the Master, the Lawyers and the Calendar Cost

As one Bucks County firm has noted, the parties generally split the fees in the case, including paying the master. Rule 1570 doesn't itself shift attorney fees, so plan on paying your own lawyer. Partition suits are uncommon in many Pennsylvania counties, which means fewer judges see them, and it's worth finding an attorney who has handled one.

  • Attorney fees: our estimate is $4,000–$10,000 for a simple case and $15,000–$35,000 if the credits or the plan are contested.
  • Master and appraisal: a few thousand dollars, split among the owners.
  • Timeline: about 9–18 months, longer with exceptions. Post-trial motions have to be filed properly before an appeal, as the Superior Court noted in Funk v. Empfield.

For how these compare, see our guide on how partition lawsuits work. If your co-owner is an ex-spouse, start with selling a house during a divorce, since Pennsylvania treats entireties property differently while a marriage lasts.

Family buyout

Often the cheapest of all

Between siblings or a parent and child, a buyout can avoid transfer tax altogether, and it needs no agent. Agree on an appraisal, adjust for credits, and let the occupant refinance. If you can get this done, it's hard to beat.

A sound house with willing owners should go on the market. You'll pay commission and transfer tax, and still come out ahead of a cash sale.

📋 What To Do This Week

  1. Pull the deed from the county Recorder of Deeds. Philadelphia's records are through the Department of Records.
  2. Write down how each owner is related. That tells you whether the family exclusion may apply.
  3. Check for delinquent real estate taxes, water bills, liens and inheritance tax on any deceased owner.
  4. Make a list of who paid what, with dates. Rule 1570 puts it in play.
  5. If you want to keep the house, see a lender now, ahead of any bidding.
  6. Call a Pennsylvania attorney who has handled a partition and ask if a buyout letter should come first.

✅ Bottom Line

A Pennsylvania co-owner can always get a partition. What matters is who's prepared when it reaches the bidding, and who the buyer is for transfer tax purposes. If the owners are family, try a buyout first. If the house is sound, list it. If someone won't cooperate, file with your financing and your ledger ready.

❓ Frequently Asked Questions

Can my co-owner stop me from partitioning a Pennsylvania house?

Generally no. Pennsylvania's Superior Court has called partition an absolute right that comes with a tenancy in common. The real dispute is usually over how it's divided or sold, and the credits.

What is a private sale in a Pennsylvania partition?

If a proposed allotment is rejected, Rule 1566(c) provides for a private sale by open bidding confined to the parties, on at least 20 days' notice. Only the co-owners bid. The court can also consider whether a public sale would yield a better price under Rule 1570.

What law governs partition in Pennsylvania?

The Pennsylvania Rules of Civil Procedure, Rules 1551 through 1574, rather than a typical partition statute.

Do siblings pay realty transfer tax on a buyout in Pennsylvania?

Generally not. 61 Pa. Code § 91.193 excludes transfers between siblings, spouses, and parents and children. The transferee shouldn't resell within a year, or tax may be due as if the original owner had made the transfer.

What is the transfer tax in Philadelphia?

The city's website lists 3.578% city plus 1% state for 4.578% total, but other pages show a 3.278% city rate. Confirm the current figure when you budget.

Does a Pennsylvania court charge the co-owner living in the house for occupancy?

Rule 1570(a)(5) says the court's findings address credits and charges for use and occupancy, taxes, rents and other amounts paid. Whether a charge is made depends on the facts.

Who pays the master in a Pennsylvania partition case?

Pennsylvania firms report that the parties generally split the case's fees, including the master's compensation. Rule 1570 doesn't itself shift attorney fees, so plan on paying your own lawyer.

What does inheritance tax have to do with selling a co-owned Pennsylvania house?

If an owner died, inheritance tax may be owed at 4.5% for children, 12% for siblings or 15% for most others, and a buyer's title company will likely ask about it.

Has Pennsylvania adopted the Uniform Partition of Heirs Property Act?

HB 1498 passed the House in June 2025, but we couldn't confirm that it became law. Ask a Pennsylvania attorney for the current status.

Should Pennsylvania co-owners list or sell for cash?

For a sound house, list it or do a family buyout. Cash fits a house with heavy repairs or back taxes, or an owner who won't cooperate.
