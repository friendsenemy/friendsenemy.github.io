Partition Lawsuits in Illinois: No Sale Below Two-Thirds of the Court's Value, and a 45-Day Right to Buy Your Co-Owner Out

Illinois is one of the few states that puts a hard price floor under a court-ordered sale: under 735 ILCS 5/17-105 the judge fixes a value first, and no sale can be approved for less than two-thirds of it. For heirs property there is also an appraisal, a 45-day buyout at full appraised value, and a listed sale through a broker instead of an auction. All of it runs off one hearing. Most people who get served with a partition complaint assume the worst: a sheriff's auction, a fire-sale price, and whatever is left split four ways. In Illinois that fear is half wrong, and the half that is wrong is the expensive half.

Illinois is one of the few states whose partition statute puts a hard price floor under a court-ordered sale. Under 735 ILCS 5/17-105, if the court finds the property cannot be divided without manifest prejudice to the owners, it must fix the value of the premises before selling — and then "no sale may be approved for less than two-thirds of the total amount of the valuation." If the bids come in under that line, the judge can revalue the property and approve, or throw out the sale and order a new one. Nothing gets rubber-stamped at 40 cents on the dollar.

That single sentence reorganizes the whole case. The most important day in an Illinois partition is not the sale. It is the hearing where the number gets set.

💵 The Two-Thirds Floor, and What It Really Protects

Work the arithmetic before you panic. A court values a Berwyn two-flat at $250,000 as it sits. Two-thirds of that is about $166,700. No bid below that can be confirmed without the judge first revaluing the property on the record. Compare that to a mortgage foreclosure sale in Illinois, where no statutory floor applies at all, and you can see why partition is the less brutal of the two forced sales.

What the floor does not do is get you market value. Two-thirds of a court-fixed value, minus the costs the statute taxes against the owners, is still a discount to a clean listing. And the floor only bites if the valuation was honest. An owner who shows up to the valuation hearing with a comparative market analysis, repair bids and recent sales of comparable two-flats is protecting two-thirds of a real number. An owner who skips the hearing is protecting two-thirds of whatever the other side's appraiser said.

⚠️ Do not miss the valuation hearing. Everything downstream — the floor, a buyout price, your share of the proceeds — is computed off the court's determination of value. In an heirs property case under 755 ILCS 75/6, you have 30 days from the notice of the appraisal to file a written objection stating your grounds, and the court holds the valuation hearing whether or not anybody objects.

🧮 How the Number Gets Set

Illinois has two tracks, and which one you are on decides how the value is found.

Ordinary partition. Section 17-105 simply directs the court to fix the value of the premises to be sold. Practically, that means appraisal testimony, sometimes competing appraisals, sometimes a commissioner's report. Section 17-106 lets the court appoint a disinterested commissioner to report in writing, under oath, whether the property can be divided without manifest prejudice and, if so, how — and authorizes a surveyor. The commissioner's and surveyor's fees, and the fees of the person conducting the sale, are taxed as costs in the case.

Heirs property. If the land qualifies as heirs property, the Uniform Partition of Heirs Property Act — 755 ILCS 75, in force since August 2019 — takes over, and valuation becomes a formal procedure. Section 6 requires the court to order an appraisal by a disinterested Illinois-licensed appraiser who values the property "assuming sole ownership of the fee simple estate." The appraisal is filed with the court; within 10 days the plaintiff must notify every party of the appraised value, where to review it, and the 30-day objection window. Then the court holds a hearing, considers any other evidence of value a party offers, and enters a determination of value before it reaches the merits of the partition. If all the cotenants agree on a value or a method, the court adopts theirs instead. And if the cost of an appraisal would outweigh what it proves, the court can set value after an evidentiary hearing without one.

🛡️ What Counts as Heirs Property in Illinois

This matters more than most Illinois families realize, because the protections are automatic when the definition fits. Section 2 of the Act defines heirs property as real property held in tenancy in common where, at the time the partition action is filed, all three of these are true: there is no recorded agreement binding all the cotenants that governs partition; at least one cotenant got title from a relative, living or dead; and one of these thresholds is met — 20% or more of the interests are held by relatives, or 20% or more are held by a cotenant who took title from a relative, or 20% or more of the cotenants are relatives.

Section 3 then does the work: in any Article XVII partition action, the court shall determine whether the property is heirs property, and if it is, the property must be partitioned under the Act unless all the cotenants agree otherwise in a record. The Act supplements Article XVII and displaces anything in Article XVII that conflicts with it.

One more protection with teeth: under Section 4, if the plaintiff asks for notice by publication and the court finds the property may be heirs property, the plaintiff must post a conspicuous sign on the property within 10 days and keep it there while the case is pending, naming the court and identifying the property. A cousin who still drives past the family house finds out, even if the mail never reached them.

🤝 The 45-Day Buyout Only Some Owners Get

Illinois's buyout right under 755 ILCS 75/7 is narrower than people assume, and the asymmetry is the point. Once value is determined, if any cotenant asked for partition by sale, the court orders notice that any cotenant except one who requested the sale may buy all the interests of the cotenants who did request it. You get 45 days from that notice to tell the court you elect to buy.

The price is not negotiated. It is the court's determination of value for the whole parcel multiplied by the selling cotenant's fractional share. One sibling with a one-third interest in a $300,000 house is bought out at $100,000, full stop.

From there the clock is strict. If more than one cotenant elects, the court allocates the right to buy in proportion to existing shares. Electing owners then have at least 60 days from the court's notice to pay their apportioned price to the clerk. Miss it and the case goes back to the partition alternatives in Section 8 as though no buyout happened; if some pay and others do not, the ones who paid get a further 20 days to pick up the rest. An electing buyer can also ask the court, within 45 days, to let them buy out defendants who were served and never appeared, at the same court-determined value.

The practical warning: if you want to keep the house, do not ask for a sale as a bargaining position. In Illinois, asking for the sale is what disqualifies you from the buyout.

🏠 A Broker Instead of the Courthouse Steps

When an heirs property case does end in a sale, Section 10 requires an open-market sale unless the court finds that sealed bids or an auction would be more economically advantageous for the cotenants as a group. If the parties agree on a licensed Illinois broker within 10 days of the order, the court appoints that broker and sets a reasonable commission; if they cannot agree, the court appoints a disinterested one. The broker must market the property in a commercially reasonable manner at a price no lower than the court's determination of value, and must report to the court within seven days of receiving a qualifying offer, listing the buyer, the price, the terms, any owner financing, the amounts going to lienholders and the commission arrangement (Section 11).

If no offer at or above the determined value arrives in a reasonable time, the court can approve the best outstanding offer, redetermine value and keep the property on the market, or order sealed bids or an auction after all. A cotenant who buys gets a credit against the price for their own share of the proceeds. That is a listed sale with a reserve, supervised by a judge — a different financial universe from a sheriff's sale, and the strongest argument for making sure the court knows the property is heirs property.

⚖️ Division, Owelty, and the Commissioner

Illinois still prefers division where division works. Under 17-105 the court first adjudicates everyone's title and interest, then decides at a further hearing whether the premises can be divided without manifest prejudice, and if they can, divides them "fairly and impartially" — with or without owelty, the cash payment that evens up unequal parcels.

In an heirs property case, Section 8 flips the default toward keeping the land: after any buyout, the court must order partition in kind unless partition in kind would cause manifest prejudice to the cotenants as a group, and it must approve a request by two or more parties to aggregate their interests into one parcel. Section 9 lists what the judge weighs: whether the land can practicably be divided, whether division would make the pieces worth materially less than the whole, how long the family has owned or possessed it, sentimental and ancestral attachment, the use a cotenant is making of it and the harm of losing that use, which cotenants have actually paid the taxes, insurance and upkeep, tax consequences, and anything else relevant — with no single factor allowed to decide the case.

Read factor six twice. In Illinois, the owner who has been paying the tax bill and fixing the furnace has a statutory argument, not just a grievance. Keep the receipts.

🧹 A Partition Case Can Also Clean the Title

Section 17-124 gives an Illinois court unusually broad powers inside a partition case: it may investigate and determine all questions of conflicting or controverted title, remove clouds on title, vest title by order without deeds from minors or unknown heirs, vest the purchaser with title on a sale, and apportion encumbrances among the parties who take the encumbered parcels.

That makes partition a title-cleanup tool for property no title company will insure — an old unreleased mortgage, a probate nobody opened, an heir who vanished in 1991. Section 17-114 handles the private liens: a mortgage or judgment against one part owner's share is bound by the partition judgment as to how the property is divided, but the lien stays in full force against the parcel assigned to that owner. Nobody's mortgage evaporates; it just follows them.

💰 Who Pays the Lawyers — the Rule That Rewards Showing Up

Illinois's cost rule, 735 ILCS 5/17-125, is unusual and worth reading closely. When the complaint properly sets out all the parties' rights and interests, the court apportions the costs among the parties in interest — including the customary cost of title evidence and "a reasonable fee for plaintiff's attorney" — so each party pays an equitable portion. In other words, the default is that everyone helps pay the lawyer who filed the case against them.

The exception is the whole game: "unless the defendants, or some of them, interpose a good and substantial defense to the complaint. In such case the party or parties making such substantial defense shall recover their costs against the plaintiff according to justice and equity." Put up a real defense — a genuine dispute about shares, a recorded agreement not to partition, a defect in the complaint's statement of interests — and the fee-shifting can run the other direction. Sulk and do nothing, and your share pays for the plaintiff's attorney.

The heirs property version, 755 ILCS 75/12, is the same rule with one addition: in deciding what is just and equitable, the court may consider "the good faith attempt of the parties to agree prior to the initiation of the complaint." A documented, reasonable buyout offer made before anyone filed is evidence under the statute.

🛑 You Can End It Any Time Before the Sale

Section 17-126 is the quiet off-ramp. If the parties adjust their rights among themselves after the judgment of partition but before the division or sale happens, the court enters an order terminating further proceedings; the judgment stands as the determination of everyone's interests, and there is no judicial division or sale. A settlement reached on the courthouse steps is not too late in Illinois, and the adjudication of shares you already paid for stays good.

There is also a tax reason to settle with deeds instead of dollars. Illinois exempts deeds of partition from the state and county real estate transfer tax under 35 ILCS 200/31-45(h), and the City of Chicago exempts them too — except that an owner who takes more than their undivided interest owes tax on the consideration paid for the excess. On a Chicago sale, the city's $3.75 per $500 buyer tax plus the $1.50 per $500 CTA tax on the seller, on top of the state's $0.50 and the county's $0.25 per $500, add real money that a properly structured partition deed can avoid. Ask an Illinois real estate attorney how your deal should be papered before you sign anything.

⌛ Cook County and Downstate: Time and Money

Partition is filed in the circuit court of the county where the premises or part of them sit (17-101); where the land spans counties, the first circuit court to acquire jurisdiction keeps exclusive jurisdiction. In Cook County these cases land in the Chancery Division downtown, and the docket is the main reason timelines differ across the state.

Realistic schedules: an uncontested downstate case where the owners agree on value and want a sale runs roughly 8 to 14 months. An heirs property case with a court-ordered appraisal, a 30-day objection window, a valuation hearing, a 45-day buyout election and a 60-day funding period runs 12 to 20 months before a broker is even appointed. A contested Cook County case with competing appraisals and a fight over shares runs 18 months to three years.

Legal fees in the Illinois market: $7,500 to $20,000 for an uncontested partition; $30,000 to $75,000 or more once appraisals and ownership are contested. Court-ordered appraisals typically run $500 to $1,500 for a single-family house, more for a multi-unit building. A broker's commission on the open-market sale is set by the court. All of it is taxed as costs under 17-125 or 75/12, which means it comes out of the owners' shares.

🏠 A Berwyn Two-Flat, Four Ways

Three siblings inherit a two-flat in Berwyn from their mother. Nobody opened a probate for two years. Repaired value is about $320,000; it needs roughly $45,000 of porch, tuckpointing and electrical work, so as-is value is near $250,000. One sibling lives on the first floor and pays the taxes. Two want out.

Carrying it is the pressure nobody plans for. Cook County taxes a two-flat at 10% of market value before the state equalization factor, and the homeowner exemption — a reduction in equalized assessed value, roughly $10,000 at this writing — plus any senior exemption apply only while an owner occupies the property. Suburban Cook composite rates commonly put a $250,000 two-flat in the $5,000 to $8,000 a year range, and losing exemptions pushes it up. Add a vacant or landlord policy at $2,000 to $3,500 a year if the occupant moves out, water and scavenger service, and a Chicago-winter heating bill you cannot turn off without risking frozen pipes.

🧭 Four Exits Before the Gavel

🤝 A buyout papered as a deed of partition

Cheapest and quietest

One appraisal, a refinance or family loan, and deeds. Under 35 ILCS 200/31-45(h) a deed of partition is exempt from the state and county transfer tax, and Chicago exempts it as well except on consideration paid for more than your undivided share. Get it drafted by an Illinois attorney.

📄 A recorded co-ownership agreement

Best prevention

A recorded agreement binding all the cotenants that governs partition takes the property outside the heirs property definition in 755 ILCS 75/2 and can bind the parties on buyout terms. It only helps if it exists before somebody files.

🏷️ List it with everyone's signature

Highest price

Illinois closings run through attorneys, which makes a three-owner listing workable once everyone agrees. This nets the most per share and requires unanimity plus money for repairs.

📋 Your First Week, in Order

  1. Read the complaint for one sentence: did anyone request partition by sale? That request is what creates — and forfeits — the Section 7 buyout right.
  2. Decide whether the property is heirs property under 755 ILCS 75/2, and say so in your appearance. The court must determine the question, but nobody will raise it for you.
  3. Get your own valuation evidence now — broker's opinion, repair bids, comparable sales. The court's number drives the two-thirds floor, the buyout price and your share.
  4. Pull together the receipts for taxes, insurance, roof, furnace and upkeep. They are a listed factor under Section 9 and they are your argument on contribution.
  5. Price a refinance if you want to keep the house. You will have 45 days to elect and about 60 more to fund; a lender cannot start that conversation on day 44.
  6. Put one written offer in front of the family before you answer. Under 755 ILCS 75/12 a good-faith attempt to agree before the complaint is something the judge can weigh when apportioning fees.

✅ Bottom Line

Illinois gives a co-owner facing a forced sale more protection than almost any neighboring state: a statutory two-thirds price floor under any court-approved sale, a formal appraisal and objection procedure for family land, a 45-day right to buy out the owner who wants out at full appraised value, and a listed sale through a licensed broker instead of a courthouse auction. Every one of those protections runs off the court's determination of value, and every one of them is waivable by inattention.

So treat the valuation hearing as the case. Show up with evidence, raise heirs property status if it fits, do not ask for a sale if you want to keep the house, and make one documented buyout offer before anyone files — because 735 ILCS 5/17-125 will otherwise hand part of the plaintiff's legal bill to your share. If the building needs more money than anyone has and the clock is already running, a cash sale of the whole property or of your own interest is worth comparing against two more years of Cook County taxes. ❓ Illinois Partition Questions We Hear Most

Can an Illinois court sell a co-owned house for less than it is worth?

There is a floor. Under 735 ILCS 5/17-105 the court must fix the value of the premises before a partition sale, and no sale may be approved for less than two-thirds of that valuation. If the property will not bring two-thirds of the fixed value, the court may hold a further hearing and either revalue the property and approve the sale or order a new sale. The floor is only as good as the valuation evidence you put in.

What counts as heirs property under Illinois law?

Under 755 ILCS 75/2, it is real property held in tenancy in common where there is no recorded agreement binding all cotenants that governs partition, at least one cotenant acquired title from a relative living or deceased, and either 20% or more of the interests are held by relatives, or 20% or more are held by a cotenant who acquired title from a relative, or 20% or more of the cotenants are relatives.

How long do I have to buy out the co-owner who filed in Illinois?

Under 755 ILCS 75/7 you have 45 days from the court's notice to elect to buy the interests of any cotenant who requested partition by sale, and then at least 60 days from the court's following notice to pay your apportioned price to the clerk. The price is the court's determination of value multiplied by that cotenant's fractional share. A cotenant who requested the sale cannot use the buyout right.

Does an Illinois partition sale have to be an auction?

Not for heirs property. Section 10 of the Uniform Partition of Heirs Property Act requires an open-market sale through a licensed Illinois broker at a price no lower than the court's determination of value, unless the court finds sealed bids or an auction would be more economically advantageous for the cotenants as a group. Ordinary partition sales under 735 ILCS 5/17-105 are public sales on the terms the court directs.

Who pays the plaintiff's attorney fees in an Illinois partition case?

By default, everyone. Section 17-125 directs the court to apportion the costs, including the usual cost of title evidence and a reasonable fee for the plaintiff's attorney, among the parties in interest so each pays an equitable share. The exception is where a defendant interposes a good and substantial defense; that party recovers costs against the plaintiff according to justice and equity.

What is a good and substantial defense in an Illinois partition case?

It is a real contest rather than a delay, and it is what reverses the fee rule in 735 ILCS 5/17-125. Typical examples are a genuine dispute about the size of the shares, a recorded agreement limiting partition, or a complaint that fails to set out the parties' rights and interests properly. Whether a defense qualifies is decided by the judge on the facts, so get Illinois counsel before relying on it.

Can an Illinois partition case clear up a bad title?

Yes, and that is often the real reason to file. Section 17-124 lets the court determine conflicting or controverted titles, remove clouds on title, vest title by order without deeds from minors or unknown heirs, vest a purchaser with title, and apportion encumbrances among the parties taking the encumbered parcels. Under 17-114, a lien against one owner's share stays in force against the parcel assigned to that owner.

Is a deed of partition subject to Illinois transfer tax?

Deeds of partition are exempt from the Illinois state and county real estate transfer tax under 35 ILCS 200/31-45(h), and the City of Chicago's ordinance exempts them as well, except that an owner who receives more than their undivided interest owes tax on the consideration paid for the excess. Have an Illinois real estate attorney paper the transaction, because how it is documented decides whether the exemption applies.

Can we settle an Illinois partition case after the judgment but before the sale?

Yes. Section 17-126 provides that if the parties adjust their rights among themselves after the judgment of partition and before the division or sale, the court enters an order terminating further proceedings. The judgment of partition stays in force to determine everyone's interests, and no judicial division or sale happens. A deal on the courthouse steps still works in Illinois.

Where is an Illinois partition case filed, and does Cook County differ?

Under 735 ILCS 5/17-101 you file a verified complaint in the circuit court of the county where the premises or part of them are located, and if the land is in two or more counties, the first circuit court to acquire jurisdiction keeps exclusive jurisdiction. In Cook County these cases are heard in the Chancery Division, and the docket there generally makes them slower than comparable downstate cases.
