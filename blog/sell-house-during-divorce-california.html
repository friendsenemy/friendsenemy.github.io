In California, the Divorce Summons Freezes the House. After That, the Fight Is Over the Credits.

The moment a California divorce starts, automatic orders bar both spouses from selling or transferring property without written consent or a court order. Getting past that is usually simple. Working out who gets what is harder, because a 50/50 split in California rarely means two equal checks.

Many California couples don't realize the house was frozen on the day of service. The notice is printed on the back of the summons, form FL-110, and most people skim right past it. It isn't a formality. It's a court order with teeth, and it's the first thing to deal with if either of you wants to sell.

🧊 The Summons Already Froze the House

Family Code section 2040 requires the summons to restrain both spouses from transferring, encumbering, concealing or in any way disposing of any property, real or personal, without the other party's written consent or a court order. The form says it covers community, quasi-community and separate property. So even a house that's plainly yours from before the marriage is covered. It binds the person who filed from filing, and the other spouse from service, and it lasts until the case is dismissed, judgment is entered, or the court says otherwise.

The exceptions are narrow: the usual course of business, the necessities of life, and reasonable attorney fees. Selling a house isn't one of them.

The fix is easy when you both agree. Sign a written stipulation that consents to the sale and says where the money goes until the case is resolved. Most lawyers have it filed as a court order, and a title officer will appreciate having one. If your spouse won't agree, your lawyer can file a request for order asking the judge to allow it.

⚠️ These orders aren't recorded, so a title search may miss them. That doesn't make it safe. A spouse with sole record title might get a sale through. But spouses owe each other fiduciary duties in California, and Family Code section 1101 lets a court award the other spouse 50% of an improperly transferred asset, or 100% where there's fraud, plus attorney fees. Selling behind your spouse's back can cost much more than the house was worth to you.

🖊️ Two Signatures, Whatever the Deed Says

Separately from the restraining order, Family Code section 1102 requires both spouses, personally or through an authorized agent, to join in selling or encumbering community real property. It applies even if the deed names only one of you. A buyer who dealt with a record owner in good faith gets a presumption of validity, and the other spouse has one year from recording to sue. No title insurer will rely on that if they know about the marriage. In practice, expect both of you to sign the listing, the contract and the deed. Our guide on what one co-owner can do alone covers other states, and California leaves far less room here than most.

📒 Why 50/50 Isn't What Reaches Your Bank Account

Family Code section 2550 says a court must divide the community estate equally. But before you split anything, these California-specific adjustments come off, and they can move tens of thousands of dollars.

  • Section 2640 reimbursement. If one spouse put separate money toward the down payment, improvements or loan principal, that spouse generally gets it back first, if they can trace it. There's no interest and no share of appreciation, and it's capped by the property's net value. Inheritance and premarital savings are the classic sources. Interest, taxes, insurance and upkeep don't count. A signed written waiver defeats it.
  • Epstein credits. Named for Marriage of Epstein, these reimburse a spouse who paid community debts, like the mortgage, from post-separation earnings. Those earnings are separate property, so the other spouse may owe half. The court has discretion here and may deny them if the payment was really support or a gift.
  • Watts charges. Named for Marriage of Watts, these work the other way. A spouse who stays in the house alone after separation may owe the community the fair rental value of it. You generally need a real estate agent or appraiser to prove that value.

The two often offset each other, which surprises the spouse who's kept the mortgage current and expected a credit. Everything hinges on the date of separation under Family Code section 70: the date one spouse told the other the marriage was over and acted consistently with that. Spouses often disagree about that date. If you do, sort it out before you close, or the proceeds could sit for months while you argue.

🔎 Before You Divide It, Is It All Community Property?

A house bought during the marriage is presumed community property under section 760. Three facts can change that.

  • Bought before the marriage. It stays separate, but if community money paid down the loan, the community earns a share of the value. California calls this the Moore/Marsden calculation, and it's usually a job for a forensic accountant, since it can be a large number on a house that's appreciated.
  • A spouse's name was taken off for a refinance. Lenders sometimes ask for an interspousal transfer deed so one spouse can qualify. Under section 852, a change in the character of property needs an express written declaration, so the wording matters. And a deed that favors one spouse can face a presumption of undue influence. Don't assume that signature settled ownership either way.
  • Bought in joint names with separate money. Section 2581 presumes a house titled jointly during marriage is community property, and section 2640 reimbursement becomes the remedy. That's why tracing the down payment matters so much.

🧭 Three Ways Out for the House

🔑 One spouse buys the other out

Often best in California, if the refinance works

Here's where California differs from most states. A transfer between spouses under a settlement or divorce decree is excluded from Prop 13 reassessment under Revenue and Taxation Code section 63, and exempt from documentary transfer tax under section 11927. So a spouse who keeps the house also keeps a low assessed value that may be years old. On a house bought in 2012, that can save thousands every year compared to buying a similar home today. The trade-off: at today's rates, refinancing to pay the other spouse may replace a 3% loan with a much pricier one. Run the monthly number before you fight for the house.

🏷️ List it and hold the money

Usually best when nobody can afford it alone

List it with a stipulation in place, close, pay the mortgage, and have the net held in a trust account or joint blocked account until the division is settled. Both of you stop bleeding money while the lawyers finish the accounting. It's also the cleanest way to end joint liability on the loan, which a divorce decree alone won't do.

🏫 Deferred sale for the children

Worth asking about with kids in school

Under Family Code sections 3800 to 3810, a court can defer the sale so a custodial parent and the children can stay, an arrangement known as a Duke order. The judge looks at feasibility, including whether the parent can pay the costs, and it can be changed later. The other parent's equity stays tied up in the meantime.

🏚️ Sell as-is for cash

Rarely the top choice in California

It's worth pricing when the house needs major work, a foreclosure is close, or one spouse keeps stalling on showings and repairs. On high California values, though, the discount is a big number, so see the example below before choosing this. If you've fallen behind, read how to sell before a foreclosure.

🗓️ The Six-Month Wait Doesn't Bar a Sale

Section 2339 says a judgment can't end the marriage until six months after service or the respondent's appearance, whichever comes first. People often think that means they can't sell for six months. It doesn't. With consent or a court order, a house can close in month two. Often it should, because selling earlier stops the bills, and with both of you on title it can also simplify taxes.

On that subject: federal law lets each owner exclude up to $250,000 of gain, or $500,000 on a joint return, if the two-of-five-year tests are met. A spouse who moved out can still count the other's use if the home's occupied under a divorce or separation instrument, so get that written into the agreement. Transfers between spouses incident to divorce generally aren't taxed under Internal Revenue Code section 1041. California also has its own 3.33% seller withholding, which a qualifying main-home sale is usually exempt from, using Form 593. On a long-held coastal house, gains above the exclusion are real, so bring in a tax preparer.

🧑‍⚖️ When One Spouse Won't Sign

A judge can order a sale, and if the spouse still refuses, the court can appoint an elisor under Code of Civil Procedure section 128(a)(4). That's usually the clerk or a designee, who signs the deed in the spouse's place. A second order may be needed, and every step adds fees. Expect the refuser to bear the cost if the judge sees stalling. If the judgment leaves you as co-owners with no sale term, you may end up with a partition suit, which is a poor way to finish a divorce. So write the sale deadline, price rules and tie-breaker into the settlement. See our national guide to divorce sales for the general picture.

💵 A Sacramento Example, Credits Included

A Sacramento house would bring $650,000 fixed up. The mortgage is $310,000 and the assessed value is $420,000. She put in $60,000 of inherited money toward the down payment. He moved out a year ago. She has lived there and paid the $2,400 mortgage for 12 months, and rent would be about $3,200.

  • Monthly carrying cost: mortgage $2,400, property tax about $400 at roughly 1.15% of assessed value, insurance $200, utilities $250, upkeep $200. That's about $3,450. If the house sits vacant, insurance gets harder to find, and some California owners already rely on the FAIR Plan.
  • Listing: $630,000 after $10,000 of prep, less about 7% in commission, transfer tax and closing costs, nets about $265,900 after the mortgage. She gets $60,000 back under section 2640, leaving $205,900 to split: $102,950 each. Her Epstein credit is $14,400, half of what she paid. His Watts credit is $19,200, half of the rent. So she owes him a net $4,800. Result: about $158,150 to her and $107,750 to him.
  • Cash sale: cash buyers pay roughly 60-80% of repaired value. At 72%, that's $468,000, or $158,000 after the mortgage, with the buyer covering closing costs. After the same adjustments: about $104,200 to her and $53,800 to him.

The cash route costs this couple about $107,900 combined. It'd take a lot of stalling, a failing foundation, or a foreclosure notice to make that worthwhile. If the house can pass an inspection and you can both sign, list it. A cash sale is for when you really can't.

📋 What To Do This Week

  1. Read the back of the summons (FL-110), and don't move, borrow against or list anything yet.
  2. Write down your date of separation and what proves it.
  3. Pull the deed and gather down payment records, even if they're old.
  4. Keep a log of every mortgage, tax and insurance payment since separation.
  5. Get a broker's opinion of value and fair rental value, ideally from someone you both accept.
  6. Ask your lawyer to draft a stipulation covering the sale and where the proceeds will be held.
  7. Call the lender about refinancing before anyone commits to a buyout.

✅ Bottom Line

In California you can't quietly sell during a divorce, and you shouldn't want to. Get written consent or a court order, sign together, and hold the money until the accounting's done. Then spend your energy on what actually moves dollars: the date of separation, the down payment trail, and the Epstein and Watts math. On most sound California houses, a listing beats a cash sale by a wide margin. The buyout deserves a real look too, since a low Prop 13 base is an asset you can't get back once it's gone.

❓ Frequently Asked Questions

Can I sell my house during a divorce in California?

Yes, with your spouse's written consent or a court order. The automatic restraining orders on the summons bar selling or transferring property without one of those, and both spouses generally must sign the deed for community real property.

What are ATROs in a California divorce?

Automatic Temporary Restraining Orders are standard orders printed on the summons, form FL-110, under Family Code section 2040. They bar both spouses from disposing of property, among other things, from filing or service until the case ends or a court changes them.

Do the restraining orders apply to my separate property house?

Yes. The form covers community, quasi-community and separate property. You still need written consent or a court order, even for a house that's clearly yours alone.

Do I get my down payment back in a California divorce?

Often yes, if separate money went in and you can trace it. Family Code section 2640 reimburses the amount, with no interest or share of appreciation, unless you signed a written waiver. It's capped by the property's net value.

What are Epstein credits and Watts charges?

An Epstein credit reimburses a spouse who paid community debts, like the mortgage, with post-separation earnings. A Watts charge makes a spouse who kept exclusive use of the house account for its fair rental value. Courts often offset one against the other.

Can my spouse sell the house if only their name is on the deed?

Not properly if it's community property. Section 1102 requires both spouses to join, and section 2040 restrains the sale. A spouse who goes ahead risks penalties under section 1101, and you'd have one year from recording to challenge it under section 1102.

Does a buyout in a divorce trigger Prop 13 reassessment?

Generally no. Revenue and Taxation Code section 63 excludes transfers between spouses under a settlement or decree from a change in ownership, so the keeping spouse usually keeps the existing assessed value. A sale to a third party resets it for the buyer.

Do we have to wait six months to sell?

No. The six months in section 2339 only limits when the marriage itself can end. With consent or a court order, a house can be sold at any point in the case.

What if my spouse refuses to sign the deed after a court orders the sale?

The court can appoint an elisor, usually the clerk or a designee, to sign in their place under Code of Civil Procedure section 128(a)(4). You'd need a further order, and the refusing spouse may be ordered to pay the fees.

Should we sell to a cash buyer or list in a California divorce?

Listing usually wins on a sound house, often by a large amount given California values. A cash sale is worth pricing if the house needs serious work, a foreclosure is close, or one spouse keeps blocking showings and repairs.
