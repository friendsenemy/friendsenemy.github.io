In Miami, Your Lender May Not Be the Only One Who Can Foreclose

Florida's mortgage foreclosures run through the courts and take a while. But a Miami condo owner can owe two creditors at once, and the association has its own right to foreclose, sometimes over far less money. Here's how both clocks run in Miami-Dade, when your right to redeem really ends, and the math on whether any sale still works, including when a cash offer can't.

By Dustin Ray, Founder of Pages of Purpose LLC. Not legal advice — Florida foreclosure and condominium law change, and laws differ by state. Before you sign or skip anything, talk to a Florida-licensed attorney or a HUD-approved housing counselor.

You can be current on your mortgage in Miami and still lose your home to foreclosure.

That sentence surprises people who've only heard about banks. In a condo building, the association holds a lien for unpaid assessments, and Florida law lets it foreclose that lien much the way a lender forecloses a mortgage. In Miami-Dade, where a huge share of homeowners live in condominiums and where older buildings are working through expensive inspection and reserve requirements, that's a live problem. A $40,000 special assessment can arrive at a building where the monthly maintenance was already a stretch.

The foreclosure load here isn't trivial, either. The Miami-Dade Clerk reports 3,392 foreclosure filings in 2025 and 2,913 through September 2026, a pace that's on track to run ahead of last year. So let's start where most guides don't, with the second creditor, and then get to the bank.

🏢 The Second Foreclosure: What Your Condo Association Can Do

Under Fla. Stat. § 718.116, a unit owner is personally liable for assessments that come due while they own the unit, and the association has a lien on the unit for what's unpaid. The rules give the association real leverage:

  • 📈 Interest. Unpaid assessments accrue interest at the rate in the declaration, or 18% a year if the declaration doesn't set one.
  • 🏷️ Late fees. Where the governing documents allow, the association may add the greater of $25 or 5% of each late installment.
  • 🧑‍💼 Attorney's fees. The association can recover reasonable fees and costs of collection, and your payments apply first to interest, late fees and collection costs, then to the assessment itself. Partial payments can feel like they're going nowhere, because they often are.
  • 🚪 Foreclosure. The 2025 statute requires at least 45 days' written notice of intent to foreclose. After that, the association can file suit to foreclose, or sue for a money judgment without giving up the lien.

Note what isn't in that list: no requirement that you be months behind on a large amount, and no federal 120-day waiting rule like the one that applies to mortgage servicers. A claim of lien stays good for a year after recording, so the association has to act, but many act well before that. Florida's strong homestead protection generally doesn't save you here either, since the lien traces back to the declaration you took title under. A lawyer can tell you how that plays out for your building.

The lender's foreclosure doesn't fix this. When a first mortgagee takes a unit by foreclosure, § 718.116 limits what the lender owes the association to the lesser of 12 months of unpaid regular assessments or 1% of the original mortgage debt. That protects the lender, not you. Assessments that came due while you owned the unit can stay your personal debt.

A single-family house in an HOA community has a parallel system under chapter 720, with its own notices. Outside any association, the mortgage is usually your only foreclosure worry, plus county tax collection.

🧾 Why Special Assessments Change Who Can Buy Your Unit

This is the part that decides whether you can sell at all. After the Surfside collapse, Florida required milestone structural inspections for older condo buildings, along with structural reserve studies and funded reserves. Many Miami associations responded with big special assessments and much higher monthly dues.

That hits a seller two ways. First, any unpaid amount has to be cleared at closing. The closing agent orders an estoppel certificate from the association, and the buyer becomes jointly liable with you for past-due assessments if they aren't paid, so no careful buyer or title company will ignore them. Second, a retail buyer's lender may refuse to finance a unit in a building with thin reserves, unfinished structural work or pending litigation. If your building has one of those problems, your pool of buyers can shrink to people paying cash, even if your unit itself is lovely.

So before you think about price, ask your manager for three things: the current ledger on your unit, what's been approved but not yet billed, and the latest milestone inspection and reserve status. Those three answers matter more to your sale than your view.

⚖️ How a Miami-Dade Mortgage Foreclosure Runs

If it's the bank, you've got more time. Florida forecloses through the courts, so your lender must sue in the Eleventh Judicial Circuit in Miami-Dade. A servicer generally can't file until the loan is more than 120 days past due (12 C.F.R. § 1024.41).

  1. Complaint and lis pendens. You're served, and a notice of the lawsuit is recorded against the property. Respond within the court's deadline, usually 20 days. Silence gets you a default.
  2. Judgment. Uncontested cases can reach final judgment in months. Contested ones can take far longer, and the docket has its own pace. A year or so from filing to sale is a fair rough guess, not a promise.
  3. Sale date. Under § 45.031, the clerk generally sets the sale not less than 20 or more than 35 days after judgment, unless the plaintiff agrees to a later date.
  4. Online auction. Miami-Dade runs its sales online through the clerk's official foreclosure site, not on the courthouse steps. The high bidder posts a 5% deposit.
  5. Certificate of sale. The clerk files it after the sale.
  6. Certificate of title. If nobody objects within 10 days after the certificate of sale is filed, the clerk issues title and the sale is confirmed.

That process gives you time to sell. It doesn't give you endless time, because interest, attorney's fees and association balances keep growing, and you don't get those back.

🚪 When Your Right to Redeem Actually Ends

Florida's rule is short. Under § 45.0315, you can redeem by paying the judgment amount plus allowed costs until the later of the filing of the certificate of sale or the time set in the final judgment. After that, the statute says, "there is no right of redemption."

Compare that to neighbors you may hear about. There's no 10-day period to pay after the sale, as there is in North Carolina, and nothing like a year-long post-sale redemption. In Miami-Dade, the clerk's foreclosure unit can give you a redemption figure, and the money must be cash or a cashier's check payable to the clerk. The certificate of sale can be filed quite soon after the auction, so treat auction day as your real deadline.

The sale of your home to a buyer works the same way. A closing must happen, and the lender must be paid, before the auction. If a contract is signed and closing is close, your lawyer can move to cancel or reset the sale. Courts often require a good reason, and the plaintiff's agreement helps a great deal. Don't rely on getting one on the last day.

Don't miss that last cell. A successful association foreclosure doesn't erase your first mortgage. The buyer takes the unit subject to it, you lose the home, and your name may remain on a loan that's still due. Ask an attorney about this before you let an association case go to judgment.

🧮 Does Any Sale Still Work? The Equity Test

Here's the honest math on a two-bedroom condo in Kendall. Say it would bring $340,000 fully updated in a healthy building, and $300,000 as it stands. The mortgage payoff is $205,000. The association is owed $34,000, counting the unpaid special assessment, interest and fees. Total to clear: $239,000. Monthly holding cost: $2,100.

  • 🪧 Listing as-is, four months. $300,000, less 6% commission ($18,000), doc stamps at $0.60 per $100 ($1,800), title and closing costs ($2,000) and four months of holding ($8,400). After clearing $239,000, about $31,000.
  • 💵 Cash at 70% of repaired value. $238,000 won't even clear the debts. After stamps and closing costs, you'd be about $4,000 short.
  • 💵 Cash at the top of the range, 80%. $272,000, less about $3,100 in costs. After clearing the debts, about $30,000. That's what a cash buyer would need to pay, and few could on this unit.

So in a sound building, the listing wins, and we'd tell you to list. Cash buyers typically pay 60-80% of repaired value, and that range leaves little room once a large association balance sits on top of a mortgage. Cash only becomes the answer when retail financing is off the table because of the building's condition, or the sale date arrives first. If you're underwater at any price, you may need a short sale, a negotiated settlement with the association, or legal advice about bankruptcy.

The rate on the stamps depends on the property. Miami-Dade's deed stamps are $0.60 per $100 on a single-family residence, and other property types carry an added $0.45 per $100 surtax. Ask your closing agent which applies to your unit.

🧭 Your Options, Honestly

🤝 Work out a payment plan

Best early, if the debt is small

Associations often prefer money to litigation, especially before attorney's fees mount. Ask for any plan in writing, and make sure it pauses collection. For the bank, ask about reinstatement or a modification, with a free HUD-approved counselor's help.

🪧 List it

Best when the building can be financed

If a lender will approve the building and you've got a few months, the open market will almost always beat a cash offer. Tell your agent everything about the assessment up front. Buyers find out anyway, and it's better priced in than discovered at the estoppel stage.

💵 Sell for cash

Best when only cash can buy it

This fits a building a lender won't touch, a house with roof or storm damage that can't be insured affordably, or an auction date too close for a financed closing. Run the numbers first. Sometimes this option doesn't clear your debts.

⚖️ Bankruptcy or a contested defense

Worth a consult before auction

Chapter 13 generally stops a sale and can spread out arrears, including association arrears. A foreclosure defense lawyer may also spot a standing or notice problem. Neither is free, and neither fixes a payment you can't sustain.

💰 After the Sale: Deficiency and Surplus

Deficiency. Under § 702.06, a deficiency judgment is within the court's discretion. For owner-occupied residential property, it can't exceed the difference between the judgment amount and the home's fair market value on the sale date. Homestead status creates a presumption you're an owner-occupant. On a one-to-four-unit dwelling, the lender has one year to bring a deficiency action under § 95.11, starting the day after the clerk issues the certificate. That cap and that short deadline give Florida owners better protection than many states do, but they don't eliminate the risk, particularly on investment units.

Surplus. If the auction brings more than the judgment, § 45.032 presumes the record owner gets the surplus after timely lienholder claims. Unclaimed surplus is reported to the state one year after the sale. Miami-Dade's clerk tells owners to check for surplus within 10 days of the certificate of sale. You don't need a lawyer to claim it.

Surplus trackers. Expect letters. Section 45.033 caps what a transferee can take at 12% of the surplus and requires disclosures, and the final judgment itself warns you that you don't have to assign your rights to anyone. Florida's § 501.1377 also bars foreclosure-rescue consultants from collecting money before they've completed everything they promised. Anyone asking for payment up front to "stop" your foreclosure, or a deed now with a promise to rent it back, is a reason to call an attorney.

📋 What To Do This Week

  1. Identify the plaintiff. Read the letter. Lender, servicer's law firm or association counsel? Search the Miami-Dade Clerk's site for a case and any lis pendens.
  2. Answer on time. If you've been served, the deadline is usually 20 days. Don't let it pass while you shop for options.
  3. Get three numbers. Mortgage payoff, reinstatement figure and the association's ledger with all pending assessments.
  4. Ask about the building. Get milestone status and reserve information from the manager or board.
  5. Ask an agent one question. Can a buyer get a loan in this building today? The answer tells you whether listing is a real option.
  6. See a counselor or lawyer. A HUD-approved counselor is free or low cost. A Florida foreclosure attorney is worth the consult once suit is filed.
  7. Do the equity test. Use the example above, with your numbers.

✅ Bottom Line

A Miami owner has to watch two creditors, not one. The bank's court case is slow and leaves room to list and sell. The association's lien can move sooner, on smaller money, with fees and interest eating every partial payment. Find out what you owe both, whether a lender will finance your building, and when your redemption right ends, which is about when the auction ends. If a lender will finance the building and there's time, list it. If only cash can buy it, make sure the number clears your debts. If it doesn't, get a lawyer's advice before you sign anything.

❓ Frequently Asked Questions

Can my Miami condo association foreclose if my mortgage is current?

Yes. Under Fla. Stat. § 718.116, the association has a lien for unpaid assessments and can foreclose it after giving the required written notice, which is 45 days under the 2025 statute. Being current with your lender doesn't stop that.

How much interest can a Florida condo association charge on late assessments?

Whatever the declaration sets, within the legal limit, or 18% a year if it sets no rate. It can also charge late fees of up to the greater of $25 or 5% of each late installment, if the documents allow, plus attorney's fees.

Does an association foreclosure wipe out my mortgage?

No. An association foreclosure sale is generally subject to a first mortgage recorded ahead of the association's lien claim. You could lose the unit and still be liable on the loan, so talk to a lawyer before the case reaches judgment.

Can I sell my Miami condo with an unpaid special assessment?

Yes, but it has to be dealt with. The closing agent gets an estoppel certificate from the association, and past-due amounts are normally paid from your proceeds. A new owner is jointly liable if they aren't, so buyers won't skip it.

How long does foreclosure take in Miami-Dade County?

It varies a lot. Florida requires a lawsuit, so a mortgage case commonly takes around a year or more from filing to sale if contested, and less if not. After judgment, the sale is usually set 20 to 35 days out.

Can I get my house back after a Miami-Dade foreclosure sale?

Usually not. Under § 45.0315, you can redeem until the later of the filing of the certificate of sale or the time set in the judgment. After that, there is no right of redemption in Florida.

Where are Miami-Dade foreclosure auctions held?

Online, through the clerk's official foreclosure sales site. Anyone can register to bid. The high bidder must post a 5% deposit, and the sale is confirmed by a certificate of title if no objection is filed within 10 days.

Will I owe money after a foreclosure in Florida?

Possibly. Under § 702.06, a court may enter a deficiency judgment, but for an owner-occupied home it can't exceed the judgment minus fair market value at the sale date. The lender generally has one year to sue on a one-to-four-unit home.

How do I claim surplus funds after a Miami-Dade foreclosure sale?

Call the clerk's foreclosure unit within 10 days of the certificate of sale to see if there is any surplus. You don't need a lawyer, and a transferee can't legally take more than 12% of it under § 45.033.

Is a cash offer always better when I'm facing foreclosure in Miami?

No. If a lender will finance your building and there's time, listing usually nets far more. Cash works when retail buyers can't get a loan or the sale is close, and it sometimes won't clear both your mortgage and your association balance.
