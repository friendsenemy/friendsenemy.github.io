Selling a Co-Owned House in Louisiana: Ownership in Indivision, Usufructs, and Why Nobody "Survives" Into the Whole House

Louisiana is the one state where a co-owned house runs on the Civil Code instead of the common law. There is no joint tenancy, a dead co-owner's share goes through a succession, and a surviving spouse can end up holding a usufruct while the children hold naked ownership. Here is what that means if you want out — and when listing beats every other option.

⚜️ Louisiana Doesn't Do Joint Tenancy: You Own "In Indivision"

Every other state's co-ownership rules grew out of English common law: joint tenancy with a right of survivorship, tenancy in common, and in many states tenancy by the entirety for married couples. Louisiana's grew out of the French and Spanish civil codes. Under Civil Code article 480, when two or more people own the same thing, they own it "in indivision," each holding an undivided share. Article 797 presumes those shares are equal unless the law or a written act says otherwise.

The practical consequence that surprises people most: Louisiana has no joint tenancy with right of survivorship for real estate. Writing "joint tenants with right of survivorship" on a Louisiana deed does not make the surviving owner the sole owner when the other one dies. The deceased co-owner's share passes through his or her succession — Louisiana's name for probate — to heirs or legatees. Louisiana estate-planning attorneys say this plainly, and it is the first thing to understand, because it means co-ownership in Louisiana tends to multiply rather than shrink. Two siblings become one sibling plus three nieces and nephews. Twenty years later there may be a dozen names in the chain of title.

⚠️ The most important warning on this page: if you co-own a Louisiana house with a parent, partner or sibling and you assumed you would "get it all" when they die, you probably won't. Their share goes through a succession unless they planned otherwise — and until a judgment of possession is signed, nobody can give clean title to that share.

If you came here from another state's rules, set aside the vocabulary of "joint tenants" and "severing." In Louisiana the questions are different: Who owns which share? Is any share split between a usufructuary and naked owners? Is any share community property that needs a spouse's concurrence? Those three questions decide what you can sell and who has to sign.

✍️ What You Can Do Alone, and What Takes Every Signature

Civil Code article 805 draws the line in one sentence. A co-owner may freely lease, alienate or encumber his own share. But the consent of all the co-owners is required to lease, alienate or encumber the entire thing. Put plainly:

  • ✅ You can sell your undivided half, third or tenth to anyone — a sibling, an investor, us — without asking the other owners.
  • ✅ You can mortgage your own share (though few lenders will lend on a fraction of a house).
  • ❌ You cannot list the whole house, lease the whole house, or sign a sale of the whole house unless every co-owner signs too.
  • ❌ You cannot make "substantial alterations or substantial improvements" without everyone's consent (article 804). A gut renovation one owner pays for is not something the others agreed to.

Article 800 gives one important exception: any co-owner may take the steps necessary to preserve the property without the others' consent. Tarping a roof after a hurricane, paying the insurance premium, keeping the lights on so the pipes don't freeze in a January cold snap — those are preservation. Article 806 then lets the co-owner who paid recover from the others, in proportion to their shares, for necessary expenses, ordinary maintenance and repairs, and management expenses paid to third persons. That reimbursement is reduced by the value of the paying owner's own enjoyment of the property, so the sibling who lived there rent-free for three years should not expect a full refund of the taxes he paid.

Use is governed by articles 801 through 803. Each co-owner may use the house "according to its destination" but cannot keep the others from using it. If the owners can't agree on use or management and partition isn't available, a court can step in. In real life that almost never happens on its own; disputes over who lives in the house usually end up as partition suits.

👵 When a Parent Holds a Usufruct and the Children Hold Naked Ownership

This is the Louisiana situation we see most, and nothing like it exists in the other 49 states. Under Civil Code article 890, when a married person dies leaving descendants, the surviving spouse receives a usufruct over the deceased spouse's share of the community property, to the extent the deceased didn't leave it some other way by testament. The children inherit the naked ownership. The usufruct lasts until the surviving spouse dies or remarries.

Take a typical Jefferson Parish house bought during the marriage. Mom dies without a will. Dad owns his own half outright as his share of the community. Mom's half goes to the three children in naked ownership, subject to Dad's usufruct. Dad can live there, rent it out and keep the rent; the children own the house's "bones" but can't use it or collect income from it until the usufruct ends.

Now suppose one child wants out. Article 543 controls: a person who holds full ownership may demand partition, but a naked owner alone cannot, and neither can a usufructuary alone. They can demand partition only jointly, because together their interests add up to full ownership. So a child holding naked ownership cannot sue to force a sale of the house while Dad's usufruct is running unless Dad joins in. What that child can do is sell the naked-ownership share itself under article 805. Buyers for naked ownership are few, and they price in the years until the usufruct ends, but it is a real exit.

Often the cleanest path is the family agreeing together: the surviving spouse and the children sign one sale of the whole house, and the usufruct shifts to the proceeds or is settled in the act of sale. That takes a Louisiana attorney or notary who knows how to draft it, and it is the single most common reason a Louisiana co-owned sale takes longer than people expect.

💍 Community Property: The Spouse Who Isn't on the Deed

Louisiana is a community-property state. Under article 2336 each spouse owns a present undivided half of the community, and the community can't be judicially partitioned while the regime lasts. Article 2347 adds the rule that trips up co-owner sales: both spouses must concur to alienate, encumber or lease a community immovable.

For a co-owned house, that matters in two ways. First, if your co-owner bought his share while married and it is community property, his spouse may need to concur in a sale of that share even though her name isn't on the deed. Second, if you are married and your own share is community, the same rule applies to you. A title company or closing notary will ask; better to answer the question before you have a buyer than at the closing table. After a divorce or death ends the community, article 2369.8 lets either former spouse demand partition of former community property at any time, and any agreement to the contrary is absolutely null.

📜 The Paperwork Louisiana Requires

Louisiana closings look different, too. A sale of an immovable must be made by authentic act or by act under private signature (article 1839), and it affects third persons only once it is filed in the conveyance records of the parish where the property sits. An authentic act is signed before a notary and two witnesses (article 1833). Louisiana notaries have far broader powers than notaries elsewhere; many real estate closings are handled by notaries who are also attorneys.

If a co-owner has died, the heirs usually need a judgment of possession from the district court recognizing them and sending them into possession (Code of Civil Procedure article 3061) before a title insurer will insure their deed. For smaller estates, a small-succession affidavit can do the job: Code of Civil Procedure article 3421 sets the ceiling at $200,000 in gross value for a Louisiana domiciliary, and there is no value limit if the death was 20 or more years ago. But under article 3431 the affidavit can't be used for a decedent who left a will and owned Louisiana immovables — those estates need a court proceeding. In New Orleans that is the Civil District Court for the Parish of Orleans, whose clerk also keeps the conveyance and mortgage records and the Notarial Archives.

One more Louisiana-only wrinkle: forced heirship. Under articles 1493 through 1495, a child who is 23 or younger, or who is permanently unable to care for himself, is a forced heir entitled to a reserved portion — one-fourth of the estate with one forced heir, one-half with two or more. A will that left the house to a single sibling may be challenged by a young or disabled child, which can change who the co-owners actually are.

🧾 What a Louisiana Co-Owned House Costs While You Argue

Louisiana property taxes are low by national standards — commonly cited effective rates run around 0.55% statewide and about 0.88% in Orleans Parish. Homes are assessed at 10% of fair market value, and the homestead exemption covers the first $7,500 of assessed value, which is $75,000 of market value (Louisiana Constitution article VII, section 20). For a house owned in indivision, the exemption applies only to the share of the co-owner who actually lives there. When the last occupying owner moves out, the exemption goes away and the bill rises.

Insurance is where Louisiana hurts. Homeowners premiums here are among the highest in the country, and a vacant house usually needs a separate vacant-dwelling policy, which costs more still. Here is a realistic monthly picture for a vacant $240,000 house in New Orleans:

  • 🏛️ Property tax at roughly 0.88% with no homestead exemption: about $176 a month
  • 🛡️ Vacant-dwelling insurance, often $250–$450 a month in south Louisiana, more in flood zones
  • 💡 Utilities kept on to run a dehumidifier and protect against mold: $80–$150
  • 🌿 Lawn, gutters, and small repairs in a climate that grows things on everything: $100–$200

That is roughly $600 to $1,000 a month, $7,000 to $12,000 a year, before anyone pays a lawyer. A contested partition can take well over a year; one Louisiana succession firm estimates 6–18 months for an uncontested case and two years or more when it is fought, with $500–$2,000 in filing, publication and sheriff's fees on top of attorney fees. Attorneys in contested co-ownership cases commonly bill by the hour, and five-figure totals are not unusual.

💵 A Gentilly House With Four Owners, Worked Through

Here is a realistic example. A 1950s raised house in Gentilly would be worth about $260,000 fully repaired. It needs a roof, an electrical update and some flood-related repairs — call it $45,000. Title is in four siblings in equal quarters after their parents' successions.

  • 💰 Listing as-is: requires all four signatures. If the house sells as-is for around $205,000 after costs, each sibling nets roughly $51,000 — the best number, if everyone signs and a buyer's inspection doesn't scare off financing.
  • 💵 Cash sale of the whole: cash buyers typically pay about 60–80% of repaired value depending on condition. At 70% of $260,000, that is about $182,000, or $45,500 per sibling, with no repairs, no showings and a closing in a few weeks.
  • 🧩 One sibling sells only her quarter: she can do this alone under article 805. A buyer of a quarter interest is buying a lawsuit, so expect well below one-quarter of the whole-house price. It is the fastest certain exit for someone who needs money now and can't get the others to move.
  • ⚖️ Partition: the court orders a private sale, giving the existing co-owners first priority to buy. Net after a year of carrying costs (about $9,000 total) and attorney fees can fall below the cash figure.

If the four siblings agree, list it. A cash offer from us makes sense here only if one of them can't wait, the repairs scare buyers off, or someone has stopped answering.

📋 What To Do This Week

  1. 🔎 Pull the last deed and any judgment of possession from the parish clerk of court's conveyance records. List every owner and each share.
  2. 👵 Check whether any share is held in usufruct and naked ownership — common when a parent died leaving a surviving spouse.
  3. 💍 Note which owners were married when they acquired their shares; their spouses may need to concur.
  4. 🧾 Gather receipts for taxes, insurance and repairs you paid. They support an article 806 reimbursement claim.
  5. 🛡️ Confirm the house is insured. If it is vacant, ask the agent about a vacant-dwelling policy now, before a storm.
  6. ✉️ Send the other owners one short written proposal: list it, buy each other out, or sell to a cash buyer.
  7. ⚖️ If no one answers within a few weeks, talk to a Louisiana attorney about partition and ask us for a written offer to compare.

✅ Bottom Line

Louisiana co-ownership is ownership in indivision, not joint tenancy. You can sell your own share without anyone's permission, but selling the whole house takes every co-owner, every concurring spouse and, where there is one, the usufructuary. Shares don't merge when an owner dies; they fragment through successions. The longer a family waits, the more names it takes to sell.

If everyone will sign and the house is sound, list it. If they won't, or the house needs work nobody wants to pay for, or you simply want out of your share, ask us. We'll tell you which situation you're in before we tell you what we'd pay.

❓ Louisiana Co-Ownership Questions We Hear Most

Can a co-owner sell a house in Louisiana without the other owners?

A co-owner can sell his or her own undivided share without anyone's consent under Civil Code article 805. Selling, leasing or mortgaging the whole house requires the consent of every co-owner.

Does Louisiana recognize joint tenancy with right of survivorship?

No. Louisiana does not recognize joint tenancy with right of survivorship for real estate. When a co-owner dies, that person's share passes through a succession to heirs or legatees rather than automatically to the surviving co-owner.

What does owning a house in indivision mean?

It is Louisiana's term for co-ownership. Under Civil Code article 480, two or more people own the same property, each with an undivided share. Article 797 presumes the shares are equal unless the law or a written act says otherwise.

Can my sibling and I force a sale if our father has a usufruct over the house?

Not on your own. Under Civil Code article 543, a naked owner alone cannot demand partition, and neither can a usufructuary alone; they can only demand it jointly. You can still sell your naked-ownership share, or the family can agree to sell the whole house together.

Does my co-owner's spouse have to sign in Louisiana?

If the co-owner's share is community property, Civil Code article 2347 requires both spouses to concur in alienating, encumbering or leasing a community immovable, even if only one spouse is on the deed.

Can I get reimbursed for taxes and repairs I paid on a co-owned Louisiana house?

Generally yes. Civil Code article 806 lets a co-owner recover necessary expenses, ordinary maintenance and repairs, and management expenses paid to third persons, in proportion to the other owners' shares, reduced by the value of the paying owner's own use of the property.

Can co-owners agree never to partition a Louisiana house?

Only for a limited time. Civil Code article 807 allows an agreement excluding partition for up to 15 years, and Revised Statute 9:1702 requires it to be in writing. Otherwise any co-owner can demand partition.

Do the heirs need a judgment of possession before selling an inherited share?

Usually yes. A judgment of possession recognizes the heirs and sends them into possession, and title insurers generally want one. A small-succession affidavit can work for intestate estates up to $200,000 in gross value, but not for a decedent who left a will and owned Louisiana immovables.

Does the Louisiana homestead exemption still apply when a house is co-owned?

It applies only to the pro rata share of the co-owner or co-owners who actually live in the house. If no owner lives there, the exemption on the first $7,500 of assessed value is lost.

Should I sell my Louisiana share to a cash buyer or list the whole house?

If every owner will sign and the house is in good shape, listing usually nets more. A cash sale of the whole house or of your own share makes more sense when the house needs major repairs, a co-owner won't cooperate, or you need to be done quickly.
