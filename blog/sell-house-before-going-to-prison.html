Going to Prison Soon? You Have Until Your Reporting Date to Make the House Decision Cheap

Everything about a house gets slower and more expensive once you're inside. Right now you can sit in any notary's office, meet with a lawyer in private and walk through the place with a buyer. Here's how to use the weeks you have left, based on how long you'll be gone, and which well-meant moves could make your case worse.

By Dustin Ray, Founder of Pages of Purpose LLC. Not legal advice — laws differ by state, and anything you do with assets while a criminal case is open should go past your defense lawyer first. Talk to an attorney licensed where the property is before you sign.

🤝 Need a buyer, not just answers? Pages of Purpose specializes in buying houses when an owner or co-owner is in jail or prison. See how we handle these sales.

This guide is for the person who hasn't gone in yet: out on bond, sentenced with a self-surrender date, or looking at a plea and doing the math. Most advice online starts after the door has closed, and our own guide to selling from inside mostly does too. The position you're in now is much better, even though it may not feel that way.

⏳ Your Reporting Date Is the Real Deadline

A notary costs a few dollars today. Inside, the same signature might take six weeks of scheduling, a mobile notary's travel bill, and a family member driving to the facility. A mistake on a form can be fixed in an afternoon today. Inside, it's another round of mail. So the goal isn't necessarily to sell. It's to make the decision and sign the paperwork while everything's still easy, so the house doesn't run on autopilot for years.

Work backward from the date. Those three numbers tell you what's possible.

  • Retail listing: plan on 60 to 90 days from listing to closing, plus any time to clean up the house. With fewer than ten weeks left, assume you'll sign a POA and someone else will finish it.
  • Cash sale: usually one to three weeks once title is clear. Liens or a bond on the house add time.
  • POA only: a lawyer can usually turn one around in days. This is the one item that should get done even if you decide nothing else.

On the other hand, a house with nobody in it, a mortgage nobody can pay and a sentence measured in years rarely gets better with time. Letting a bank foreclose is the most expensive way to end it.

🚫 The Moves That Can Blow Up on You

⚠️ Don't deed the house to a relative for $10 without your lawyer's sign-off. It's the most common idea families have and the riskiest. It can look like hiding assets from the court, victims or creditors, and it can be undone.

Here's why that matters.

  • Voidable transfers. State laws modeled on the Uniform Voidable Transactions Act let creditors undo transfers meant to dodge them, usually looking back around four years. A gift to family while a case is pending, when you keep living there or get little in return, has most of the warning signs courts look for.
  • Restitution and fines. In federal cases, a lien under 18 U.S.C. section 3613 attaches to all of a defendant's property once judgment is entered. Your presentence report will likely ask what you own and what you've recently moved. Our federal prison guide explains how that lien gets paid.
  • Forfeiture. If the house is tied to the alleged offense, 21 U.S.C. section 853 says the government's interest vests when the act was committed. A later transfer doesn't clear it unless the buyer paid value and had no reason to know, and a court can restrain the property even earlier.
  • Bond and release conditions. If the house secures your bond, you can't sell it without releasing that bond. Some release orders also restrict moving assets. Violating one can land you in custody earlier.
  • Your mortgage. A transfer can trigger a due-on-sale clause in a loan. And the person you deed it to takes on your tax basis, which can mean a bigger tax bill later.

A sale at fair market value to a stranger is a different thing. The cash stays traceable and nothing's hidden. Even so, tell your defense lawyer first. Done openly, a sale can help: it can pay off the mortgage, cover legal fees and put money toward restitution, and judges generally respond better to payment than to excuses.

🖊️ Sign This Before You Go, Even If You Keep the House

A power of attorney signed in a lawyer's office may be the best $300 to $800 you spend this year. Ask for these things.

  • Durable and effective now. A "springing" POA that takes effect only on some later event forces your agent to prove the event happened, and title companies dislike them.
  • Express real-estate powers with the property's legal description, so it can be recorded with the deed.
  • Your state's formalities. Some states require witnesses and exact wording. See how strict Florida and Pennsylvania are, and use a lawyer licensed where the house is.
  • A backup agent. People move, get sick and burn out, and replacing an agent from prison is the hard kind of paperwork you're trying to avoid.
  • A second notarized original held by your lawyer, since recorders and banks often want an original.

Choose your agent like you'd choose someone to hold your wallet for years, because that's what this is. If you can't name one person you'd trust, that's a real reason to sell now. If a co-owner or heir is involved, read what a co-owner can do alone. If you're married, our guide to selling when a spouse is locked up shows who has to sign, so get both signatures done while it's easy.

🧾 The Three-Year Tax Clock Starts When You Leave

Federal tax law lets you exclude up to $250,000 of gain on a main home ($500,000 on a joint return) if you owned and lived in it for at least two of the five years before the sale. Once you stop living there, that means you generally have up to three years to sell and keep the exclusion. Time in prison doesn't count as time living in the home. So a person with a big gain and a six-year sentence who waits could turn a tax-free sale into a taxable one.

An example: bought at $110,000, sold at $290,000, $180,000 gain. Sold within three years, likely no federal tax. Sold in year five, you might owe 15% federally, or $27,000, plus any state tax. Rough math, so have a tax preparer run yours, especially if you've rented it out.

🔑 Can You Actually Be a Landlord From a Cell?

Not really. Someone else has to do it, and they'll have your POA. A property manager runs about 8-10% of rent. Tell your insurer the house is now a rental, because a homeowner's policy may not cover a tenant-occupied place, and if it stands empty, many policies reduce or drop coverage after about 60 days. And the mortgage stays yours. If the tenant stops paying, your agent has to file the eviction and your credit takes the hit.

💵 The Math on a Real Decision

Suppose you report in ten weeks and expect about 30 months. Your house would be worth $280,000 fixed up, you owe $140,000, and it's in fair shape. Here's how each path looks.

Rent it out

Best if you have a real manager

Rent $1,900, less manager $190, mortgage $1,300, tax $230 at a 1% rate, landlord insurance $130 and a $150 repair reserve. That's about $100 a month short before any vacancy. You'd come home to a house with more equity, assuming nothing big breaks and your agent stays on top of it. You'd be back inside the tax window too.

List it now

Best price if the agent can finish it

Paint and cleanup of $5,000, list at $270,000, about 8% in selling costs. You'd net about $103,400 after the mortgage. It may not close before you report, so the POA finishes it.

Sell for cash

Best if you have under a month or a messy house

Cash buyers tend to pay about 60-80% of repaired value. At 72% it's $201,600, about $61,600 after the mortgage, closed before you report. That's roughly $41,800 less than listing. It's a fair trade only if time or the condition of the house really forces it.

If that same house sat empty for 30 months instead, at about $1,900 a month with a vacant-home policy, you'd spend about $57,000 holding it, more than the cash discount. That's why doing nothing is the one option that doesn't belong on this list.

📋 What To Do This Week

  1. Ask your defense lawyer one direct question: "Is there any problem with me selling or transferring this house?"
  2. Check whether the house secures any bond or is named in a charging document.
  3. Pull your mortgage balance, and order a title search to see if anything's attached.
  4. Pick an agent and a backup, and have the conversation in person.
  5. Get the POA drafted and signed. Don't wait until you've made the larger decision.
  6. Get one agent's opinion of value and one written cash offer, so you're comparing actual numbers.
  7. Decide who gets the mail, who pays the insurance and who has keys.

✅ Bottom Line

You have an advantage most people in this position only wish they'd used: you can still sign things yourself. Clear any move with your defense lawyer, get a durable POA signed this week, and let the length of the sentence guide the rest. Short term and family in the house, keep it. Long term and an empty house, sell it while the choice is still yours. And don't hand the deed to a cousin to "keep it safe," since that's the option most likely to cost you twice.

❓ Frequently Asked Questions

Should I sell my house before I go to prison?

It depends mostly on how long you'll be gone and who'd live there. Under a year with family in the home, keeping it usually makes sense. Several years with an empty house and a mortgage nobody can pay, selling usually protects more of your equity.

What happens to my house if I go to jail and don't do anything?

You still own it, but the bills keep coming. A missed mortgage leads to foreclosure, and unpaid property tax leads to a tax sale. A house left empty can lose insurance coverage after around 60 days. Doing nothing is usually the most expensive choice.

Can I sign my house over to a family member before sentencing?

Physically yes, but it can be undone as a voidable transfer, can look like hiding assets, may trigger a due-on-sale clause and passes along your tax basis. Clear it with your defense lawyer first.

Can I sell my house while out on bond?

Often yes, unless the house secures your bond, your release order restricts it, or the property is restrained or named in a forfeiture. Check all three with your lawyer before signing a contract.

Will selling my house before sentencing hurt my case?

A fair-price sale to an unrelated buyer, done openly and through your lawyer, usually doesn't. It may help if the money goes to debts or restitution. Hidden proceeds or a sale to a relative for little money are what cause problems.

Can the court take my house for restitution?

It can happen. Federal restitution and fines create a lien on the defendant's property, and state courts have their own collection tools. The result depends on the state, your equity, and exemptions like homestead. Ask your lawyer what's likely.

What kind of power of attorney do I need before going to prison?

A durable one that takes effect immediately, with express powers over real estate and the legal description of the house, signed in the form the property's state requires. Name a backup agent and keep a second original with your lawyer.

Do I lose the capital gains exclusion if I'm in prison for years?

You can. The exclusion generally needs two years of living in the home within the five years before the sale, and prison time isn't time living there. After about three years away, you may lose it, so ask a tax preparer before waiting.

How fast can I sell my house before I report?

A retail sale usually takes 60 to 90 days. A cash sale can close in one to three weeks if title is clear. If you run out of time, a POA lets your agent finish the closing after you've reported.

Is it better to rent out my house while I'm in prison?

It can be, if the rent covers the costs and you have a dependable manager and agent. It works best for sentences of a few years or less. You stay liable for the mortgage, and a bad tenant is hard to handle from inside.
