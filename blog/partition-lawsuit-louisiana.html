Partition Lawsuits in Louisiana: Since 2020 the Existing Co-Owners Get the First Chance to Buy, Before Any Auction

For most of Louisiana's history, a co-owned house that couldn't be divided went to a sheriff's auction. Laws passed in 2020 and 2021 changed that: when a co-owner hasn't consented, the court orders a private sale, the current co-owners of record get first priority, and the price can't be below the appraisal. Here is how a Louisiana partition actually runs now.

By Dustin Ray, Founder of Pages of Purpose LLC. Not legal advice — laws differ by state, and Louisiana partition procedure was rewritten recently and is still being amended. Before you file or answer a partition suit, talk to an attorney licensed in Louisiana, where the property is.

🔑 The 2020 Reform: A Private Sale, and Co-Owners Buy First

Louisiana's Civil Code has always said that no one can be forced to stay in indivision (article 807) and that the action to partition never prescribes (article 817). What changed in 2020 and 2021 is how an indivisible property gets sold. Acts 2020 No. 281 and Acts 2021 No. 27 rewrote Civil Code article 811 and the partition articles of the Code of Civil Procedure. Today article 811(B) says that when a co-owner is an absentee or has not consented to the sale, the court shall order a private sale — and the first priority goes to a sale among the existing co-owners of record, ahead of a licitation (auction) or a sale to third persons.

Code of Civil Procedure article 4607 supplies the guardrail: a private sale without the consent of every co-owner must be for no less than the appraised value, and the court appoints a representative — who may be one of the co-owners — to sign the act of sale for anyone who won't or can't. Article 4614, added by Acts 2022 No. 636, lets a co-owner who buys at a partition sale take a credit for his own share of the price, so a co-owner with half the house only has to come up with the other half in cash.

⚠️ What this means for you: in a Louisiana partition, the person most likely to end up owning the house is a co-owner who can raise the money to buy the others out at appraisal. If you want the house, start lining up financing the day the suit is filed. If you want out, the realistic question is not "what will an auction bring?" but "will my co-owner buy at appraisal, and is the appraisal right?"

That is a sharp break from the old pattern, where Louisiana co-owners lost properties at sheriff's sales that one law firm estimates bring about 70–85% of market value. Licitation still exists — it is the fallback when no private sale happens — but it is no longer the default when someone refuses to cooperate.

🔢 The Small-Share Buyout: Revised Statute 9:1113

Louisiana has a second protection that most states lack. Revised Statute 9:1113 applies when the co-owners demanding partition together own 15% or less of the property — or 20% or less if a common ascendant (a grandparent, say) once owned the whole thing. In that case, the remaining co-owners may buy out the petitioners' shares at a price fixed by a court-appointed appraiser.

  • ⏱️ The other co-owners have 30 days to elect to buy, or 90 days in the common-ascendant case.
  • 🔁 If some co-owners decline, their right is offered to the others, who get 10 more days.
  • 🧭 The statute is aimed at the classic heirs'-property problem: an investor or distant relative buys a sliver of a family property and then sues to force a sale of the whole.

Louisiana has not adopted the Uniform Partition of Heirs Property Act that many other states have adopted. A 2025 bill to enact it (HB 175) stalled in the House Civil Law and Procedure Committee. So the protections here are Louisiana's own: the co-owner priority in article 811(B), the appraisal floor in article 4607, and the minority-interest buyout in R.S. 9:1113.

🧱 When a Louisiana Court Must Divide in Kind

Article 810 sets a test that is stricter than many states': partition in kind is mandatory when the property can be divided into lots of nearly equal value and the lots together are not worth significantly less than the whole. Code of Civil Procedure article 4606 restates it: partition in kind unless the property is indivisible or can't conveniently be divided.

For a single house on a city lot, that test almost always fails — you can't cut a shotgun double in half and get two lots worth as much as the whole. But for rural acreage in Acadiana or timberland in north Louisiana, physical division is a real outcome, and a co-owner who wants a sale has to show why the land can't be split. Special rules apply when a co-owner is an absentee: article 4621 allows a licitation or private sale even of divisible property, but article 4630 says partition will be in kind if the absentee appears through counsel and asks for it.

⚖️ How a Louisiana Partition Case Moves

Partition is judicial when a party is an unrepresented absentee, a minor or an incompetent, or when the co-owners simply can't agree (Code of Civil Procedure article 4602). It is an ordinary proceeding filed in the district court of the parish where the immovable is located (articles 4603 and 80). A few features are specific to Louisiana:

The court appoints a notary

Under article 4605 the case is tried with preference over other ordinary proceedings, and the court appoints a notary to carry out the partition. The notary prepares a procès-verbal of the partition; parties can oppose it, and the court homologates (approves) it — as soon as 10 days after service of the rule if nobody objects (articles 4608–4612). Louisiana notaries are civil-law officials with real authority, which is why this role exists nowhere else.

Absentee co-owners get an appointed attorney

Heirs'-property cases often include a cousin nobody has heard from in a decade. When a co-owner is an absentee, the court appoints an attorney to represent him and orders publication (article 4623); the absentee has 15 days from publication to answer (article 4624). The appointed attorney's fee comes out of the absentee's share (article 4625). Taxes and preservation costs paid by the other owners are reimbursed from the proceeds (article 4626), and the absentee's share of the money is deposited in the court registry (article 4628).

The private-sale petition must disclose everything

A petition for a private sale under article 4622 must lay out the terms, identify the buyer, say whether the buyer is a co-owner or a third person, disclose the source of the funds, any entity relationships, and related-party costs. The buyer has to pay within 24 hours of the judgment, in cash or certified funds. That disclosure requirement is aimed squarely at insiders and investors who once used partition to pick up family property cheaply.

You can still settle at any point

Article 4607 expressly preserves the parties' right to agree on a nonjudicial partition at any time before the sale. Most Louisiana partition suits end this way: the filing gets everyone's attention, an appraisal comes in, and one side buys the other out or they agree to list.

🏦 Mortgages, Liens and Usufructs in a Partition

Partition doesn't wipe out what's recorded against the property. Real rights survive partition (article 812). In a partition in kind, a mortgage or lien on one co-owner's share follows the piece allotted to him (article 813). In a licitation, it attaches to his share of the proceeds instead (article 815). So if your brother's share carries a judgment lien, that lien comes out of his money, not yours.

Usufructs are trickier. Under article 543, a naked owner alone can't demand partition against the property, and neither can a usufructuary alone; they must act together. A family where a surviving parent holds a usufruct over a deceased parent's share has a co-ownership problem that partition may not solve until the usufruct ends.

⌛ Time and Money in a Louisiana Partition

One Louisiana succession law firm estimates that an uncontested partition takes 6–18 months and a contested one two years or more, with $500–$2,000 in filing, publication and sheriff's fees on top of attorney fees. Article 4613 lets the plaintiff's attorney in an uncontested case recover a reasonable fee taxed as costs — meaning it is paid from the common fund. In a fought case, each side generally pays its own lawyer, and hourly bills add up fast.

Meanwhile the house costs money. A vacant house in Caddo Parish worth $180,000, at a commonly cited statewide effective tax rate around 0.55% with no homestead exemption because no owner lives there, runs about $83 a month in property tax. Add vacant-dwelling insurance (in Louisiana often $200–$400 a month), utilities ($75–$125) and upkeep ($100–$200), and you are carrying roughly $450 to $800 a month — $8,000 to $15,000 over an 18-month case. Someone has to advance that money; article 806 lets them recover it from the others in proportion to their shares, reduced by the value of their own use.

💵 A Lafayette Parish House, Three Owners, Worked Through

Three cousins inherited a brick ranch in Lafayette Parish. Repaired, it would sell for $210,000; it needs about $35,000 in work. Cousin A owns half and lives in Houston; Cousins B and C own a quarter each, and C lives in the house.

  • 🏡 C buys at appraisal: the court appraiser values the house as-is at $170,000. With C's quarter credited under article 4614, C needs $127,500 to pay A ($85,000) and B ($42,500). If C can borrow, this is the likely outcome under article 811(B).
  • 🏷️ Agreed listing: if all three sign, an as-is listing might net $160,000 after costs: A $80,000, B and C $40,000 each. Fastest money for everyone, if they can agree.
  • 💵 Cash offer on the whole: cash buyers usually pay about 60–80% of repaired value. At 70% of $210,000, that is $147,000 — less than listing, but no repairs, no showings, and no one has to advance 18 months of carrying costs.
  • 🧩 A sells only her half: she can do it without permission under article 805. A buyer of half a house in a pending partition will discount heavily, but A is out in weeks.

🗂️ Your Moves in a Louisiana Partition

🏡 Buy the others out yourself

Strongest position in Louisiana

Article 811(B) puts existing co-owners first. If you want the house and can finance the other shares at appraisal, this is the outcome the law now favors.

📐 Challenge the appraisal

Protects every outcome

The appraisal sets the floor for any private sale. Bring comparables, repair bids and flood-zone information to the appraiser or the court before it becomes the number everyone is stuck with.

🤝 Settle before the sale

Least expensive

Article 4607 lets the parties agree on a nonjudicial partition at any time before the sale. An agreed listing almost always nets more than any court-run sale.

💵 Sell your share for cash

Fastest certain exit

We buy undivided shares in Louisiana, including shares in pending cases. Take this route only if a buyout or agreed listing is out of reach.

🚫 Don't File If a Conversation Would Do

A partition suit is the right tool when a co-owner is missing, unreasonable or holding the family hostage. It is the wrong tool when the other owners would sign a listing agreement if someone just organized it. Before filing, send a written proposal with an appraisal attached. If the house is sound and everyone will sign, list it with an agent and skip both the lawsuit and us. ✅ Bottom Line

Louisiana partition is no longer a straight road to the courthouse steps. When a co-owner won't consent, the court orders a private sale at no less than appraisal, and the existing co-owners get the first chance to buy. Small-share petitioners can be bought out at an appraised price under R.S. 9:1113. Physical division is mandatory when it really works. The court-appointed notary, the appointed attorney for absentees and the 24-hour payment rule are all Louisiana's own.

❓ Ten Questions About Louisiana Partition

What changed in Louisiana partition law in 2020?

Acts 2020 No. 281 and Acts 2021 No. 27 rewrote Civil Code article 811 and the partition articles of the Code of Civil Procedure. When a co-owner is an absentee or hasn't consented, the court now orders a private sale, with first priority to a sale among the existing co-owners of record, before any licitation or sale to third persons.

Can a Louisiana partition sale be below the appraised value?

A private sale made without the consent of all co-owners must be for no less than the appraised value under Code of Civil Procedure article 4607. A licitation, which is a public auction, can bring less.

Does Louisiana follow the Uniform Partition of Heirs Property Act?

No. Louisiana has not enacted it; a 2025 bill to adopt it stalled in committee. Louisiana relies instead on the co-owner priority in Civil Code article 811(B), the appraisal floor in article 4607, and the minority-interest buyout in Revised Statute 9:1113.

What is the Louisiana minority-interest buyout rule?

Under Revised Statute 9:1113, when the co-owners asking for partition together own 15% or less of the property, or 20% or less if a common ascendant once owned it all, the other co-owners may buy their shares at a price set by a court-appointed appraiser, generally electing within 30 days or 90 days in the common-ascendant case.

Can a co-owner who buys at the partition sale get credit for their own share?

Yes. Code of Civil Procedure article 4614 lets a co-owner who buys at the partition sale take credit for his or her own share of the price, so they only pay cash for the other owners' shares.

Will a Louisiana court divide the property instead of selling it?

Partition in kind is mandatory under Civil Code article 810 when the property can be divided into lots of nearly equal value that together are not worth significantly less than the whole. A single house rarely meets that test; rural land sometimes does.

What happens if one co-owner can't be found?

The court appoints an attorney to represent the absentee and orders publication. The absentee has 15 days from publication to answer, the attorney's fee is paid from the absentee's share, and the absentee's share of the proceeds is deposited in the court registry.

How long does a partition take in Louisiana?

One Louisiana succession firm estimates 6 to 18 months for an uncontested partition and two years or more for a contested one. Partition cases are tried with preference over other ordinary proceedings, but appraisal, publication and sale steps all take time.

Does a mortgage on one co-owner's share follow the property after partition?

In a licitation, a mortgage or lien on one co-owner's share attaches to that owner's share of the sale proceeds under Civil Code article 815. In a partition in kind, it follows the part allotted to that owner under article 813.

Can co-owners stop a Louisiana partition by agreeing on something else?

Yes. Code of Civil Procedure article 4607 preserves the parties' right to agree on a nonjudicial partition at any time before the sale, such as a buyout or an agreed listing, and most cases end that way.
