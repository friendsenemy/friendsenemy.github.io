Selling Before a Phoenix Trustee's Sale: Arizona's Deficiency Shield and the Equity It Doesn't Protect

In most states, the fear in foreclosure is owing the bank money after you lose the house. In Arizona, for most owner-occupied homes, the law takes that fear off the table. What it leaves on the table is your equity, and a 91-day non-judicial clock that moves faster than most Phoenix homeowners expect. Here is how to tell which situation you are in, and what to do about it.

By Dustin Ray, Founder of Pages of Purpose LLC. Not legal advice. Arizona foreclosure and anti-deficiency law has exceptions that turn on how your loan was used, and laws differ by state. Before you sign anything, talk to an attorney licensed in Arizona, where the property is.

A Notice of Trustee's Sale taped to the front door of a Phoenix house usually lands like a verdict. It names a date, a trustee, and a courthouse, and it reads as if nothing can be done. That is not true, but the window really is short, and the decision you make inside it depends on one Arizona rule most homeowners have never heard of.

This guide starts with that rule because it changes the whole conversation. Then it walks through the clock, the options, the real carrying costs of a Phoenix house in 2026, and the honest math on whether you should list the house, sell it for cash, or in some cases simply let the sale happen.

🛡️ Arizona's Anti-Deficiency Shield Changes the Question

When a house sells at a foreclosure auction for less than the loan balance, the gap is called a deficiency. In many states the lender can sue the former owner for that gap and chase wages and bank accounts for years. Arizona is different. Under A.R.S. § 33-814(G), if a lender forecloses through a trustee's sale on property of two and one-half acres or less that is limited to and used as a single one-family or two-family dwelling, the lender cannot bring an action to recover the shortfall. A separate statute, A.R.S. § 33-729(A), gives similar protection on purchase-money loans, meaning the money you borrowed to buy the house.

For a typical Phoenix homeowner with a first mortgage used to buy a single-family house on a normal city lot, that means a trustee's sale ends the debt. You lose the house. You do not get a bill afterward for the difference.

That protection has edges, and they matter:

  • 🏘️ Size and use. The shield covers lots of 2.5 acres or less used as a one- or two-family dwelling. A fourplex, a commercial building, or a large horse property in the far West Valley may fall outside it.
  • 💳 Second loans and HELOCs. A home-equity line or second mortgage that was not used to buy the house is the classic gap. If the first lender forecloses and wipes out the second lender's lien, the second lender may still be able to sue you on the note. Whether it can depends on how the money was used and what the loan documents say.
  • 🏗️ Houses never finished or lived in. The legislature narrowed the shield in 2014 for certain trust deeds on dwellings that were never completed or occupied. If you were building, ask an attorney before relying on it.
  • ⚖️ Judicial foreclosure. A lender can choose to foreclose a deed of trust through a lawsuit instead of a trustee's sale. That is rare on Phoenix houses, but it is a different path with different rules.

Even where a lender does have a deficiency claim after a trustee's sale, § 33-814(A) gives it only ninety days after the sale to file, and the court measures the deficiency against the higher of the auction price or the property's fair market value, not the lowball auction bid alone.

⚠️ The most important thing on this page: the anti-deficiency shield protects you from owing money. It does nothing to protect your equity. If your house is worth $380,000 and you owe $230,000, a trustee's sale can turn $150,000 of your money into a much smaller surplus check, months later, after a court process. The shield is good news for underwater owners. For owners with equity, the trustee's sale itself is the danger.

So the question in Phoenix is not "how do I avoid owing the bank?" It is "how much is my equity, and how do I get it out before the sale date?" Everything below follows from that.

🧮 The Equity Test Decides Which Path Is Yours

Before you call anyone, write down three numbers: what the house would sell for today in its current condition, the payoff on every loan against it (first, second, HELOC, solar lien, HOA balance), and how many days are left until the sale date on the notice. ⏱️ How the 91-Day Arizona Clock Actually Runs

Arizona deeds of trust are foreclosed outside of court, through a trustee. There is no judge, no lawsuit for you to answer, and no hearing where you can ask for more time. The steps are set by statute, and they are fast.

📬 Before the notice: the 120-day federal rule

For most home loans, federal servicing rules (12 C.F.R. § 1024.41) bar the servicer from making the first foreclosure notice or filing until you are more than 120 days behind. Those same rules also say that if you submit a complete loss-mitigation application more than 37 days before a scheduled sale, the servicer generally cannot go forward with the sale until it has evaluated you. That 37-day line is worth circling on your calendar.

📄 Day 0: the Notice of Trustee's Sale is recorded

The trustee records the notice with the Maricopa County Recorder. Under A.R.S. § 33-808, the sale date can be no sooner than the ninety-first day after recording. The trustee must mail copies to you within five business days of recording under A.R.S. § 33-809, post a copy on the property at least twenty days before the sale, and publish it in a newspaper of general circulation once a week for four consecutive weeks.

💸 Any time before the last business day: reinstatement

Under A.R.S. § 33-813, you can stop the sale by reinstating: paying what is actually past due (not the whole accelerated balance), plus the trustee's costs, and a trustee's fee equal to the greater of $600 or one-half of one percent of the unpaid principal. The deadline is 5:00 p.m. Mountain Standard Time on the last business day before the sale. Phoenix doesn't observe daylight saving time, so Mountain Standard Time is simply local time. A reinstatement quote can easily run $10,000 to $25,000 once several months of payments, late fees, and costs are added, so ask for it in writing early.

🔨 Day 91 or later: the sale

The sale is a public auction held between 9:00 a.m. and 5:00 p.m. on a business day, at the property, at the trustee's office, or at a superior court building in the county. Bidders may be required to put down a deposit, and the winning bidder pays the balance by the close of the next business day. Sales are frequently postponed, sometimes more than once, but you cannot count on a postponement.

🚪 After the sale: no redemption

Once the trustee's deed is delivered, it is over. Arizona gives no right to buy the house back after a trustee's sale. If you are still living there, the new owner can file a forcible detainer (eviction) case in a Maricopa County justice court, and those move quickly.

Ninety-one days sounds like a lot. In practice, many owners don't open the notice for a few weeks, spend another few weeks hoping the servicer calls back, and start thinking about selling with 40 days left. A normal Phoenix listing, with prep, photos, a few weeks on the market, and a 30-day financed escrow, needs most of the full window.

🧭 Your Real Options in Maricopa County, Ranked Honestly

🏦 Reinstate or modify

Best if you want to keep the house

If the default came from a one-time hit (a job gap, a medical bill, a divorce) and your income has recovered, reinstating or getting a modification keeps the house and your equity. Get the reinstatement figure in writing, send a complete loss-mitigation application before the 37-day mark, and keep copies of everything you send. A free HUD-approved housing counselor can help you build the application.

🏡 List with an agent

Best when there is equity and time

If the house is in decent shape and you have 75 or more days before the sale date, listing will almost always put more money in your pocket than any cash buyer, including us. Tell the agent about the sale date on day one. A good listing agent will price to sell fast, push for a short escrow, and coordinate with the trustee if a postponement is needed to close. If you are in this group, list it.

💵 Sell for cash

Best when time is short or the house needs work

A cash buyer makes sense when the clock is under about 60 days, when the house needs a roof, a new AC system, foundation work, or a full rehab that a financed buyer's lender won't accept, or when you've already moved out and can't keep the house show-ready. You trade some price for certainty: no appraisal, no repairs, and a close in as little as 10 to 21 days, timed ahead of the sale date.

📉 Short sale or deed in lieu

Best when you're underwater with an exposed second loan

If you owe more than the house is worth, a short sale lets the lender accept less than the balance. Its main value in Arizona is the chance to negotiate a written release of any second loan or HELOC that might otherwise survive. Short sales are slow; start immediately and ask the servicer to postpone the trustee's sale while it reviews the file.

🧾 Let the sale happen

Sometimes rational, never by default

For an underwater owner with only a qualifying first mortgage, letting the trustee's sale go forward may cost less than any alternative. You will take a credit hit and lose the house, but the shield means no deficiency on that loan. Make that choice deliberately, after confirming with an attorney that no second loan, HOA balance, or other debt survives, not by default because nobody returned your calls.

💵 What a Cash Offer on a Phoenix House Actually Looks Like

Cash buyers, including us, generally pay about 60% to 80% of what the house would be worth fully repaired (its after-repair value), minus the repairs. Where in that range an offer lands depends on condition, location, and how much risk the buyer is taking on. Here's a worked example on a common Phoenix house.

The house: a 1,450 sq ft 1978 block ranch in west Phoenix. Repaired and updated, comparable sales put it at about $375,000. It needs a new AC unit, a roof underlayment, and interior work, roughly $45,000. The owner owes $205,000 on a first mortgage, is $14,000 behind, and the sale date is 48 days away.

  • 📊 Cash offer range: 60% to 80% of $375,000 is $225,000 to $300,000. With $45,000 of known repairs, a fair offer on this house lands around $255,000 to $275,000.
  • 🏦 Payoff at closing: about $205,000 principal plus $14,000 arrears, plus trustee's fees and costs, say $222,000 in total.
  • 💰 Owner walks away with: roughly $33,000 to $53,000, in about three weeks, before the sale date.

Now compare listing. As-is, the house might bring $330,000 from a financed buyer, if one's lender will accept the old AC. After a roughly 5% to 6% commission and seller concessions, the owner might net around $85,000 after the payoff. That's meaningfully more, but only if the house sells and closes in 48 days. If it doesn't, the trustee's sale happens, a bidder may buy it for close to the opening bid, and the owner waits for a surplus check that will be smaller than either number above. With 48 days, a cash sale is the safer path. With 120 days, listing is clearly better, and we'd tell you so.

🔥 What It Costs to Hold a Phoenix House While You Decide

Every month you wait has a price, and in Phoenix some of those costs are seasonal.

  • 🏛️ Property tax: Phoenix has one of the lower effective property-tax rates of any large U.S. city, roughly 0.46% of value by recent estimates. On a $375,000 house that's about $145 a month. Low, but it keeps adding to what is owed if it's escrowed into a loan that isn't being paid.
  • 🛡️ Insurance: If you've moved out, a standard homeowner's policy may not cover a vacant house. A vacant-dwelling policy typically runs $100 to $250 a month.
  • ❄️ Electricity: A vacant house still needs the AC set to around 85 degrees from June through September to protect cabinets, flooring, and anything with glue in it. Expect $150 to $350 a month in summer, far less in winter.
  • 🏊 Pool and yard: A neglected pool turns green within weeks in the summer heat and invites code complaints. Basic pool service runs about $100 to $175 a month.
  • 🏘️ HOA dues: Many Phoenix-area subdivisions have them, and unpaid dues and fines are paid from your sale proceeds.
  • 📈 Interest and fees: On a $205,000 loan, about $1,100 a month of interest keeps accruing, plus late fees and the trustee's costs that grow with each step.

Add those together and a vacant Phoenix house in summer can cost $1,600 to $2,000 a month in carrying costs and accrued debt, all of it coming out of your equity.

💰 If the Sale Happens Anyway: Surplus Funds and the People Who Chase Them

If a bidder at the trustee's sale pays more than the debt and costs, the extra money doesn't vanish. Under A.R.S. § 33-812, the trustee pays the expenses of the sale and the secured debt, then any junior liens in priority, and the remaining balance goes to the former owner. When there's a question about who is entitled to it, the trustee can deposit the surplus with the county treasurer, and claimants apply to the superior court for its release. The Maricopa County Superior Court publishes forms for this application.

Two warnings. First, the surplus is almost always far less than what the house would have sold for on the open market, because auction bidders buy at a discount, sight unseen, and with no inspection. Second, as soon as a sale records, people start calling former owners offering to "recover your money" for a percentage, sometimes a third or more. You don't need them. The court process is designed for individuals, and a local attorney will usually handle it for a much smaller fee.

🚩 Rescue Offers to Walk Away From

  • 🚫 Anyone who asks you to sign your deed over to them while "they handle the bank," with you staying on as a renter. This is the most common foreclosure-rescue scheme in the country.
  • 🚫 Anyone who charges an upfront fee to negotiate a modification. Legitimate housing counseling is free.
  • 🚫 A cash offer with a long inspection period that runs past your sale date. Ask for a closing date in writing that is at least a week before the sale.
  • 🚫 Pressure to sell to an investor when you're underwater on a protected first loan. As explained above, that rarely helps you.

📋 What To Do This Week

  1. Find the sale date. It's on the Notice of Trustee's Sale. If you can't find the paper, search your name or address in the Maricopa County Recorder's online records.
  2. Count back 37 days. If you want a modification, your complete application needs to be in before that date.
  3. Request a written payoff and a written reinstatement quote from the servicer or trustee, for every loan on the house.
  4. Check for a second loan or HELOC. If there is one and you're underwater, talk to an Arizona attorney before deciding to let the sale happen.
  5. Get a free value opinion from a local agent, and, if time is short, a written cash offer. Compare the net to you, not the headline price.
  6. Call a HUD-approved housing counselor. It's free, and they can often reach the servicer's loss-mitigation department faster than you can.
  7. Protect the house. Keep the power on, the AC set, the pool treated, and the insurance active until you close.

✅ Bottom Line

Arizona gives Phoenix homeowners something rare: for most owner-occupied houses with a first mortgage, losing the house at a trustee's sale doesn't leave a debt behind. That's real protection for people who are underwater, and it means many of them should not sell to a cash buyer at all.

For everyone with equity, the same fast, non-judicial system is the threat. The 91-day clock leaves little room, there's no redemption after the sale, and auction prices and the surplus process rarely return what the house is worth. If you have equity and time, list it with a good agent and tell them the sale date. If you have equity and little time, or a house that won't pass a buyer's inspection, a fast cash sale before the sale date protects more of your money than waiting. Either way, find out which group you're in this week.

❓ Frequently Asked Questions

Will I owe the bank money after a trustee's sale in Arizona?

Usually not, if the house is on 2.5 acres or less and used as a one- or two-family dwelling and the lender forecloses by trustee's sale. A.R.S. § 33-814(G) bars a deficiency action in that case. The main exceptions involve second loans or HELOCs not used to buy the house, larger or commercial properties, and certain dwellings that were never completed or occupied. Confirm your situation with an Arizona attorney.

How long do I have after a Notice of Trustee's Sale is recorded in Arizona?

The sale cannot happen sooner than the 91st day after the notice is recorded under A.R.S. § 33-808. Sales are often postponed, but you should plan around the date printed on the notice.

Can I stop a trustee's sale in Phoenix by catching up on payments?

Yes. Under A.R.S. § 33-813 you can reinstate by paying the past-due amount, the trustee's costs, and a trustee's fee of the greater of $600 or 0.5% of the unpaid principal, before 5:00 p.m. Mountain Standard Time on the last business day before the sale.

Do I get time to buy my house back after an Arizona trustee's sale?

No. Arizona has no right of redemption after a trustee's sale. Once the trustee's deed is delivered, the buyer owns the house and can file an eviction case if you're still living there.

Does Arizona's anti-deficiency law protect my HELOC or second mortgage?

Not always. If a second loan was not used to buy the house and the first lender forecloses, the second lender may still be able to sue you on the note. If you have a second loan and are underwater, talk to an Arizona attorney before letting the sale go forward; a short sale may let you settle it.

Should I sell my Phoenix house to a cash buyer if I'm underwater?

Usually not. A cash buyer can't pay more than the house is worth, and if your only loan is a qualifying first mortgage, the anti-deficiency statute already protects you from a shortfall. A loan modification, short sale, or deed in lieu is usually the better conversation.

When does listing with an agent beat a cash sale before foreclosure in Phoenix?

When you have real equity, the house is in decent condition, and there are 75 or more days left before the sale date. In that case listing will usually net you noticeably more. A cash sale makes more sense with fewer than about 60 days left or a house that needs major repairs.

What happens to the extra money if my house sells for more than I owe at the trustee's sale?

Under A.R.S. § 33-812 the surplus pays junior liens in priority and then goes to the former owner. If there is a dispute, the trustee can deposit it with the county treasurer, and you apply to the superior court to have it released. You do not need to pay a surplus-recovery company a percentage.

How fast can a cash buyer close on a Phoenix house facing foreclosure?

Often in 10 to 21 days once title is clear, since there's no lender appraisal or loan approval. Make sure the written contract sets a closing date at least a week before the trustee's sale, and that the title company has the trustee's payoff.

Can my servicer foreclose while I'm applying for a loan modification?

Federal rules generally bar a servicer from going ahead with the sale if you submit a complete loss-mitigation application more than 37 days before it, until the servicer has evaluated you. Servicers also can't make the first foreclosure filing until you're more than 120 days behind on most home loans.
