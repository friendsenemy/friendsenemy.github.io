Selling a Probate House in Phoenix When the Owner Was a Snowbird

A large share of Phoenix-area estates belong to people who wintered in Arizona and legally lived in Minnesota, Illinois, Washington or Canada. Their main probate happens back home, but the Arizona house still needs someone with authority to sign the deed. Arizona makes that easier than most states: an executor appointed in the home state can often record proof of authority in Maricopa County and sell without opening a second full probate. Here's how that works, when you need local probate instead, the deadlines that matter, and whether to list or sell for cash.

By Dustin Ray, Founder of Pages of Purpose LLC. Not legal advice. Arizona probate law, federal tax withholding rules and Maricopa County practice change, laws differ by state and country, and every estate is different. Talk to an attorney licensed in Arizona, and a cross-border tax adviser if the owner was not a U.S. resident, before you sign anything.

Phoenix has a particular kind of probate house. The owner spent October through April in a ranch house in Mesa or a patio home in Sun City, kept a driver's license and voter registration back in the Midwest or Canada, and died with their will, bank accounts and family all somewhere else. The executor lives 1,500 miles away, has never dealt with Arizona law, and is now responsible for a house in the desert with a pool, an HOA and an air conditioner that has to keep running.

If that's your situation, this guide is written for you. It starts with the question that decides everything else: where did the owner legally live when they died?

🌵 If the Owner Lived Out of State, Arizona May Not Need a Second Probate

When a person dies owning real estate in a state other than their home state, the usual answer across the country is ancillary probate: a second, smaller probate case in the state where the property sits. Arizona offers a shortcut that many states don't.

Under A.R.S. 14-4204 and 14-4205, a personal representative appointed in the owner's home state (the "domiciliary foreign personal representative") can file certified copies of their appointment, and of any bond or bond waiver, with the Clerk of the Superior Court in the Arizona county where the property is. Arizona superior courts commonly provide a "proof of authority" form for this filing. Once filed, the foreign representative can exercise the powers of a local personal representative over the Arizona property, including selling the house, without opening a separate Arizona administration.

In practice, the executor:

  1. Gets appointed in the home state and obtains certified copies of letters.
  2. Files the proof of authority, certified letters and bond documents with the Maricopa County Clerk of Superior Court and pays the filing and certification fees.
  3. Records the filing with the Maricopa County Recorder.
  4. Signs and records a personal representative's deed to the buyer at closing.

The shortcut has limits. It isn't available if someone has already applied for a local Arizona administration, and the filing of a local application ends the foreign representative's power. Title companies also make their own decisions about what they'll insure, so check with the escrow company early. And it only helps if someone has actually been appointed in the home state; if the family is avoiding probate back home, the Arizona house still needs a path.

The most important question: where was the owner domiciled at death? Snowbirds often have ties to both places: a homestead exemption in one state, a driver's license in another, tax returns filed from a third. If domicile is unclear, ask an attorney before anyone files in either state, because it determines which court supervises the estate and what law governs the will.

🍁 Canadian Owners: The Federal Withholding Problem

The Phoenix area has one of the largest concentrations of Canadian seasonal homeowners in the United States. When a Canadian (or any non-U.S. resident) dies owning an Arizona house, the sale raises a federal issue that has nothing to do with probate: FIRPTA, the Foreign Investment in Real Property Tax Act.

When a foreign person, including a foreign decedent's estate, sells U.S. real estate, the buyer generally must withhold 15% of the gross sales price and send it to the IRS. The escrow company handles this in practice, taking it from the seller's proceeds. There's an important exception that changes the list-versus-cash decision:

  • If the buyer is an individual who intends to live in the house, the withholding is zero on a price up to $300,000 and 10% on a price from $300,000 to $1,000,000.
  • That residence exception doesn't apply to an investor or company buying to resell or rent. A cash sale to an investor (including us) means the full 15% is withheld.

The withheld amount is a deposit against the estate's actual U.S. tax, not the tax itself. If the real tax is lower (often the case, because the estate's basis is typically stepped up to the value at death), the estate can apply in advance for a withholding certificate to reduce it, or file a return afterward to get a refund. Either way, the money can be tied up for months. A Canadian estate also has its own tax filings in Canada. Bring in a cross-border accountant before listing.

We'll say this plainly: for a Canadian estate selling a house in decent condition under $300,000, an owner-occupant buyer can mean thousands of dollars less withheld at closing than a sale to any investor. Factor that in.

⏳ Arizona's Two-Year Window for Informal Probate

If the owner was an Arizona resident, or the family prefers a local case, the usual route is informal probate in Maricopa County Superior Court. One deadline catches families who wait: under A.R.S. 14-3108, an informal probate or appointment proceeding generally can't be started more than two years after the death.

The statute has exceptions, including one for re-probating a will that was already probated in the owner's home state and a limited exception when no court proceeding about the estate occurred in those two years. But missing the two-year mark typically pushes the family into formal proceedings in front of a judge, which take longer and cost more. If a parent died 18 months ago and the house is still in their name, it's time to act.

Who gets appointed

A.R.S. 14-3203 sets the priority for appointment as personal representative: first a person named in a probated will, then a surviving spouse who is a devisee, then other devisees, then a surviving spouse, then other heirs. Arizona doesn't bar nonresidents from serving, which helps families who all live out of state. A bond can be waived by the will or in other circumstances the court allows.

🏠 How an Estate Sale Closes in Arizona

Arizona real estate closings run through escrow and title companies rather than attorneys, which surprises executors from attorney-closing states. For an estate sale, the escrow officer will typically want:

  • Certified, recently issued letters of appointment (or the recorded proof of authority for a foreign representative).
  • A certified death certificate.
  • The personal representative's deed, signed by the representative in that capacity.
  • An affidavit of property value, which Arizona requires with most deeds.
  • Payoffs for any mortgage, HOA balance and property taxes, plus the solar lease if there is one.

An Arizona personal representative in an unsupervised estate can usually sell without a court order confirming the price, and Arizona has no state real estate transfer tax, so closing costs tend to be modest. Executors can sign many documents remotely, but plan ahead: some steps need a notary or mobile signing service where the executor lives.

A smaller estate may not need a personal representative at all. Arizona's real property affidavit now covers houses with a net assessed value up to $300,000 for affidavits filed since September 26, 2025, after a six-month waiting period. 👵 Selling in an Age-Restricted Community

Many snowbird estates include houses in age-restricted communities such as Sun City, Sun City West and parts of the East Valley. These communities restrict who can live there (generally households with at least one resident 55 or older, with limits on younger residents). That has practical effects for an estate sale:

  • The buyer pool is limited to qualifying households, though it's a large and active one, much of it paying cash.
  • An heir under 55 may not be able to move in, which removes the "one of us keeps it" option for some families.
  • Recreation center fees and transfer or preferred-buyer fees are charged at sale; get them from the association early.
  • Many of these homes are well kept but dated, with original kitchens, older roofs and aging AC units, which affects price more than location does.

🧾 What the Estate Pays While the House Sits

Out-of-state executors tend to underestimate the cost of holding a Phoenix house, mostly because of the heat:

  • Electricity: $200-$400 a month in summer to keep the AC running. Shutting it off risks damage to the interior.
  • Property tax: typically $150-$250 a month. Arizona doesn't reassess on death, but the homeowner's rebate on school taxes ends when nobody lives there as a primary residence; for a snowbird whose house was already a secondary home, nothing changes.
  • Insurance: many policies limit coverage after the house has been vacant 30-60 days. A vacant-home policy commonly runs $100-$200 a month.
  • HOA and recreation fees: $50-$300 a month in many communities.
  • Pool, landscaping and house checks: $150-$300 a month, especially when no family member lives nearby.

That's roughly $650-$1,450 a month. Six months of drift while the home-state probate gets organized costs $4,000-$9,000.

🔀 Choosing a Path for the Estate

List it with a local agent

Usually the most money

An executor who can coordinate cleanout, minor repairs and a listing from a distance will usually net the most, especially in active 55-plus communities where buyers are plentiful. For a foreign estate, an owner-occupant buyer can also lower FIRPTA withholding. If the house is in reasonable shape, start here.

Sell as-is for cash

For distance, condition and speed

A cash sale makes sense when the executor can't manage repairs and showings from another state, the house needs a new AC unit or roof, the contents haven't been cleared, or the estate needs to close quickly. For a U.S. estate, there's no withholding issue. For a foreign estate, factor in the full 15% withholding.

Distribute the house to the heirs

When someone wants it

An heir who wants the house can take it as part of their share. If several heirs inherit it together and later disagree, the options shift to the rules covered in our guides on co-owner sales and heirs who can't agree.

💵 Running the Numbers on a Sun City West Estate House

Cash buyers generally pay about 60-80% of after-repair value. Here's a realistic example.

A 1980s two-bedroom patio home in Sun City West. Updated homes nearby sell for about $340,000. This one has its original kitchen and baths, a 17-year-old AC unit and an aging roof, and is still full of furniture: about $45,000 of updating to bring it to market standard.

  • U.S. estate, cash sale: an offer around $215,000-$245,000 (roughly 63-72% of repaired value), contents left behind, closing in two to three weeks after the executor's authority is recorded.
  • U.S. estate, listed as-is: maybe $285,000-$300,000 to a qualifying 55-plus buyer, minus about 5-6% commission, escrow and title costs, community transfer fees, a cleanout ($2,000-$4,000) and three months of carrying costs. Net around $260,000-$275,000. Listing nets more if the executor can manage it.
  • Canadian estate, same house: on the cash sale, 15% of the price (about $34,000) is withheld at closing until the estate's tax is settled. On a listing to an owner-occupant at $290,000, the residence exception may mean no withholding at all. For a foreign estate, that alone is a strong reason to list.

The honest summary: listing is usually better for a sound house. Cash makes sense when distance, condition, contents or time make a listing impractical, and the estate is better off with a fast, certain sale than a higher, slower one.

📋 What To Do This Week

  1. Settle the domicile question. Gather the owner's driver's license, voter registration, tax returns and homestead filings, and confirm with an attorney where the main probate belongs.
  2. Check the date of death against the two-year informal probate limit if an Arizona case may be needed.
  3. If an executor is already appointed back home, order several certified copies of the letters and ask an Arizona attorney or the escrow company about filing a proof of authority in Maricopa County.
  4. Protect the house: keep the AC and power on, notify the insurer of the death and vacancy, and arrange weekly checks and pool service.
  5. If the owner wasn't a U.S. resident, contact a cross-border accountant about FIRPTA and a withholding certificate before signing any contract.
  6. Collect the house paperwork: HOA or community association contacts, solar contract, mortgage statement and property tax status from the Maricopa County Treasurer.
  7. Get two numbers: an agent's pricing opinion and a written cash offer, and compare net proceeds after withholding, costs and time.

✅ Bottom Line

For a Phoenix-area house owned by someone who lived elsewhere, the first step isn't a Maricopa County probate filing. It's confirming where the owner was domiciled and whether a home-state executor can simply record proof of authority in Arizona and sell. For an Arizona resident, informal probate is usually quick, but it has a two-year window. For a Canadian owner, FIRPTA withholding can make an owner-occupant buyer worth much more than any investor.

If the house is in good shape and you can manage a listing from a distance, list it. If the distance, the condition, the contents or the calendar make that unrealistic, ask us. We'll give you a straight comparison, including what withholding would do to your net.

❓ Frequently Asked Questions

Does an out-of-state executor need to open probate in Arizona to sell a house?

Often not. Under A.R.S. 14-4204 and 14-4205, a personal representative appointed in the owner's home state can file certified copies of their appointment and bond documents with the Clerk of Superior Court in the Arizona county where the property is, then exercise a local representative's powers, including selling. This is not available if a local Arizona administration has been applied for.

What is a proof of authority in Arizona probate?

It is the filing a domiciliary foreign personal representative makes in the Arizona county where the decedent's property is located, with certified letters of appointment and bond or bond-waiver documents. After it is filed and recorded, the representative can sign a deed transferring Arizona real property.

Is there a deadline to open informal probate in Arizona?

Generally yes. A.R.S. 14-3108 says an informal probate or appointment proceeding generally cannot be commenced more than two years after the death, with limited exceptions. After that, families usually need formal proceedings before a judge.

Who has priority to be personal representative of an Arizona estate?

Under A.R.S. 14-3203, priority goes first to a person named in a probated will, then a surviving spouse who is a devisee, other devisees, a surviving spouse, and then other heirs. Arizona does not bar nonresidents from serving.

What is FIRPTA withholding on a Canadian estate selling a Phoenix house?

When a foreign person or foreign estate sells U.S. real estate, the buyer generally must withhold 15% of the gross price for the IRS. If the buyer is an individual who will live in the house, withholding is zero on a price up to $300,000 and 10% up to $1,000,000. Investor buyers do not qualify for that exception.

Can FIRPTA withholding be reduced for an estate?

Yes. The withheld amount is a deposit against the actual tax. The estate can apply to the IRS for a withholding certificate to reduce it, or file a U.S. return after the sale to get a refund of the excess. Both take time, so plan with a cross-border tax adviser.

Does an Arizona personal representative need court approval to sell the house?

Usually not in an unsupervised estate. The personal representative can generally sell real estate without a court order confirming the price, signing a personal representative's deed through escrow.

How do Arizona estate closings work for an executor who lives far away?

Arizona closings are handled by escrow and title companies. The escrow officer will usually want certified letters or the recorded proof of authority, a death certificate, the personal representative's deed and an affidavit of property value. Many documents can be signed with a notary where the executor lives.

Can heirs under 55 move into an inherited Sun City house?

Often not as permanent residents. Age-restricted communities such as Sun City and Sun City West limit occupancy, generally requiring households to include someone 55 or older and restricting younger residents. Check the community's rules before planning to keep the house.

Is it better for an estate to list a Phoenix house or sell it for cash?

For a house in reasonable shape, listing usually nets more, and for a foreign estate an owner-occupant buyer can also reduce FIRPTA withholding. A cash sale tends to make sense when the executor is far away, the house needs major work or still holds the contents, or the estate needs a fast and certain closing.
