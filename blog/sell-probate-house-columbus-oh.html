Selling a Probate House in Columbus, Ohio: Start With the Power-of-Sale Clause

In Ohio, one sentence in the will decides how hard it is to sell the house. With a power of sale, the executor signs and Franklin County Probate Court never has to approve the deal. Without one, every heir has to consent in writing and the price can't fall below 80% of the appraised value, or the estate files a land sale lawsuit. Here's how each path works, what it costs, and when a cash sale makes sense.

By Dustin Ray, Founder of Pages of Purpose LLC. This is general information, not legal advice. Ohio probate statutes and Franklin County Probate Court practice change, laws differ by state, and every estate is different. Talk to a probate attorney licensed in Ohio, where the property is, before you sign or file anything.

Most families who inherit a house in Columbus ask the same first question: how long until we can sell it? The honest answer in Ohio is that it depends less on the court's calendar than on what the will says and whether the family agrees. Ohio gives executors three very different routes to a sale, and the will usually decides which one you're on before you ever walk into the probate court at 373 South High Street.

This guide starts with that decision, then covers the shortcuts that can skip probate entirely, the rights a surviving spouse holds over the house, a realistic Franklin County timeline, the money the estate burns while the house sits, and the honest comparison between listing it and taking a cash offer.

🔑 The Clause in the Will That Decides Your Columbus Sale

Find the will and look for language giving the executor the power to sell real estate. It's often phrased as "full power to sell, at public or private sale, any real or personal property" or something close. Under Ohio Revised Code § 2113.39, when a will gives the executor a power to sell, the executor can sell the house without an order from the probate court, unless the will itself limits that power.

That's the fast lane. Once Franklin County Probate Court appoints the executor and issues letters of authority, the executor can list the house, accept an offer, and sign the deed. No hearing on the price. No appraisal requirement tied to the sale. No need for every beneficiary to sign. The executor still owes the beneficiaries a duty to get a fair price and account for the money, but the court isn't approving the transaction.

If the will has no power of sale, or there's no will at all, Ohio law puts you on one of two slower routes. Which one depends on whether the family is unanimous.

⚠️ Read the will before anyone signs a listing agreement or a purchase contract. An executor without a power of sale who signs a contract as if they had one has promised something they can't legally deliver on their own. The buyer can't close, and the estate may end up in a dispute it didn't need.

✍️ No Power of Sale? Unanimous Consent and the 80% Floor

Ohio's middle route is in R.C. § 2127.011. If the will doesn't grant a power of sale, or there is no will, the executor or administrator can still sell the house without a lawsuit if the surviving spouse and all of the beneficiaries (or, with no will, all of the heirs) sign written consents and those consents are filed with the probate court. Franklin County Probate Court has its own form for this, titled "Consent to Power to Sell Real Estate."

The catch is a price floor. Under the consent route, the house can't be sold for less than 80% of its appraised value as shown in the approved inventory. The consent route also isn't available if any person whose consent is needed is a minor.

That 80% floor matters a great deal if you're thinking about a cash buyer. Suppose the inventory values the house at $250,000. Under the consent route, the lowest legal sale price is $200,000. Many cash offers on houses that need work come in below that. If the house really is worth less than the inventory says (because the appraiser didn't see the water in the basement), the estate either has to revisit the valuation, move to a different route, or find a buyer who will pay at least the floor.

We'd rather tell you that up front than have you discover it at closing. If our number can't clear the 80% floor, the consent route won't work for our offer, and we'll explain the alternatives.

⚖️ When Someone Won't Sign: The Chapter 2127 Land Sale Action

If the will has no power of sale and even one beneficiary or heir won't consent, or one of them is a minor, the executor files a complaint to sell real estate under R.C. Chapter 2127 in Franklin County Probate Court. The court requires a deposit when the complaint is filed (Franklin County lists a $175 deposit for a land sale complaint), every person with an interest has to be made a party and served, and the court decides whether and on what terms the house can be sold.

Land sale actions are also the required path when the estate needs the house sold to pay debts and the personal property isn't enough. They add months, attorney time, and title work to the process. They also put a judge between the family and the deal, which can be a relief when the family is stuck, because it moves the argument out of the kitchen and into a courtroom with a clear decision at the end.

🔎 Check for a Shortcut Before Opening an Estate

A large share of Columbus houses never need a probate sale at all. Before anyone files, check these three things.

📄 A transfer-on-death designation affidavit

Ohio lets an owner record a transfer-on-death designation affidavit (R.C. § 5302.22) naming who gets the house at death. If one was recorded before the owner died, the beneficiary signs an affidavit of confirmation, attaches a certified death certificate, presents it to the Franklin County Auditor, and records it with the Franklin County Recorder (R.C. § 5302.222). No probate filing is needed to move title, and the beneficiary can then sell as the owner. Search the property on the Recorder's site before you do anything else.

👥 A survivorship deed

If the deed says the owners hold "for their joint lives, remainder to the survivor," the surviving owner already owns the house. They record an affidavit with the death certificate and can sell without the estate.

📉 Release from administration

Ohio's simplified process, release from administration under R.C. § 2113.03, is available when probate assets are $35,000 or less, or $100,000 or less when everything passes to the surviving spouse. Franklin County lists the filing cost at roughly $105 to $115, against a recommended $250 deposit for a full administration. With most Columbus houses worth well over $35,000, a house in the decedent's sole name usually rules out release unless the spouse is the only heir and the estate is modest.

When the house does go through the estate, an executor can also apply for a certificate of transfer under R.C. § 2113.61 after the inventory is filed. That moves title out of the estate and into the names of the heirs or beneficiaries, who then sell as co-owners. The 80% floor doesn't apply to a sale by the heirs as owners, but every co-owner has to sign the deed.

👩‍❤️‍👨 The Surviving Spouse Holds Rights Over the House

Ohio gives a surviving spouse a set of rights in the "mansion house," the decedent's home, that can change the whole plan. These are spelled out in Franklin County's own summary of surviving spouse rights:

  • 🏠 One year rent-free. The spouse may remain in the mansion house for one year from the date of death without paying rent (R.C. § 2106.15). Nobody can force a sale out from under them during that year.
  • 💲 The right to buy it at appraisal. If the house wasn't specifically left to someone else, the spouse can petition to purchase it at the appraised value (R.C. § 2106.16). The petition has to come after the inventory is filed and within one month after it's approved.
  • 🏛️ Taking it as part of the share. The spouse may also elect to take the mansion house as part of their share of the estate (R.C. § 2106.10).
  • 💵 The $40,000 allowance. A surviving spouse is entitled to an allowance for support of $40,000 (R.C. § 2106.13), which comes ahead of most other distributions and can mean the house has to be sold to pay it.

These rights have deadlines. Franklin County's summary says they must be exercised within five months of the first appointment of an executor or administrator. If you're a child of the decedent and a stepparent is living in the house, don't plan a sale until you know which of these rights the spouse intends to use.

When there's no will, R.C. § 2105.06 decides who inherits. If all of the decedent's children are also the surviving spouse's children, the spouse takes everything. If not, the spouse takes a fixed dollar amount ($20,000 or $60,000 depending on the family) plus a fraction of the rest, and the children share the remainder. Blended families in Ohio are exactly where those numbers start to matter. Our guide on what happens to a house when there's no will walks through the general rules.

Two Ohio deadlines run in the background of every estate. Creditors must present claims within six months of the date of death under R.C. § 2117.06, or they're barred, whether or not an executor was ever appointed. And the executor has to file an inventory within three months of appointment (R.C. § 2115.02) and generally a final account within six months unless the court extends it. Those deadlines are why many Columbus estates that start in spring are ready to sell by late summer, and why a house usually can't be distributed outright before the six-month creditor window closes.

💸 What the Estate Pays While the House Sits

Ohio property taxes are high compared to much of the country, and Franklin County is no exception. Recent estimates put the county's median effective rate around 1.6% to 1.7% of value. On a $250,000 house, that's roughly $4,000 a year, or about $335 a month. The owner-occupancy credit only applies when the owner lives in the home as their primary residence on January 1, so it isn't something the estate can count on once the house is empty.

  • 🏛️ Property tax: about $335 a month on a $250,000 house.
  • 🛡️ Vacant-home insurance: usually $100 to $200 a month once the standard policy's vacancy clause kicks in.
  • 💡 Utilities: $150 to $250 a month to keep gas heat on through a Columbus winter and avoid frozen pipes.
  • 🌿 Lawn, snow, and upkeep: $100 to $200 a month, more if the City of Columbus code enforcement gets a complaint.
  • ⚖️ Attorney fees: Franklin County probate attorneys commonly charge hourly; a straightforward estate with a power of sale often costs a few thousand dollars, and a land sale action costs considerably more.
  • 📊 Executor commission: R.C. § 2113.35 sets the executor's commission at 4% of the first $100,000, 3% of the next $300,000, and 2% above that, applied to the gross proceeds of real estate sold through the estate. Real estate that isn't sold carries 1%. Family executors often waive it, but it's the executor's to claim.

Put together, an empty Columbus house typically costs the estate $700 to $1,000 a month before any mortgage payment. Six months of waiting is $4,000 to $6,000 out of the heirs' pockets.

🧮 Listing vs. a Cash Sale: Two Columbus Houses

Cash buyers generally pay about 60% to 80% of what a house would be worth fully repaired, landing lower in that range the more work it needs. Here's how that plays out on two very different estates.

🏡 Clintonville bungalow, good condition, three agreeable heirs

List it

Updated 1920s bungalow, worth about $420,000 as it stands, executor holds a power of sale. Listed, it could sell in a few weeks; after a 5% to 6% commission and closing costs, the estate nets around $390,000. A cash offer, even at the top of the usual range, would be roughly $300,000 to $335,000. That gap is far bigger than anything the estate saves in carrying costs. List it with a good agent. We'd tell you the same thing if you called us.

🔧 Linden two-story, deferred maintenance, heirs in three states

A cash sale is a fair option

Worth about $240,000 fully repaired, needs roughly $55,000 of work (furnace, roof, a basement that leaks every spring), consent route because the will is silent on selling, inventory value $180,000, so the 80% floor is $144,000. A cash offer at 60% to 80% of $240,000 is $144,000 to $192,000, and with that much work it lands near the bottom, around $145,000 to $160,000, just above the floor. Listed as-is, a financed buyer's inspection would likely stall the deal, and an investor buying through an agent will pay about the same as a cash buyer, minus commission. If the cash number clears the floor and saves six months of $800 carrying costs and three out-of-state heirs managing contractors, it's a reasonable choice. If it doesn't clear the floor, the estate needs a different route.

🤝 One heir wants out, the others want to keep it

Buy out or sell the share

After a certificate of transfer, each heir owns a share. The heirs who want to keep the house can buy out the one who doesn't. If they can't, the leaving heir can sell their share. If it comes to a partition case under R.C. Chapter 5307 in the court of common pleas, Ohio lets a co-owner elect to take the whole property at its appraised value, paying one-third in cash, one-third in a year, and one-third in two years, which can be a workable buyout. See our partition lawsuit guide.

🏥 Ohio's Medicaid Recovery Reaches Past Probate

If the decedent was 55 or older and received Medicaid, or lived permanently in a nursing facility, the Ohio Attorney General's office may pursue estate recovery on behalf of the Ohio Department of Medicaid. Ohio defines the recoverable "estate" broadly: it includes non-probate assets like survivorship interests and transfer-on-death property, not just what goes through probate court. That means a TOD affidavit that skips probate doesn't necessarily skip a Medicaid claim. Recovery is delayed while a surviving spouse is alive, and it's barred when there is a surviving child under 21 or a blind or disabled child. Hardship waivers exist but have short deadlines. If Medicaid paid for long-term care, factor the claim in before you calculate what the heirs will net from either a listing or a cash sale.

📋 What To Do This Week

  1. Search the Franklin County Recorder for the property. Look for a transfer-on-death designation affidavit or a survivorship deed. If you find one, you may not need probate at all.
  2. Read the will for a power of sale. Mark the paragraph and bring it to the attorney.
  3. List every heir or beneficiary, note any who are minors, and find out whether each one would sign a written consent to sell.
  4. Ask whether a surviving spouse plans to stay in the house for the rent-free year or buy it at appraisal.
  5. Secure the house: change the locks, keep the heat on, switch to a vacant-dwelling policy, and forward the mail.
  6. Find out if Medicaid paid for any of the decedent's care after age 55.
  7. Get two numbers: a written cash offer and a local agent's opinion of value, and compare what each nets after commission, repairs, and months of carrying costs.

✅ Bottom Line

In Columbus, the route to selling a probate house is mostly decided before anyone files: a recorded TOD affidavit or survivorship deed skips probate; a power of sale in the will lets the executor sell with no court approval; unanimous written consent works if the price clears 80% of the inventory value; and anything less than unanimity means a land sale action. Layer in the surviving spouse's rights and the six-month creditor window, and you have the real timeline.

If the house is in good condition and the family agrees, list it and keep the difference. If it needs serious work, the heirs are scattered, or every month of Ohio property tax and winter utilities is eating the estate, a cash sale can make sense, as long as the number clears whatever floor your route imposes. ❓ Frequently Asked Questions

Does an Ohio executor need court approval to sell a house?

Not if the will gives the executor a power to sell. Under R.C. 2113.39, an executor with a power of sale can sell real estate without a probate court order unless the will limits that power. Without a power of sale, the executor needs either written consent from the spouse and all beneficiaries or heirs, or a land sale order under R.C. Chapter 2127.

What is the 80% rule when selling a probate house in Ohio?

When an executor sells under the consent route in R.C. 2127.011, the sale price can't be less than 80% of the appraised value shown in the approved inventory. The rule doesn't apply when the will grants a power of sale or when heirs sell as owners after a certificate of transfer.

Can heirs sell a Columbus house if one of them is a minor?

Not through the consent route, which isn't available when anyone whose consent is required is a minor. The estate generally has to use a land sale action in Franklin County Probate Court unless the will grants the executor a power of sale.

How do I sell a house that has an Ohio transfer-on-death designation affidavit?

The named beneficiary signs an affidavit of confirmation under R.C. 5302.222, attaches a certified death certificate, presents it to the Franklin County Auditor, and records it with the Recorder. Once recorded, the beneficiary owns the house and can sell it without opening a probate estate.

Can a surviving spouse stay in the house during probate in Ohio?

Yes. Under R.C. 2106.15 a surviving spouse may live in the mansion house for one year from the date of death without paying rent. The spouse may also have the right to buy the house at its appraised value under R.C. 2106.16 if it wasn't specifically left to someone else.

How long do creditors have to file claims against an Ohio estate?

Six months from the date of death under R.C. 2117.06. Claims not presented within that window are barred, even if no executor was ever appointed.

What does it cost to open a probate estate in Franklin County?

Franklin County Probate Court lists a statutory minimum deposit of $125 for a full administration and recommends depositing $250; unused deposit money is refunded when the case closes. Release from administration costs roughly $105 to $115. A land sale complaint requires a $175 deposit. Attorney fees are separate.

Who inherits a house in Ohio when there is no will?

R.C. 2105.06 controls. If all of the children are also the surviving spouse's children, the spouse inherits everything. If some or all of the children are from another relationship, the spouse receives $20,000 or $60,000 plus a share of the rest, and the children split the remainder. With no spouse, the children inherit equally.

Can Ohio Medicaid estate recovery take an inherited house that skipped probate?

Potentially. Ohio's estate recovery definition includes non-probate assets such as survivorship and transfer-on-death interests. Recovery waits until a surviving spouse dies and is barred if there's a surviving child under 21 or a blind or disabled child.

Is listing or a cash sale better for a probate house in Columbus?

If the house is in good shape and the heirs agree, listing usually nets more, often tens of thousands of dollars more. A cash sale makes sense when the house needs major repairs, heirs live far away, or carrying costs are mounting, and only if the offer clears any price floor your route requires.
