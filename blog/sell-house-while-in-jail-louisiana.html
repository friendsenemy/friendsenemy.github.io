Selling a Louisiana House From Jail or Prison: One Procuration, One Notary, Two Witnesses

Louisiana is a civil-law state, and a power of attorney to sell a house here is a procuration that has to match the authentic act used to pass title. Getting a notary and two witnesses into a DOC prison or parish jail is the real work. Here's how that signing, community property, successions and Louisiana's fast executory-process foreclosure fit together, with honest numbers on when listing beats a cash sale.

By Dustin Ray, Founder of Pages of Purpose LLC. Not legal advice. Laws differ by state, Louisiana's Civil Code and corrections procedures change, and every title examiner has its own requirements. Before anyone signs a procuration or act of sale, or files in a succession or partition, talk to an attorney licensed in Louisiana, where the property is.

Families calling us about a Louisiana house usually arrive with a generic "power of attorney" form printed off the internet, already signed in front of a jail notary. Then the closing attorney looks at it and says it won't work. Nobody explained that Louisiana runs on the civil law, that a power of attorney here is a contract called a mandate, that the document itself is called a procuration, and that the paper authorizing a home sale has to be built for the way Louisiana passes title to land.

So this guide starts with the signature, not with whether a sale is possible (it is; see our national guide on selling while incarcerated for the basics). In Louisiana the question that decides how long this takes is narrower: can you get a notary and two witnesses into the same room as the owner, with a procuration that a title insurer will accept, before the lender's executory process gets to the sheriff's sale?

📜 A Louisiana Procuration Has to Match the Act of Sale

Article 2993 of the Louisiana Civil Code says a mandate needs no particular form, then adds the sentence that matters: when the law prescribes a form for an act, the mandate authorizing that act must be in that same form. Article 1839 requires a transfer of immovable property to be made by authentic act or by act under private signature, and it only binds third persons once it is filed in the conveyance records of the parish where the house sits.

On paper, then, a signed writing is the legal floor. In practice, Louisiana closings are passed as authentic acts in front of a notary, the procuration gets recorded alongside the act of sale, and title underwriters want the procuration in authentic form too. An authentic act, under article 1833, is a writing executed before a notary public in the presence of two witnesses and signed by the party, both witnesses and the notary. Article 1836 adds that a merely acknowledged private act, even one the signer swore to, "cannot substitute for an authentic act." Expect the closing attorney to ask for the full ceremony.

Two more Civil Code rules shape what the procuration must say:

  • 🖊️ Express authority to sell. Article 2996 says the power to alienate, acquire, encumber or lease a thing "must be given expressly." A broad "handle all my affairs" clause is not enough. The good news is that the same article says the property and its location need not be described, though a legal description and municipal address help the title examiner.
  • 🧾 Express authority to deal with a succession. Article 2997 lists further powers that must be spelled out, including accepting or renouncing a succession, compromising a claim and borrowing. If the inmate is an heir to a parent's house, the procuration should say the agent may accept the succession and sign the judgment of possession paperwork.

One pleasant surprise: Louisiana mandates don't need the "durable" magic words other states require. Article 3024 lists what ends a mandate beyond ordinary contract causes: the death of the principal or of the mandatary, the interdiction of the mandatary, and the qualification of a curator after the principal is interdicted. A long sentence is not on that list. A procuration signed in the first month of a ten-year term still works in year eight, as long as it hasn't been revoked.

⚠️ The mistake we see most: a one-page out-of-state form, signed before one notary with no witnesses, that never mentions selling immovable property. In Louisiana that document may let someone talk to the bank, but a title company will almost always refuse to insure a sale signed under it. Have a Louisiana attorney or notary draft the procuration first, then arrange the signing.

🏛️ Why the Prison's Own Notary May Not Be Allowed to Help

Families assume the easiest notary is the one already on staff. Louisiana law makes that less simple than it sounds. Under R.S. 15:825.1, the secretary of corrections may designate department employees at each unit to serve as ex officio notaries, and their acts are free. But the same statute limits their authority to acts and instruments to which the department or its secretary is a party, and to oaths and documents concerning matters in which the department has an interest.

A procuration letting a sister sell a house in Gentilly is not a Department of Corrections matter. Some staff notaries also hold a regular commission and may help in that capacity, and some facilities have their own policies, but don't count on it. The safer plan is an outside Louisiana notary, often the closing attorney who will pass the act of sale, scheduled as a legal or professional visit.

Then there are the witnesses. Article 1833 lets the parties, notary and witnesses sign at different times and places only in a limited way: each party must still sign in the presence of a notary and two witnesses. That means three outsiders, or a notary plus two people the facility allows to serve as witnesses. Ask the facility directly whether staff or the notary's own assistant may witness, because some wardens forbid employees from witnessing private documents and every added visitor needs clearance.

🗺️ State Prisoner, Parish Jail: Who Controls the Room

Louisiana is unusual in where its state prisoners actually sleep. Reporting from the Louisiana Illuminator found that in 2022 more than 14,000 people serving Department of Public Safety and Corrections sentences, about 53 percent, were held in local parish jails, the highest share in the country. A person "in DOC custody" may be at Angola, at Elayn Hunt in St. Gabriel, or in a sheriff's jail three parishes away.

That changes the phone calls:

  • 🏢 DOC-run prisons (Louisiana State Penitentiary, Dixon, Elayn Hunt, Raymond Laborde and the others): start with the inmate's classification officer or the facility's legal programs office. Approval for an outside notary and witnesses commonly takes two to five weeks, and transfers between units can scrap a scheduled visit.
  • 🚔 Parish jails holding DOC inmates or pretrial detainees: the sheriff sets the rules. Some allow attorney contact visits within days; others offer only video or glass. The 15:825.1 staff-notary designation applies to department units, so don't assume a parish jail has any notary at all.
  • 🦅 Federal facilities in Louisiana (Oakdale, Pollock): federal staff can take acknowledgments, but the two-witness authentic act still has to be satisfied for a Louisiana title company. Our federal prison guide covers the Bureau of Prisons side.

If the owner is held outside Louisiana, a procuration executed there in the form that state accepts is often valid, but Louisiana underwriters still prefer a notary and two witnesses. Build the Louisiana format into the document and the out-of-state notary only has to watch it signed.

💍 Community Property: The Spouse at Home Can't Sign Alone, Unless He Renounces

Most married Louisiana couples live under the legal regime of community of acquets and gains. A house bought during the marriage is presumed community property even if only one name is on the act of sale. Article 2347 requires the concurrence of both spouses to alienate, encumber or lease a community immovable. A wife running the household while her husband is at Angola cannot sell, refinance or even lease the house on her own signature.

There are two ways around it. The obvious one is a procuration from the incarcerated spouse naming her as agent. The less known one is article 2348: a spouse may expressly renounce the right to concur in the alienation, encumbrance or lease of a community immovable, and the renunciation may be made irrevocable for a stated term of up to three years. For a family facing a long sentence, a recorded renunciation can be cleaner than a procuration, because it lets the spouse at home act in her own name. Have the lawyer pass it in authentic form so the conveyance records show it.

If the marriage is ending, a judgment of divorce ends the community retroactively to the day the petition was filed, and the spouses become co-owners. Then the rules in our guide to selling during a divorce take over, and a partition of the former community in the divorce court can award the house outright to one spouse.

🧬 When the House Came Through a Succession

Louisiana calls probate a "succession," and it is handled by the district court of the parish where the person lived; in New Orleans that is the Civil District Court for the Parish of Orleans. Three civil-law features catch families with an heir in prison:

  • 👵 The surviving spouse's usufruct. Under article 890, when a spouse dies leaving descendants, the survivor gets a usufruct over the deceased's half of the community, unless a testament disposed of it, lasting until the survivor dies or remarries. The children own the naked ownership; the parent has the use. A sale needs both the naked owners, including the one in prison, and the usufructuary.
  • ⚖️ Forced heirship, narrowly. Only children 23 or younger at the parent's death, or children permanently unable to care for themselves, are forced heirs. An adult son in prison can be left out of a testament entirely, which sometimes means he has no signature to give.
  • 📄 The small succession affidavit. If the Louisiana property is worth $125,000 or less gross, or the death was at least 20 years ago, Code of Civil Procedure articles 3421 and 3431-3432 allow heirs to transfer it by sworn affidavit without opening a court succession, when there is no testament or the testament qualifies. The affidavit has to list every heir, and an heir who doesn't sign must be accounted for.

Once a judgment of possession or affidavit puts the heirs in title, they own in indivision, and any one of them can demand a partition. If the incarcerated heir refuses or nobody can get his signature, a co-heir can ask the court to sell the house by licitation, the Louisiana version of a partition sale. See our partition lawsuit explainer for what that costs and how long it runs.

🚫 Interdiction Is Not a Workaround for a Competent Inmate

Every few months a relative asks whether they can "get a curatorship" so they can sign for the person in prison. Louisiana's version of guardianship is interdiction, and article 389 allows full interdiction only of a person who, because of a medical condition, is unable to consistently make reasoned decisions about his care and property, or to communicate them, and only when less restrictive means won't do. Being locked up is not a medical condition. A competent prisoner who simply won't sign cannot be interdicted for convenience.

Interdiction is the right tool in one situation we do see: an owner who has dementia, a brain injury or serious mental illness, whether in a jail medical unit or a nursing home. Then a curator appointed by the district court can sell with court authority. Budget $3,000 to $8,000 in legal fees and two to four months, and note that article 3024 ends any earlier procuration once that curator qualifies.

⚡ Executory Process: The Clock Runs Faster Here

Most Louisiana mortgages are passed in authentic form and contain a confession of judgment. That lets the lender use executory process: instead of suing and waiting for a trial, it files a verified petition with the note and a certified copy of the mortgage, and a judge can sign an order of seizure and sale within days. The sheriff records the seizure, advertises the sale, and auctions the house. Louisiana real estate lawyers at Kean Miller describe a typical timeline of 75 to 120 days from petition to sheriff's sale.

Add the federal servicing rule that generally bars a first foreclosure filing until the loan is more than 120 days delinquent (our mortgage guide walks through it), and a family has roughly seven to eight months from the first missed payment to an auction. That sounds like plenty until you subtract five weeks to clear a notary and two witnesses into a prison.

The appraisal detail matters for the equity. If the sale is held with appraisal, bidding opens at two-thirds of the appraised value and the lender keeps its right to a deficiency judgment. On a second, reset sale with no bidders, the opening bid can drop to costs. Either way, an auction rarely brings what a listed sale would, and whatever surplus is left must be claimed. 🛡️ What the Homestead Protects, and What It Doesn't

Louisiana has two homestead rules, and families confuse them.

The property tax homestead exemption removes the first $7,500 of assessed value, which is $75,000 of market value because Louisiana assesses homes at 10 percent of fair market value. It requires that the owner live there as a primary residence. When a single owner goes to prison and the house sits empty, the assessor may remove it, and the next bill can jump by several hundred dollars.

The creditor homestead exemption in R.S. 20:1 shields up to $35,000 of a homestead's value from seizure, on up to five acres in a municipality or 200 acres outside one. It does not apply to mortgage debt or taxes. And one exception in subsection C matters for this audience: the exemption does not protect against "any obligation arising from the conviction of a felony or misdemeanor which has the possibility of imprisonment of at least six months." Restitution, fines and civil judgments tied to the conviction can reach the house, and a title search may turn up those liens at closing.

🧭 Four Louisiana Routes, Weighed Plainly

Authentic-form procuration, then list with an agent

Usually the most money

If the house is in decent shape, the mortgage isn't near executory process and a relative can handle showings, an agent will usually net more than any cash buyer. One well-drafted procuration signed with a notary and two witnesses lets the agent sell without another trip inside.

Spouse renounces concurrence and sells

Cleanest for married owners

For community property with a cooperative incarcerated spouse, an article 2348 renunciation lets the spouse at home sign in her own name for up to three years. It still needs a signing session inside, but only once, and it isn't tied to any single buyer.

Sell as-is to a cash buyer

Best when the clock is short

When the lender has filed for executory process, the house needs serious work, or nobody can manage a listing for four to six months, an as-is sale trades some price for speed. We often close in three to five weeks once the procuration is recorded.

Partition by licitation or a curator's sale

Last resort

When a co-heir in prison won't sign or a disabled owner can't, the district court can order a sale. It works, but expect $8,000 to $20,000 in legal fees for a contested partition and six to eighteen months.

📋 What To Do This Week

  1. 📑 Pull the act of sale from the parish conveyance records and confirm whose names are on it and whether the house is community or separate property.
  2. 🏢 Find out exactly where the owner is held, DOC prison or parish jail, and call that facility's legal visits or classification office about an outside notary and two witnesses.
  3. 🖊️ Have a Louisiana attorney-notary draft a procuration that expressly authorizes selling immovable property and, if there is a succession, accepting it.
  4. 🏦 Write to the mortgage servicer for the reinstatement and payoff figures, and check the clerk of court's suit index for any executory process petition.
  5. 💍 If the owners are married and cooperative, ask the attorney whether an article 2348 renunciation is simpler than a procuration.
  6. 🧾 Order a title search early so restitution liens or judgments surface before closing, not on closing day.

✅ Bottom Line

Louisiana lets an incarcerated owner sell, but its civil law asks for more ceremony than its neighbors: a procuration that expressly authorizes the sale, passed in authentic form before a notary and two witnesses, and both spouses' concurrence for community property. The prison's own staff notaries are limited by statute to department business, so plan for an outside notary from the start. Get that one signing right and every option opens up, including listing with an agent, which is often the better choice. Get it wrong while executory process is running and the sheriff decides the price.

❓ Frequently Asked Questions

What is a procuration in Louisiana?

It is the written document that creates a mandate, Louisiana's version of a power of attorney. Under Civil Code article 2996 it must expressly give the agent authority to sell or mortgage property, and under article 2993 it must be in the form the law requires for the act it authorizes.

Does a Louisiana procuration to sell a house need two witnesses?

The Civil Code's floor for transferring immovable property is a signed writing, but Louisiana closings are passed as authentic acts and title insurers generally require the procuration in authentic form too: signed before a notary and two witnesses under article 1833, then recorded in the parish conveyance records.

Can the notary who works at a Louisiana prison notarize a power of attorney to sell a house?

Often not. Department employees designated as ex officio notaries under R.S. 15:825.1 are limited to acts to which the Department of Corrections is a party and matters in which it has an interest. A family's procuration to sell a house usually falls outside that, so plan on an outside notary.

Does a Louisiana procuration stop working during a long prison sentence?

No. Under Civil Code article 3024 a mandate ends on the death of the principal or agent, the interdiction of the agent, or the qualification of a curator after the principal is interdicted, plus ordinary causes such as revocation. Incarceration alone does not end it, and no special durable wording is needed.

Can my spouse sell our community home while I am incarcerated in Louisiana?

Not alone. Article 2347 requires both spouses to concur in selling, mortgaging or leasing a community immovable. The incarcerated spouse can sign a procuration naming the other spouse, or under article 2348 expressly renounce the right to concur, irrevocably for up to three years.

Can a family have an incarcerated owner interdicted so a curator can sell the house?

Only if the owner has a medical condition that keeps him from consistently making reasoned decisions about his person and property. Under Civil Code article 389, interdiction is not available simply because someone is in prison or refuses to sign.

How fast is foreclosure in Louisiana by executory process?

Most Louisiana mortgages contain a confession of judgment, so the lender can get an order of seizure and sale on its papers within days of filing. A sheriff's sale commonly follows 75 to 120 days after the petition, after the federal 120-day delinquency wait, so roughly seven to eight months from the first missed payment.

Does Louisiana's homestead exemption protect a house from restitution or fines?

No. The creditor exemption in R.S. 20:1 shields up to $35,000 of a homestead's value, but it does not apply to mortgage debt, taxes, or any obligation arising from a conviction carrying possible imprisonment of six months or more.

Can an heir in prison block the sale of an inherited Louisiana house?

He can delay it but not stop it forever. His signature, or a procuration expressly allowing his agent to accept the succession and sell, is needed for a voluntary sale. If he refuses, any co-owner can ask the district court to partition the house by licitation, which is a court-ordered sale.

What does it cost each month to hold a vacant house in New Orleans?

On a house with a $200,000 as-is value and no homestead exemption, the 2026 east bank property tax of about 133.5 mills is roughly $222 a month. With vacant-home insurance, utilities and upkeep, plan on about $800 to $1,000 a month before any mortgage payment.
