Heirs Can't Agree on an Alabama House? Asking for a Sale Can Get You Bought Out Instead

Alabama law gives the relatives who want to keep a family house the right to buy out the ones who ask a court to sell it, at a price set by court-appointed appraisers, with only weeks to pay. That one rule changes how every Alabama heirs dispute should be handled. Here's how both buyout laws work, what the probate judge's office can do, what waiting costs, and when listing beats a cash offer. Or ask us a question, free.

Here is a scene that plays out in Alabama probate offices every week. Four siblings inherit their late father's brick house outside Montgomery. Two want it sold. One lives in it. One hasn't answered a text since the funeral. The sibling who most wants out calls a lawyer and says, "File the partition. Make them sell." What the lawyer should say next surprises most families: in Alabama, the moment you ask a court to sell the house for division, you also give your brothers and sisters the legal right to buy your share instead, at a price set by court-appointed appraisers.

That rule is older and broader than the heirs property law most articles talk about, and it changes the whole strategy. The heir who wants cash isn't really choosing between "sell" and "stay stuck." In Alabama, the realistic question is who ends up as the buyer: one of the siblings at an appraised number, the open market, or an investor buying a share. This guide is built around that question.

⚖️ In Alabama, Asking for a Sale Lets the Others Buy You Out

Alabama's partition chapter contains a short article, Ala. Code §§ 35-6-100 through 35-6-104, titled "Purchase of Interest of Joint Owner Filing for Partition." The core sentence is blunt. When anyone files a petition for a sale for division of property held by joint owners or tenants in common, "the court shall provide for the purchase of the interests" of the people who filed, by the other owners or any one of them. It applies to any co-owned property, not just inherited houses, and it is mandatory once a co-owner asks for it.

The mechanics are tight:

  • 📅 The co-owner who wants to buy must tell the court no later than 10 days before the trial date. The statute says they can do this even if a default has already been entered against them, so the silent sibling who ignored the lawsuit can still show up late and claim the buyout.
  • 🧮 If the family can't agree on a price, the court appoints one or more real estate appraisers or commissioners, who must report in writing within 30 days of their appointment (§ 35-6-101).
  • 💵 The buyers then have 30 days after the appraisal report to pay the price into court (§ 35-6-102). Once the court approves the payment, the clerk signs the deed transferring the filer's share.
  • 🚫 If they don't pay in time, the buyout fails and the case goes back to being an ordinary partition (§ 35-6-103).

Read that from both sides. If you are the heir who wants out, filing for a sale is, in practical terms, an offer to sell your share to your siblings at whatever the court's appraiser says the share is worth. If you are the heir who wants to keep the house, you have a statutory right to buy, but only if you can raise the money within a 30-day window. A sibling who "wants to keep Mama's house" but has no cash and no mortgage approval holds a right she can't use.

⚠️ The warning that matters most: before anyone in an Alabama family files for a sale, get an appraisal and decide whether you would be content to be bought out at that number. If the answer is no, a lawsuit is the wrong first move. And if you are the one who wants to keep the house, start the loan or cash conversation now. Thirty days after the appraisers report is not long enough to begin.

🧾 Two Buyout Laws Can Reach the Same House

In 2014 the Legislature passed the Alabama Uniform Partition of Heirs Property Act, Ala. Code § 35-6A-1 and following, which has been in effect since 2015. It covers "heirs property," which the statute defines narrowly. The house must be held as tenants in common, there can be no written agreement binding all the owners about partition, at least one owner must have gotten title from a relative, and one of three 20 percent tests must be met: 20 percent or more of the interests held by relatives, 20 percent or more held by someone who inherited from a relative, or 20 percent or more of the owners being relatives. A house left to siblings by a parent almost always qualifies.

When it does, the court must determine the fair market value of the whole property before anything else, by agreement, by appraisal or otherwise. Then the Alabama version of the buyout runs on its own clock. Under § 35-6A-7, the other owners have 30 days after the court's notice of value to elect to buy the interests of everyone who asked for a sale. That is shorter than the 45 days in the national model act, a difference worth knowing if your lawyer is reading a summary written for another state. The court then sets a payment date at least 60 days after the election period ends. If nobody elects or nobody pays, the court moves on to decide between dividing the land and selling it, weighing factors such as the family's ties to the property and whether a sale would cost someone their home.

What matters for a family deciding what to do is the common thread: Alabama law keeps pushing a disputed house toward a family buyout at an appraised price before it lets a stranger buy it at auction. That's good news for the sibling who wants to keep the house and has the money. For everyone else, it sets the ceiling on what a lawsuit can get you, which is your share of the appraised value, minus your share of the costs, months from now.

🏛️ The Probate Judge's Office Is Where Most of This Starts

Alabama's probate courts are unusual. Each county has an elected judge of probate, and the same office records deeds, handles marriage licenses and, in many counties, runs a large share of county administration. When you pull the deed to the family house, you are usually pulling it from the same office that will open the estate. That makes the probate office the cheapest place to start untangling a dispute: you can see who is on the deed, whether a will was ever filed, and whether an estate was opened years ago and never closed.

The Code of Alabama also has a separate article for partition in probate court, alongside partition in circuit court, so a sale for division doesn't always require a circuit court lawsuit. And inside an open estate there is a third route. Under § 43-2-443, the probate court handling the estate may order the decedent's land sold when it cannot be equitably divided among the heirs or devisees and an adult heir or devisee files written consent to the sale. A single house can rarely be split fairly among four people, so in many estates this is the most direct court path to a sale. Your attorney will choose among these; the point for you is that "going to court" in Alabama often means going to the probate judge, not starting a full-blown civil suit.

🪪 The House Already Belongs to the Heirs, Not to "the Estate"

Many family fights rest on a wrong assumption: that the house sits in limbo until someone "probates" it. Under § 43-2-830, an Alabama decedent's real property passes at death directly to the people named in the will or, with no will, to the heirs. Personal property goes to the personal representative; land does not. The heirs' ownership is subject to the family's statutory allowances, creditors' claims and the estate's administration, but from the day of death each heir holds an undivided share.

Two consequences follow. First, the sibling living in the house is not a trespasser. She owns part of it, and the others can't simply change the locks. Second, nobody can sell the whole house without either every owner's signature, a personal representative with power to sell, or a court order. Who those owners are depends on what the parent left behind:

  • 📜 A will that names who gets the house. It must be filed for probate within five years of death or it isn't effective (§ 43-8-161). Families who leave a will in a drawer for six years can find the house passing as if there were no will at all.
  • 💍 No will, with a surviving spouse. Under § 43-8-41, if all the children are also the spouse's children, the spouse takes the first $50,000 in value plus half of the rest. If any child is from another relationship, the spouse takes half. With no children but a surviving parent, the spouse takes the first $100,000 plus half the balance.
  • 👨‍👩‍👧 No will, no spouse. The children share equally, and a child who died earlier is represented by his or her own children. This is how one house ends up with nine owners in two generations.

Alabama also gives a surviving spouse a $15,000 homestead allowance under § 43-8-110, and if there's no spouse, the minor and dependent children divide that $15,000. Those allowances come ahead of the heirs' shares, so a stepmother or a dependent grandchild can have a claim that has to be paid out of a sale before siblings split anything. If you don't know who the legal heirs are, start with our guide to what happens to a house when there's no will, then have an Alabama attorney confirm the list.

🧍 Three Holdouts, Three Different Fixes

The sibling who lives there and won't leave

As a co-owner, the occupying heir can't be evicted like a tenant. But she also can't stop a sale for division, and in Alabama she has the clearest buyout right of anyone if she can finance it. Treat the appraisal as the opening offer. Ask her lender, or a local credit union, whether she can qualify for a loan big enough to pay the others their appraised shares. If she can, a negotiated buyout using the court's numbers (without the court) is usually the cheapest outcome for everyone. If she can't, the buyout right is a bluff, and an attorney's letter explaining the 30-day payment window often ends the standoff without a trial.

The heir who won't answer

Silence doesn't protect a co-owner in Alabama. A partition or probate sale proceeds after proper service, including service by publication when someone truly can't be found, and the court can take steps to protect an absent heir's share of the money. The practical cost is time: locating and serving an out-of-state cousin can add months. Do the search first. Obituaries, social media and a skip-trace often turn up the "missing" heir in an afternoon, and a signed deed from a cooperative relative is worth far more than a court finding about an absent one.

The heir who thinks the house is worth more

This is the most common Alabama standoff, and the statutes help settle it, because both buyout procedures replace opinion with a court-ordered appraisal. Agree on one licensed Alabama appraiser now, split the fee, and agree in writing that everyone will treat that number as the value for a buyout. You will have recreated the result of a lawsuit for a few hundred dollars.

🛤️ Choosing a Path

🤝 Negotiate a buyout using an agreed appraisal

Best first move

Use the statutes as leverage rather than as a lawsuit. Everyone knows that if this goes to court, the likely result is a buyout at an appraised price. Agreeing to that result now saves each side thousands of dollars and most of a year.

🏡 List the house on the open market

Best price if everyone signs

If the house is in decent shape and every owner (or a personal representative with power to sell) will sign, a listing with a local agent usually puts the most money in everyone's pocket. Alabama's low property taxes make it cheaper than in most states to carry a house for the few months a listing takes.

⚖️ File, knowing you may be bought out

When talks have failed

If one heir is blocking everything and nobody can afford a buyout, filing is legitimate. Just file expecting that the other side may claim the buyout, and be ready to accept your appraised share.

💼 Sell your share or the whole house for cash

When time or condition rules

An investor can buy one heir's undivided share and step into the dispute, or buy the whole house as-is when everyone is willing but nobody wants to fix it. You give up price for speed and certainty. That trade only makes sense when the house needs major work, someone needs the money soon, or the family relationship is costing more than the discount.

💵 What Waiting Costs on an Alabama House

Alabama's property taxes are the lowest in the country per person, and that leads many families to think waiting is free. It isn't, and there's an Alabama-specific trap. Owner-occupied single-family homes are Class III property, assessed at 10 percent of appraised value. A house nobody lives in as an owner can be reclassified as Class II and assessed at 20 percent, which doubles the tax bill. Tell the revenue commissioner or tax assessor promptly about the death and ask how the house will be classified.

Take a house in the Montgomery or Mobile area with a repaired value of $200,000. Using an illustrative combined rate of 50 mills (check your own county's millage on the tax bill):

  • 🏷️ Property tax: at Class III, $200,000 × 10% × 0.050 = $1,000 a year, about $83 a month. At Class II it becomes about $167 a month.
  • 🛡️ Vacant-house insurance: commonly $100–$250 a month in Alabama, more near the Gulf where wind coverage is expensive.
  • 💡 Utilities and lawn: $150–$250 a month to keep power on, the lawn cut and the city from citing the property.
  • 🔧 Upkeep: humidity, termites and roof leaks punish empty Alabama houses; budget $100–$200 a month for a vacant house.

That's roughly $450–$900 a month, or $5,400–$10,800 a year, before legal fees. A contested partition at $10,000–$30,000 can take a third of a modest house's equity.

What a cash buyer would pay, worked out. Cash buyers generally offer about 60–80 percent of repaired value, depending on condition and complications. On that $200,000 house needing $25,000 of roof and HVAC work, an offer might land around $130,000–$150,000. Split four ways, that's $32,500–$37,500 each. Compare a listing: if the house sells for $185,000 as-is, minus 6 percent commission and about $4,000 in closing costs, each sibling nets about $42,500 after a few months. Compare a buyout: if the appraisers value the house at $180,000, a sibling who files and gets bought out receives $45,000, but only after paying a share of fees and waiting months. On a sound house with willing heirs, the cash offer loses. It wins only when the repairs are heavy, someone needs out quickly, or the fight is costing more than the gap.

🌾 Old Family Land in the Black Belt and Rural Counties

Some Alabama disputes aren't about a single house left by a parent. They involve land that has passed without probate for three or four generations, especially across the Black Belt counties, where heirs property has long been tied to land loss. By the time anyone tries to sell, there may be dozens of owners holding tiny fractions, some of whom have never seen the place. The heirs property act was written with these families in mind, and it gives relatives who want to keep the land real protection against a quick sale to an outsider.

Two practical points matter here. First, the property tax: if one relative has been quietly paying it for years, record that, because it may matter when the court settles accounts between owners. If nobody has paid, check with the county now. When a third party buys a tax lien at an Alabama tax sale, the owners generally have three years from the sale to redeem it. Second, timber and mineral rights: on rural tracts, the land may be worth more than the house on it, so get a forester's or land appraiser's opinion before agreeing to any number.

Alabama Cooperative Extension publishes plain-language material on heirs property and partition, and legal aid organizations in the state help some families clear title. If your situation looks like this, those resources are a better first call than any buyer, including us.

📋 What To Do This Week

  1. Pull the deed and check for a will at the county probate office. Note whether an estate was ever opened.
  2. List every heir under the will or under § 43-8-41 and § 43-8-42, including children of any heir who has died.
  3. Call the revenue commissioner about the tax status and classification so the bill doesn't double unnoticed.
  4. Insure the house as vacant if no one lives there.
  5. Order one appraisal everyone agrees to accept, and share it with all the heirs.
  6. Ask the sibling who wants to keep it whether they can finance a buyout at that number, and by when.
  7. Talk to an Alabama probate or real estate attorney before anyone files, so the filer knows they could be bought out.

✅ Bottom Line

Alabama treats a family fight over a house as a buyout problem first. Ask the court for a sale, and your siblings can buy your share at an appraised price; qualify as heirs property, and the court values the house and offers the family a buyout window before any sale. Use that to your advantage outside the courtroom: get one appraisal, find out who can actually pay, and settle on those numbers. List the house if everyone will sign and it's in good shape. Bring in a cash buyer only when repairs, deadlines or a broken relationship make speed worth more than the last dollar.

❓ Frequently Asked Questions

If I file for a partition sale in Alabama, can my siblings buy me out instead?

Yes. Under Ala. Code § 35-6-100, when a co-owner files for a sale for division, the court must provide for the other co-owners to buy the filer's interest. They must notify the court at least 10 days before trial. If the price isn't agreed, court-appointed appraisers set it, and the buyers have 30 days after the appraisal report to pay it into court.

How is the buyout price set in an Alabama heirs dispute?

By agreement if the family can reach one. Otherwise, under the older partition buyout statute, the court appoints real estate appraisers or commissioners who report within 30 days. Under the Alabama Uniform Partition of Heirs Property Act, the court determines the value of the whole property first and each owner's share is based on that value.

What counts as heirs property under Alabama law?

Property held as tenants in common with no written agreement among all owners about partition, where at least one owner got title from a relative, and where 20 percent or more of the interests are held by relatives, 20 percent or more are held by someone who inherited from a relative, or 20 percent or more of the owners are relatives. A house left to several siblings usually qualifies.

How long do Alabama heirs have to elect a buyout under the heirs property act?

Thirty days after the court sends notice of the property's value, under Ala. Code § 35-6A-7. That is shorter than the 45 days in the national model act. The court then sets a payment deadline at least 60 days after the election period ends.

Does an inherited Alabama house have to go through probate before the heirs own it?

No. Under Ala. Code § 43-2-830, real property passes at death directly to the people named in the will or to the heirs, subject to family allowances, creditors and administration. But selling the whole house still takes every owner's signature, a personal representative with power to sell, or a court order.

Can the Alabama probate court order an inherited house sold?

Yes. Under Ala. Code § 43-2-443, the probate court handling the estate may order land sold for division when it cannot be equitably divided among the heirs or devisees and an adult heir or devisee files written consent. Alabama law also allows partition proceedings in probate court as well as circuit court.

Is there a deadline to probate a will in Alabama?

Yes. Under Ala. Code § 43-8-161, a will is not effective unless it is filed for probate within five years of the death. If a will sits unfiled longer than that, the house may pass under the intestacy rules instead of the will.

How much does a surviving spouse inherit in Alabama if there is no will?

Under Ala. Code § 43-8-41, if all the children are also the spouse's, the spouse takes the first $50,000 in value plus half the rest. If any child is from another relationship, the spouse takes half. With no children but a surviving parent, the spouse takes the first $100,000 plus half. A surviving spouse also has a $15,000 homestead allowance under § 43-8-110.

Will property taxes go up on an inherited Alabama house nobody lives in?

They can. Owner-occupied homes are Class III property assessed at 10 percent of appraised value. A house that is no longer owner-occupied can be classified as Class II and assessed at 20 percent, which doubles the bill. Contact the county revenue commissioner or tax assessor after the death to confirm the classification.

When should Alabama heirs list the house instead of taking a cash offer?

When the house is in reasonable shape and every owner, or a personal representative with power to sell, will sign a listing. An open-market sale usually nets the family more. A cash sale makes sense when repairs are heavy, someone needs out quickly, no one can afford a buyout, or the dispute is costing more than the discount.
