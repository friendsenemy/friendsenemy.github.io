Selling a Florida House From Jail or Prison: The Two-Witness Rule Comes First

Being incarcerated doesn't take away the right to sell a house in Florida. What makes it hard is paperwork Florida is unusually strict about: a power of attorney needs two witnesses and a notary, and a deed needs two witnesses whose addresses now have to appear on it. Inside a Department of Corrections facility or a county jail, lining that up is the real project. Here's how families get it done.

The phone call usually comes from a mother, a sister, or a spouse. The owner is serving a sentence somewhere in the Florida Department of Corrections system, or sitting in a county jail awaiting trial, and the house is a problem: empty, behind on taxes, maybe with a mortgage nobody is paying. The family's first question is whether it can even be sold. It can. Incarceration doesn't remove ownership or the right to convey it.

The real question is how to get valid signatures from someone who can't walk into a title company. In Florida, that question has a specific answer, and it starts with witnesses.

✍️ Florida's Witness Rules Are the Real Obstacle From Inside

Florida layers witness requirements on top of notarization for the two documents that matter most here:

  • 📜 A power of attorney must be signed by the principal and by two subscribing witnesses, and acknowledged before a notary public (Fla. Stat. § 709.2105). A POA signed in front of a notary alone doesn't meet Florida's standard.
  • 🏠 A deed conveying Florida real estate must be signed in the presence of two subscribing witnesses under Florida's conveyance statute, and acknowledged before a notary to be recorded.
  • 📍 Witness addresses. Since January 1, 2024, Fla. Stat. § 695.26 requires each witness's post office address to appear on deeds and mortgages offered for recording. Documents signed and acknowledged outside Florida are exempt from that address requirement.

Outside, none of this is hard; a title company has notaries and witnesses down the hall. Inside a prison or jail, you need two people who are allowed to be in the room, willing to sign as witnesses, and able to print their mailing addresses, plus a notary with authority to notarize in that setting. Many failed attempts come down to a document that was notarized but had one witness, or none.

⚠️ Check the witness lines before anything is mailed in. A Florida power of attorney or deed missing a witness signature can be rejected by the title company or the county recorder, and every round trip through prison mail costs weeks. Have a Florida attorney or the closing title company prepare the documents, with the witness and address blocks already laid out, before anything goes into the facility.

🏢 How Notarizing and Witnessing Work in an FDC Facility or County Jail

🏛️ State prisons

Florida Department of Corrections rules on legal documents and legal mail (Fla. Admin. Code R. 33-210.102) provide for each warden to designate one or more employees as notaries for inmates' legal documents. The notary is supposed to confirm that the inmate has read and understands the document, notarize it, and send it out promptly. Access usually runs through the inmate's classification officer or the law library; the inmate requests the service, and the facility schedules it.

Witnesses are the harder part. Ask the facility, through the classification officer or the attorney, who can serve as witnesses and whether staff will sign. Some facilities allow staff witnesses; in others, the practical route is an attorney visit or legal visit where the attorney and a staff member witness the signing. Policies differ by facility, and they change, so confirm before you schedule.

🚔 County jails

County jails, from Duval to Orange to Miami-Dade, set their own procedures. Some have staff notaries on a schedule; others only allow an outside mobile notary during a professional visit. Call the jail's records or inmate services office, explain that you need a power of attorney or deed notarized with two witnesses, and ask exactly how it's done there.

💻 Why remote notarization rarely solves it

Florida allows remote online notarization, but its rules on remote witnessing are strict, and under Fla. Stat. § 117.285 a remote witness isn't effective when the signer is a vulnerable adult. More practically, most facilities don't give inmates the private video access and identity verification the process requires. Plan on an in-person signing.

✈️ Federal custody

If the owner is held in a federal facility, whether in Florida or out of state, federal prisons have their own procedures for legal documents. A document signed and acknowledged outside Florida is exempt from the witness-address rule, but Florida's two-witness requirements for the deed and the POA still matter for a Florida title company. Coordinate with the closing agent before the signing.

📜 A Florida Power of Attorney a Title Company Will Accept

Most families end up using a power of attorney, so that a trusted person outside can list the house, sign the contract, and sign the deed at closing. To be useful, the POA needs a few things beyond correct execution:

  • 🔍 Specific real estate authority. Florida's POA act doesn't let general "do all acts" language stand in for specific powers. Name the authority to sell real property, and ideally identify the property by its legal description.
  • 🧾 A recordable original. When an agent signs a deed, the title company will usually want the original POA recorded with the deed in the county where the house is. Florida's statute recognizes that recording may be required when the POA is used to affect title.
  • ⏳ Durability. Florida's grounds for ending a POA, in § 709.2109, include death, revocation, and certain incapacity findings, but not incarceration. A durable POA keeps working through a long sentence.
  • 🤝 An agent who will actually do the work. The agent signs listing agreements, answers the buyer's questions, deals with utility shutoffs, and handles closing. Choose someone organized and trustworthy, and remember they owe the owner a duty to act in the owner's interest.

If the owner would rather keep control, they can sign the purchase contract and the deed themselves inside the facility, with the same witness and notary requirements. That works for a single transaction but means two or more signing sessions, so a POA is usually faster. Our national guide on selling a house while incarcerated covers the general approach.

🏠 Married? Florida's Homestead Joinder Rule Comes Next

Under Article X, Section 4(c) of the Florida Constitution, an owner who is married can sell or mortgage homestead property only if joined by the spouse. That applies even when the spouse's name isn't on the deed. If the incarcerated owner is married and the house is their homestead, the spouse has to sign the deed too.

The POA statute reinforces this. Under Fla. Stat. § 709.2201(2)(b), an agent can't mortgage or convey homestead property without the joinder of the principal's spouse or the spouse's guardian. The spouse can join through their own power of attorney, and the statute allows either spouse to appoint the other as agent. In practice that means:

  • 👫 If the spouse is outside and cooperative, the spouse signs the deed as spouse, and the incarcerated owner signs either directly or through an agent under a POA.
  • 🔁 If the incarcerated owner signs a POA naming the spouse as agent, the spouse can sign for the owner and also join in their own right.
  • 🧱 If the spouse won't cooperate, a homestead sale stalls. That may require a divorce settlement or court order, and it's a conversation for a Florida attorney. Our guide to selling a house during a divorce explains how courts handle that.

If the owner isn't married, the joinder rule doesn't apply, but homestead can still affect who inherits if the owner dies before the sale, which is another reason families move quickly.

🧾 Does the House Stay "Homestead" While the Owner Is Incarcerated?

Florida uses the word homestead for two different things, and incarceration affects them differently.

🛡️ Protection from forced sale

The constitutional homestead shields a residence (up to half an acre inside a city, 160 acres outside) from most creditors, regardless of value. Florida attorneys generally explain that involuntary absence, including incarceration, isn't treated as abandoning homestead when the owner intends to return, and that the home keeps its character if the owner's spouse or children still live there. Facts matter here, especially for a long sentence and an empty house.

💲 The property tax exemption

The tax exemption under Fla. Stat. § 196.031, which removes up to $50,000 of assessed value, requires the owner to make the home their permanent residence, or that of their dependents. Physical presence isn't always required, but renting the house out can end it: under § 196.061, renting all or substantially all of a homestead for more than 30 days per calendar year for two consecutive years is treated as abandonment. Families who rent the house while the owner is away should know that.

Keeping an exemption you're not entitled to is expensive. Under § 196.161, the county can assess back taxes for up to ten years, a 50% penalty per year, and 15% annual interest, and record it as a lien. If you're unsure whether the house still qualifies, ask the county property appraiser before it becomes a problem, and remember that a Save Our Homes cap lost with the exemption usually means a much larger tax bill the following year.

💳 Liens That Surface at Closing

The title search on a house owned by someone who's incarcerated often finds more than a mortgage. Common items:

  • ⚖️ Civil restitution liens. Florida's civil restitution lien statute (§ 960.29) allows liens against an offender's real and personal property.
  • 🏛️ Incarceration cost liens. Under § 960.293, the state can seek a lien for the cost of incarceration; for non-capital, non-life felonies the statute sets $50 per day of the sentence.
  • 📑 Court costs, fines, and child support judgments recorded in the county's official records.
  • 💸 Delinquent property taxes. Unpaid Florida taxes become tax certificates, and two years after April 1 of the year a certificate was issued, the holder can apply for a tax deed under § 197.502.

Whether a given lien attaches to a homestead, and what has to be paid at closing, is a question for the title company and a Florida attorney. What matters for planning is that these show up in the title search, so order it early. A liened house can still be sold; the liens are usually paid from the proceeds or negotiated with the lienholder before closing.

🏡 List with an agent under the POA

Usually best for a sound house with time

If the house is in good shape, the agent is organized, and there's no tax deed or foreclosure deadline, listing almost always nets more than a cash sale. A buyer's lender won't care that the seller is incarcerated, as long as the POA and deed are valid.

💵 Sell for cash

Best when the house needs work or a deadline is close

When the house has a roof at the end of its life, storm damage, or years of deferred maintenance; when a mortgage foreclosure or tax deed application is coming; or when there's nobody outside who can manage showings and repairs, a cash buyer who can close on whatever schedule the signing allows is often the practical choice.

🧩 Sell just the share

Best when the owner is one of several heirs

If the incarcerated person inherited a share of a family house and the others want to keep it, the owner can sell their share alone. It brings less than a proportional share of the whole, but it turns an illiquid interest into money without forcing a family fight.

💵 The Numbers: A Jacksonville Example

Cash buyers, including us, generally pay about 60% to 80% of what a house would be worth fully repaired, lower in that range the more work it needs. Take a 1960s block house on the Westside of Jacksonville worth about $260,000 repaired. It sat empty for a year; it needs a roof, AC, and interior repairs, about $50,000. The owner is single, serving a five-year sentence, and owes $85,000 on a mortgage that's four months behind.

  • 💵 Cash sale: with that much work, an offer near $160,000 to $180,000 is realistic. After the mortgage payoff, arrears, and seller closing costs, including Florida documentary stamp tax of $0.70 per $100 on the deed (about $1,200 on $170,000), the owner nets roughly $70,000 to $85,000.
  • 🏡 Listing as-is: maybe $205,000 to an investor or a buyer with a renovation loan, if the roof passes their insurer's inspection. After a 5% to 6% commission and closing costs, about $100,000 to the owner, but it takes longer and depends on someone outside managing showings and inspections.
  • 🔥 Carrying costs: Florida insurance is expensive, and vacant-dwelling coverage often costs $150 to $400 a month when you can find it. Add property tax (higher once the homestead exemption ends), utilities, lawn care, and accruing mortgage interest, and holding this house costs $900 to $1,400 a month.

On those numbers, if a reliable agent under a POA can manage a listing over a few months, listing nets more. If no one can, or the lender is about to file foreclosure, the cash sale may protect more of the equity. In Florida, mortgage foreclosure has to go through the courts (§ 702.01), which buys time, but every month adds fees to the payoff.

⚖️ When the Inmate Owns Only a Share

Many of these houses are inherited and co-owned with siblings or a parent. Florida's partition statute (Chapter 64) lets any co-owner ask the court to divide the property or, where it can't be divided, order it sold. For inherited family property, Florida also adopted the Uniform Partition of Heirs Property Act (§§ 64.201 to 64.214) for cases filed on or after July 1, 2020, which gives the other co-owners a chance to buy out a share at a court-determined value before any sale (§ 64.207). Because a partition case is litigation, an incarcerated co-owner almost always needs counsel to participate. Our partition lawsuit guide and our guide to whether a co-owner can sell without the others explain the basics.

📋 What To Do This Week

  1. Confirm exactly where the owner is held and how long they're likely to be there, since a short jail stay and a long state sentence call for different plans.
  2. Pull the deed and a tax status from the county property appraiser and tax collector, and note whether the homestead exemption is on the house.
  3. Find out whether the owner is married. If so, plan for spousal joinder.
  4. Have a Florida attorney or title company draft the POA or deed, with two witness lines, witness address blocks, and a notary acknowledgment.
  5. Call the facility (classification officer for state prisons, inmate services for county jails) and ask how notarization and witnessing are scheduled.
  6. Order a title search so liens, restitution, and back taxes are known before you choose a route.
  7. Protect the house: insurance, locks, utilities, and someone checking on it every week.

✅ Bottom Line

An incarcerated owner can sell a Florida house. The legal right isn't the problem; the paperwork is. Florida's two-witness requirements for a power of attorney and for a deed, the witness-address rule, and the homestead joinder rule for married owners are where most attempts stall. Get the documents drafted by someone who does Florida closings, confirm the facility's notary and witness procedure first, and order the title search early so the liens don't surprise you.

If there's a capable family member outside, a sound house, and no deadline, list it under a POA and let the market pay full price. If the house needs heavy work, a foreclosure or tax deed is close, or nobody can manage a listing from outside, a cash sale timed to the signing is often the cleaner path. ❓ Frequently Asked Questions

How many witnesses does a Florida power of attorney need?

Two. Under Fla. Stat. 709.2105, a Florida power of attorney must be signed by the principal and by two subscribing witnesses and be acknowledged before a notary public.

Does a Florida deed need witnesses if the owner signs it in prison?

Yes. A deed conveying Florida real estate must be signed in front of two witnesses and acknowledged before a notary to be recorded. Since January 1, 2024, each witness's post office address must also appear on the deed, unless the deed is signed and acknowledged outside Florida.

How does an inmate get documents notarized in a Florida Department of Corrections facility?

Florida Department of Corrections rules provide for wardens to designate employee notaries for inmates' legal documents. The inmate usually requests the service through classification or the law library. Ask the facility in advance how witnesses are handled, because the notary alone isn't enough for a Florida deed or power of attorney.

Does going to prison end a Florida power of attorney?

No. Florida's termination grounds for a power of attorney include the principal's death, revocation, and certain findings of incapacity, but not incarceration. A durable power of attorney stays effective during a sentence.

Can a POA agent sell a married inmate's Florida homestead alone?

No. Under Fla. Stat. 709.2201(2)(b), an agent can't convey homestead property without the joinder of the principal's spouse or the spouse's guardian. The spouse can join directly or through their own power of attorney, and either spouse may appoint the other as agent.

Does my spouse have to sign if the house is only in my name and I'm incarcerated?

If you're married and the house is your Florida homestead, yes. Article X, Section 4(c) of the Florida Constitution requires the spouse to join in a sale or mortgage of homestead property even when the spouse isn't on the title.

Will I lose my Florida homestead exemption while I'm incarcerated?

Not automatically, but it depends on the facts. The exemption requires the home to be your permanent residence or that of your dependents. Renting it out for more than 30 days a year for two consecutive years is treated as abandonment, and keeping an exemption you don't qualify for can bring up to ten years of back taxes, penalties, and interest.

Can restitution or incarceration-cost liens be paid out of a Florida house sale?

They often are. Florida allows civil restitution liens and liens for the cost of incarceration, which can show up in the title search. How they affect a homestead and what has to be paid at closing is a question for the title company and a Florida attorney.

Can an incarcerated heir sell just their share of an inherited Florida house?

Yes. A co-owner can deed their own share without the others' consent. For inherited property, Florida's Uniform Partition of Heirs Property Act also gives the other co-owners a chance to buy out a share in a partition case filed on or after July 1, 2020.

Is listing or selling for cash better when the Florida owner is in prison?

If the house is in good condition, there's no deadline, and a trusted agent outside can manage the listing under a power of attorney, listing usually nets more. A cash sale makes more sense when the house needs major work, a foreclosure or tax deed is close, or no one outside can handle showings and repairs.
