Heirs Can't Agree on an Ohio House? In Ohio, the Appraisal Is What You're Really Fighting Over

Ohio gives a family several ways to settle an inherited house, and nearly all of them run through one number: the appraised value in the estate inventory. Heirs who understand that number argue less and sell faster. Here is how the consent sale, a probate land-sale case, the spouse's purchase right and partition actually work in Ohio, and when listing beats a cash offer. Or ask us a question, free.

By Dustin Ray, Founder of Pages of Purpose LLC. Not legal advice. Ohio probate and partition procedure is technical, county probate courts add their own local rules, and laws differ by state. Before anyone files in probate court, signs a consent, or records a deed, talk to an attorney licensed in Ohio, where the property is.

Here is a situation we hear from Ohio families every month. Dad died in Akron or Toledo or a Columbus suburb. The house is in his name alone. One sibling is the executor and wants to sell. Another sibling thinks the house is worth far more than the appraiser said and refuses to sign anything until the number goes up. A third lives in another state and just wants it over with.

That second sibling has put their finger on the thing that actually controls inherited houses in Ohio. The state's probate and partition statutes return again and again to one figure: the appraised value. It sets the floor for a quick sale with everyone's consent, the price a surviving spouse pays to keep the house, the price a co-owner pays to buy out the others in partition, and the minimum a sheriff's auction can accept. If your family can settle the value, the rest usually follows. If it can't, Ohio has a court process for each step, and each one costs months.

📐 Ohio Ties Nearly Every Exit to the Appraised Value

Most states let an executor sell for whatever a buyer will pay, subject to a general duty to get a fair price. Ohio is more specific. Once an estate is opened in the probate division of the court of common pleas (every county has one at the county seat under R.C. 2101.01), the executor or administrator files an inventory, and real estate in that inventory carries an appraised value. That appraisal then shows up in four separate rules:

  • 🖊️ The consent sale. Under R.C. 2127.011, the fiduciary can sell without a court land-sale case if the spouse and all heirs or beneficiaries consent in writing, but the price must be at least 80% of the appraised value.
  • 🏠 The spouse's purchase right. Under R.C. 2106.16, a surviving spouse can buy the "mansion house" at its appraised value, even while serving as executor, unless the will specifically gave the house to someone else.
  • ⚖️ The partition election. Under R.C. 5307.09, when court-appointed commissioners find a house can't be divided, any co-owner can elect to take it at the commissioners' appraised value by paying the others their shares.
  • 🔨 The auction floor. Under R.C. 5307.12, a partition sale cannot go for less than two-thirds of the value the commissioners returned.

So the argument "the appraiser lowballed it" is not just a family grievance in Ohio. It shifts real dollars between siblings. A low appraisal makes a spouse's purchase cheap and lowers the consent-sale floor. A high appraisal protects the heirs who aren't keeping the house but can make a buyout unaffordable and a consent sale impossible if the market won't pay it. That is why the first fight we usually see in a stuck Ohio estate is about the inventory, not about selling.

The warning that matters most: objections to the inventory and its appraisal have a short window in Ohio probate courts, and the spouse's petition to buy the mansion house must be filed within one month after the inventory is approved. If you think the appraisal is wrong, raise it with the court promptly through an Ohio attorney. Waiting until a sale is on the table is usually too late to change the number everyone else is relying on.

✍️ The Consent Sale: One Form, Every Signature

When the heirs mostly agree, R.C. 2127.011 is the cleanest way to sell an Ohio estate house. The executor or administrator finds a buyer, the surviving spouse and every heir (or every beneficiary under the will) sign a written consent, the consents are filed with the probate court, and the sale closes without a lawsuit. A title company will want to see the filed consents and the fiduciary's letters of authority.

Three limits matter:

  1. The 80% floor. If the house was appraised at $180,000, the consent route can't be used for an offer below $144,000. A house that needs a roof, a furnace and a cleanout may not fetch 80% of an appraisal that assumed it was livable. Then the family either asks for a re-appraisal or uses a court sale.
  2. No minors. If any heir or beneficiary whose consent is needed is a minor, the consent route is not available. A grandchild who inherits through a parent who died earlier can push the whole sale into court.
  3. The will can forbid it. A will that expressly prohibits the procedure takes it off the table. A will that gives the executor a power of sale works differently: under R.C. 2127.01, the executor can then sell under the will itself without consents or a land-sale case.

Notice what the consent route does not require: it does not require the heirs to agree to anything except this particular sale at this particular price. Families who are deadlocked over who "deserves" the house sometimes find they can agree on a buyer and an amount, and leave the rest of the argument for the distribution.

🏛️ When One Heir Won't Sign: The Probate Court Land-Sale Case

If the will has no power of sale and someone refuses to consent, the fiduciary can still sell, but only through a land-sale proceeding under R.C. Chapter 2127. In practice that means filing a complaint in the probate court, naming every heir, the spouse and any mortgage holder or lienholder as parties, serving them, and asking the court to authorize the sale. The court decides whether a sale is needed (to pay debts and costs, or because it is in the estate's best interest), sets terms, and later confirms the sale before the deed is delivered.

For a family in conflict, the land-sale case has advantages and costs:

  • ✅ One heir can't veto it. The objecting sibling gets notice and a chance to be heard, but not a blocking vote.
  • ✅ Liens get sorted. Mortgages, tax liens and judgment creditors are parties, so the buyer receives clean title on confirmation.
  • ⏳ It adds months. Service on every party, an answer period, any hearing, an appraisal or sale terms, and a confirmation entry commonly stretch the timeline by four to nine months, longer if someone appeals or can't be located.
  • 💸 It adds cost. Attorney fees for a contested land sale often run several thousand dollars beyond ordinary administration, and those fees come out of the estate, meaning out of every heir's share, including the share of the heir who forced the case.

We tell objecting siblings the same thing their siblings' lawyer will: in Ohio, refusing to sign the consent rarely stops a sale. It mostly changes how long the sale takes and how much of everyone's inheritance goes to process.

🏠 The Surviving Spouse Goes First in Ohio

Many Ohio heir disputes are really disputes between a stepparent and the children from a first marriage. Ohio gives the surviving spouse several rights that come ahead of the children's wishes:

  • 🛏️ One year in the house, free. R.C. 2106.15 lets the spouse remain in the mansion house for one year after death without paying rent. If the house must be sold sooner to pay debts, the spouse is compensated for the fair rental value of the rest of that year.
  • 🏷️ The right to buy it at appraisal. R.C. 2106.16 lets the spouse purchase the house at its appraised value, unless it was specifically devised. The petition is due no later than one month after the inventory is approved.
  • 💵 A $40,000 support allowance. R.C. 2106.13 sets aside up to $40,000 for the spouse (and minor children) ahead of most other claims, which can leave less cash in the estate to cover expenses and make a sale of the house more likely.
  • 📏 A large intestate share. Under R.C. 2105.06, if there's no will and all of the children are also the spouse's children, the spouse takes everything. If the decedent had a child who is not the spouse's, the spouse takes a fixed amount ($20,000 or $60,000 depending on the family) plus one-half or one-third of the rest.

Put together, a stepchild who wants the house sold and the proceeds split is often in a weak position against a surviving spouse who wants to stay, at least for the first year and through the purchase-right deadline. On the other hand, a spouse who wants to keep the house has to come up with the appraised value (less the spouse's own share) and may need a mortgage to do it. When the spouse can't finance it, the family usually ends up selling anyway.

📜 No Estate Opened? The Certificate of Transfer and What It Leaves You Holding

Plenty of Ohio houses never go through a full sale inside the estate. The more common pattern is that the fiduciary applies for a certificate of transfer under R.C. 2113.61 after the inventory is filed. The court issues it within days, it names each heir or beneficiary and the interest each receives in each parcel, and it is recorded with the county recorder. If no estate was opened at all, an heir can apply for one. Small estates can also qualify for release from administration under R.C. 2113.03 (assets of $35,000 or less, or $100,000 or less when everything passes to the spouse), and the court can appoint a commissioner to convey real estate in those cases.

The certificate solves the title problem and creates a people problem. Once it is recorded, the siblings own the house together, each with a fractional share, and Ohio's estate-level shortcuts stop applying. No more consent form, no more fiduciary who can sell for everyone. From that point a sale needs every co-owner's signature on the deed, or a partition case.

Two more Ohio details change this picture. First, a parent who recorded a transfer on death designation affidavit under R.C. 5302.22 before death passed the house straight to the named beneficiaries without probate, so the heirs may already be co-owners with no estate involved at all. Second, Ohio's Medicaid estate recovery statute, R.C. 5162.21, defines the estate broadly enough to reach property that passed outside probate, including by survivorship or "other arrangement." If a parent was on Medicaid at 55 or older or in a nursing home, check for a recovery claim before treating the house as free and clear. Recovery is deferred while a spouse, or a child under 21 or blind or disabled, is living.

⚖️ Partition Under Chapter 5307: Ohio's Built-In Buyout

When co-owners can't agree, R.C. 5307.01 lets any of them "compel" partition in the court of common pleas. If you have read about the Uniform Partition of Heirs Property Act in other states, note that Ohio has not adopted it. Ohio's own procedure, though, already contains a buyout that heirs elsewhere had to wait for legislation to get:

  1. 🧭 Commissioners view the property. The court appoints commissioners to decide whether the house can be divided. A single-family house on one lot almost never can.
  2. 🧾 They return an appraisal. Under R.C. 5307.09, when division would cause "manifest injury" to the value, the commissioners appraise the property instead.
  3. 🙋 Any co-owner can elect to take it. One or more parties can elect to keep the property at the appraised value by paying the others their shares. If more than one co-owner elects, the court decides between them under the statute's procedure.
  4. 🔨 Otherwise, it's sold. If no one elects, R.C. 5307.11 lets the court order a public auction by the sheriff or a licensed auctioneer, and R.C. 5307.12 bars a sale below two-thirds of the appraised value.

A contested Ohio partition commonly takes six months to a year or more and can cost each side several thousand dollars in attorney fees, plus commissioners' fees and costs of sale that the court divides among the owners. The two-thirds floor protects against a giveaway, but it also tells you something uncomfortable: Ohio law expects that a forced public sale may bring well under full value. A family that settles before the auction usually does better than one that waits for the gavel. We explain the general mechanics in our partition lawsuit guide.

🧩 Four Ways Ohio Families Break the Deadlock

1. Re-open the value conversation, not the sale conversation

Best first move

If the holdout's real complaint is the appraisal, get a second opinion: a licensed appraiser's report or two agents' broker price opinions on the house as it actually sits. Sometimes that raises the number enough to satisfy the holdout; sometimes it shows the inventory value was generous and the holdout accepts it. Either way the family is now arguing about evidence instead of feelings.

2. Let the heir who wants it buy the others out

Good if they can finance it

Ohio already anchors buyouts to the appraised value, so a voluntary buyout at that number, documented by an attorney and recorded with a deed from the other heirs, mirrors what a court would do anyway and skips the court. The buyer usually needs a mortgage or cash for the others' shares.

3. Use the land-sale case or partition and stop negotiating

When one person won't move

Sometimes the kindest thing for a family is to let a judge decide. Filing doesn't end settlement talks; it gives them a deadline. Many Ohio partition cases settle once the commissioners' appraisal comes back and everyone can see what an auction at two-thirds would mean for them.

4. One heir sells their share and walks away

Fast, but discounted

After a certificate of transfer, each heir owns a recorded fractional interest and can usually sell it without the others. Buyers of fractional interests, including us, pay well below a proportional share because they inherit the dispute. It is the right move for an heir who needs out now and can't wait a year for partition.

⏰ Ohio Deadlines That Change Who Has Leverage

  • 📅 Six months: creditor claims. Under R.C. 2117.06, most claims must be presented within six months after death or they are barred. Before then, heirs can't be sure what the estate owes, which is one reason fiduciaries hesitate to distribute the house.
  • 📅 Three months: will contests. Under R.C. 2107.76, a will contest must be filed within three months after the certificate of notice of probate is filed. A sibling threatening to contest the will loses that leverage once the window closes.
  • 📅 One month after inventory approval: the spouse's purchase election. Miss it and the spouse loses the statutory right to buy at appraisal.
  • 📅 Six months after appointment: the final account. R.C. 2109.301 expects a final account within six months, but a pending civil action, a spouse's election, or a will contest can extend it. A dispute among heirs is often exactly what keeps an estate open.
  • 📅 January 1: property-tax status. Ohio's owner-occupancy credit depends on the home being someone's primary residence on January 1. The senior and disabled homestead exemption ($29,700 of market value for tax year 2026, with an income limit of $41,000) can continue for a surviving spouse who was at least 59 at the death, but not for children who don't live there.

💵 What Waiting Costs, With Numbers

Take a typical Ohio estate house: a three-bedroom with a dated kitchen, a 20-year-old roof and a basement that seeps in spring. In fixed-up condition (its "after repair value") it would sell for about $200,000. The inventory appraisal said $185,000.

Monthly carrying cost while the heirs argue (use the real bill from your county auditor; these are illustrative):

  • 🧾 Property tax: Ohio bills vary widely by school district. On this house, assume about $3,000 a year, or $250 a month, and expect it to rise if the owner-occupancy credit or homestead exemption drops off.
  • 🛡️ Vacant-home insurance: a standard homeowner's policy often won't cover a house left empty for more than 30 to 60 days. A vacant policy commonly runs $100 to $200 a month.
  • 💡 Utilities kept on to prevent frozen pipes in an Ohio winter: $100 to $200 a month.
  • 🌱 Lawn, snow, gutters and minor repairs: $75 to $150 a month.

That's roughly $525 to $800 a month, or $6,300 to $9,600 a year, before any mortgage. Add the executor's statutory commission under R.C. 2113.35 (4% of the first $100,000 of the estate, 3% of the next $300,000, and 2% above that, plus 1% of real estate that is not sold), attorney fees that often reach several thousand dollars more in a contested land-sale or partition case, and the probate court's own costs (Franklin County, for example, asks for a $250 deposit to open a full estate).

What a cash buyer would pay. Cash buyers typically offer about 60% to 80% of after-repair value, depending on repairs and complexity. On this house, repairs are about $30,000, so a fair cash offer would land around $130,000 to $150,000, call it $140,000 (70%). Notice the Ohio wrinkle: $140,000 is below 80% of the $185,000 appraisal ($148,000), so a consent sale at that price isn't available without a re-appraisal or a court order. A listing, by contrast, might sell for $185,000 to $195,000 after a few thousand in clean-up, less about 6% in commissions and closing costs, netting perhaps $170,000 to $180,000 if the heirs can agree on it within a few months.

The gap between those numbers is real money, and it's why we tell families that agree with each other to list. The calculation flips when the dispute will run a year or more: twelve months of carrying costs, a contested land-sale case and the risk of a sheriff's sale at two-thirds of appraisal can easily consume $20,000 to $40,000 of the difference.

🙅 When You Should List Instead of Calling Us

Don't sell to a cash buyer if every heir will sign, the house is in reasonable shape, and nobody needs money in the next 60 days. Ohio's consent sale or a simple all-heirs listing will usually put more in everyone's pocket. Don't sell to us if the surviving spouse wants the house and can finance the appraised value; the statute was written for exactly that. And don't sell a fractional share to anyone until you understand what a partition case would likely produce, because a share sale only makes sense when you can't afford to wait for one.

Where we're useful in Ohio: houses that need too much work for a retail buyer, estates where the appraisal is higher than any realistic offer and the family needs a court-approved sale, heirs scattered across states who can't coordinate showings, and individual heirs who need to sell their share now.

📋 What To Do This Week

  1. Get the probate case number from the county probate court and read the inventory. Find the appraised value of the house and the date the inventory was approved.
  2. Check the deed at the county recorder for a transfer on death designation affidavit or a recorded certificate of transfer. Those tell you whether the house is still in the estate or already belongs to the heirs.
  3. If a surviving spouse is involved, write down the one-month deadline for the purchase election and the one-year occupancy period.
  4. Get one independent value opinion on the house as-is, so the family can test the appraisal instead of arguing about it.
  5. Ask every heir one question in writing: would you sign a consent to sell at a specific price? Their answers tell you whether you are on the consent path or the court path.
  6. Book a consultation with an Ohio probate attorney before anyone files, records or signs. Bring the inventory, the will and the deed.

✅ Bottom Line

In Ohio, a stuck inherited house is usually a stuck appraisal. The consent sale needs every signature and 80% of the appraised value. The surviving spouse can buy at the appraised value within a month of inventory approval. A partition co-owner can elect to keep the house at the commissioners' appraisal, and an auction can't go below two-thirds of it. Settle the number with evidence, and most families find they can settle the sale.

If the heirs agree and the house is sound, list it. If the house needs work, the appraisal is above what any buyer will pay, or one heir needs out before a court case would finish, ask us. We'll tell you which situation you're in before we tell you what we'd pay.

❓ Frequently Asked Questions

Do all heirs have to agree to sell an inherited house in Ohio?

Not always. If all heirs and the surviving spouse sign written consents under R.C. 2127.011, the executor or administrator can sell without a court case, at no less than 80% of the appraised value. If someone refuses, the fiduciary can ask the probate court to authorize the sale in a land-sale proceeding, and one heir cannot veto that. If the will gives the executor a power of sale, no consents are needed. After a certificate of transfer is recorded, though, every co-owner must sign the deed or someone must file for partition.

What is the 80% rule for selling estate property in Ohio?

R.C. 2127.011 lets a fiduciary sell estate real estate without a land-sale lawsuit if the spouse and all heirs or beneficiaries consent in writing, but the sale price must be at least 80% of the value in the approved inventory. If the best offer is lower, the family needs a new appraisal or a court-approved sale.

Can a minor heir block a consent sale in Ohio?

A minor can't give the written consent the statute requires, so the consent procedure under R.C. 2127.011 isn't available when a minor's consent would be needed. The fiduciary would generally use a probate court land-sale proceeding instead, with a guardian ad litem or guardian protecting the minor's interest.

Can a surviving spouse keep the house in Ohio?

Often, yes. Under R.C. 2106.15 the spouse can live in the house free of charge for one year after the death, and under R.C. 2106.16 the spouse can buy the house at its appraised value unless the will specifically gave it to someone else. The purchase petition must be filed within one month after the inventory is approved.

What is a certificate of transfer in Ohio?

It is a probate court document issued under R.C. 2113.61 that lists the heirs or beneficiaries and the share of each parcel of real estate each one receives. Recorded with the county recorder, it moves title out of the decedent's name. After that, the heirs are co-owners and a sale needs all of their signatures or a partition case.

Does Ohio have the Uniform Partition of Heirs Property Act?

No. Ohio uses its own partition statutes in R.C. Chapter 5307. They already include a buyout: when commissioners find a house can't be divided, they appraise it, and any co-owner can elect to take it at the appraised value by paying the others their shares under R.C. 5307.09.

How low can a partition auction go in Ohio?

Under R.C. 5307.12, real estate sold in a partition case cannot be sold for less than two-thirds of the value returned by the commissioners. If no co-owner elects to buy at the appraised value, the court can order a public sale by the sheriff or a licensed auctioneer under R.C. 5307.11.

How long do Ohio heirs have to wait before selling an inherited house?

There's no fixed waiting period, but timing matters. Creditors generally have six months after death to present claims under R.C. 2117.06, a will contest must be filed within three months after the notice of probate is filed under R.C. 2107.76, and the spouse has one month after inventory approval to elect to buy the house. Many fiduciaries sell after the inventory is approved and the spouse's election period has passed.

Can Ohio Medicaid take an inherited house?

It can file a claim. Ohio's estate recovery law, R.C. 5162.21, applies to Medicaid recipients who were 55 or older or permanently institutionalized, and it defines the estate broadly enough to reach some property that passed outside probate. Recovery is deferred while a surviving spouse, or a child under 21 or blind or disabled, is alive. Ask the estate's attorney whether a claim has been filed before you sell.

Should our family list the Ohio house or take a cash offer?

If every heir will sign and the house is in decent shape, listing usually nets more. A cash offer makes more sense when the house needs major repairs, when the realistic price is below 80% of the appraisal so a consent sale won't work, when a land-sale or partition case would take a year, or when one heir wants to sell their share now.
