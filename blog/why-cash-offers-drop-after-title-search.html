Why Cash Offers Drop After the Title Search (and How to Spot One Before You Sign)

A fast cash offer is often built on what you remember about your loans. The title search is built on what's recorded. When those two don't match, the price gets cut, usually right before closing. Here's what turns up, what it does to your number, and how to protect yourself.

By Dustin Ray, Founder of Pages of Purpose LLC. Not legal advice. Title, lien and recording rules differ by state and county. Before you sign a purchase agreement, have an attorney licensed in the state where the property sits review it.

The short answer: many cash buyers, especially those buying a co-owner's share or an inherited house, make their first offer before anyone has pulled the deed, the recorded mortgages or the court records. The offer assumes the numbers you gave them are complete. Then, a few days into the contract, the title company sends a report. If it shows a second mortgage, a home equity line, a judgment, a tax lien or an heir nobody mentioned, the buyer "re-trades": they lower the price, often by tens of thousands of dollars, and you're left choosing between a smaller check and starting over.

That isn't always a scam. Sometimes the problem really was hidden, even from you. But a buyer who knew the title search could change everything and still quoted you a firm-sounding number has made the drop your problem instead of theirs. The fix is simple: get the records checked before you accept a number, and know which contract clauses let a buyer walk away or cut the price.

How a "fast" cash offer is usually built

Most quick offers come from a two-minute phone call or web form. The buyer asks three things: what's the house worth, how much is owed, and what share do you own. They plug your answers into a formula, usually a percentage of the equity, and send a number within a day or two. Nobody has read the deed yet.

That works fine when your answers are complete. The trouble is that sellers in hard situations often don't have the full picture, through no fault of their own:

  • Co-owners who aren't on the loan often don't know the balance. They may know the first mortgage roughly and not know a home equity line exists at all.
  • Heirs rarely know what a parent borrowed, refinanced or let lapse in the last ten years of their life.
  • Divorcing spouses may not know the other spouse drew on a credit line or let a judgment go unpaid.
  • Owners of land or rentals often forget small things that become liens: a water bill, an HOA dispute, a contractor who never got paid.

The contract then gives the buyer a "due diligence" or "inspection" period, often 10 to 30 days, and a clause like "subject to clear title acceptable to buyer." The title report arrives inside that window. Anything on it becomes a reason to renegotiate.

What a title search actually finds

A title company (or a closing attorney, in states that use them) searches the county land records, the courts and the tax office for anything recorded against the property and against every owner on the deed. These are the items that most often blow up a quick offer, roughly from most to least common in our experience:

1. A second mortgage or home equity line (HELOC)

The single most common surprise. Many buyers since 2018 financed with a first mortgage plus a home equity line taken out the same day, sometimes called a "piggyback," to avoid mortgage insurance or put less down. A co-owner who isn't the borrower may never have seen the paperwork. Because a credit line can be drawn again, the balance can also grow after the buyer made their offer. A recorded line with no release on file is treated as open until the lender says otherwise.

2. Judgments against any owner

A court judgment against one owner, for a credit card, a car repossession, a medical bill or a contractor, can become a lien on that owner's share of real estate in the county where it's recorded. In Maryland, for example, a money judgment is a lien on the debtor's land in the county for 12 years and can be renewed. If you're selling your share, judgments against you come off your check. Judgments against your co-owner usually affect only their share, but a careless buyer may not have priced either.

3. Federal and state tax liens, child support liens

IRS and state tax liens attach to everything the taxpayer owns. Child support liens do too in most states. These generally have to be paid, released or "discharged" from the property before a title company will insure the sale.

4. Unpaid property taxes and tax sale certificates

If taxes went unpaid long enough, the county may have sold a tax lien certificate to an investor. That investor is owed the taxes plus interest and fees, and in many states can eventually foreclose. The payoff is often larger than the tax bill the seller remembers.

5. Water, sewer, code and HOA liens

Municipal water and sewer charges, code-enforcement fines, demolition or grass-cutting bills, and HOA "statement of lien" filings are easy to forget and show up on the report.

6. Old mortgages that were paid but never released

A loan paid off years ago can still show as open if the lender never recorded a release. This one usually gets fixed, but it takes time, and a buyer in a hurry may use it to cut the price instead.

7. Ownership problems

  • Someone on title is dead and no estate was ever opened, so nobody has authority to sign for their share.
  • Married owners who hold title "as tenants by the entirety", which in many states means one spouse can't sell a share alone while the marriage lasts.
  • A pending divorce with a court order restricting transfers, or a lis pendens recorded by a spouse.
  • An open bankruptcy, which puts a trustee in control of the debtor's property.
  • A reverse mortgage, which grows every month and must be paid off in full.

Any of these can turn a "close in five days" promise into weeks or months, or end the deal.

What this does to a co-owner's offer: a real example

Here's a situation we looked at recently, with identifying details removed. Two unmarried partners bought a newer single-family house together for a little over $800,000. One partner is on the mortgage; the other is only on the deed. They separated, and the partner on the deed wanted to sell their half.

From memory, the seller believed the house was worth "over a million" and that a little over $600,000 was owed. Another company offered in the mid-$80,000s for the half share, with closing in about a week. On those numbers it looks reasonable: $1,000,000 minus $600,000 is $400,000 of equity, half is $200,000, and an offer in the mid-$80,000s is a typical discount for a share.

Then we pulled the land records:

ItemWhat the seller rememberedWhat was recorded
Market valueOver $1,000,000About $900,000–$950,000, based on recent sales of similar houses in the same community
First mortgage"Six-something"About $550,000 original; roughly $495,000 today by amortization
Second loanNot mentionedA purchase-money home equity line of up to about $245,000, recorded the same day, no release
Total debtAbout $600,000About $650,000–$740,000, depending on the line's balance
Half of the equity, after about 7% selling costsAbout $200,000About $50,000–$120,000

Once a title report shows that home equity line, an offer in the mid-$80,000s for the half either gets cut sharply or the buyer walks, unless the line happens to be mostly paid down. The seller didn't do anything wrong. The first number was simply built on incomplete information, and that's predictable when the person selling isn't the borrower.

Honest note: a recorded home equity line shows the limit, not the balance. If it's mostly paid off, the original offer could be fine. That's exactly why the right move is to check the recorded documents and the real balances before anyone commits to a number.

Red flags in the offer or contract

Not every buyer who uses these clauses plans to re-trade. But each one is a door, and you should know where the doors are before you sign:

  • "Subject to title satisfactory to buyer" with no limit on what counts as unsatisfactory.
  • A long "inspection" or "due diligence" period on a share or a house sold as-is. What are they inspecting?
  • "Buyer and/or assigns" on the buyer line. This often means the "buyer" plans to sell your contract to someone else, and the price can change when they do.
  • A tiny earnest deposit ($10 to $100) or one that's refundable for any reason.
  • "Partner approval," "funding approval" or "investor approval" contingencies.
  • A closing promise faster than a title search can be done. A careful title search and payoff letters usually take several business days on their own.
  • No written breakdown. If the buyer can't show you how they got their number, you can't tell what would change it.

How to protect yourself, for free

  1. Look up your own records. Most counties put deeds and mortgages online through the recorder, register of deeds or clerk of court. In Maryland, it's Maryland Land Records, free with an account. Search every owner's name and look for mortgages, deeds of trust, releases and liens.
  2. Check the courts. Search every owner's name in your state's court case search for judgments, divorces and foreclosure or tax-sale cases.
  3. Ask the buyer one question in writing: "Is this price firm after your title search, assuming the title shows only what I've told you?" Then ask what exactly would change it.
  4. Ask to see the title commitment before you're past the point of no return, and compare it to what you were told.
  5. Get the payoff numbers for any loan you're on, or ask the borrower for a recent statement.
  6. Get a second offer. Two buyers' math side by side tells you a lot.
  7. Have an attorney read the contract. An hour of a real estate attorney's time costs far less than a re-trade.

Frequently asked questions

Is it legal for a buyer to lower the price after the title search?

Usually, yes, if the contract lets them. Most investor contracts include a title or due-diligence contingency. If the buyer can cancel, they can also offer a lower price and let you decide. Read those clauses before you sign.

How long does a title search take?

A basic search with payoff requests commonly takes several business days, longer for older properties, estates or counties with slow records. Be skeptical of firm closing dates that leave no room for it.

Can I see what's recorded on my property myself?

Yes. Deeds, mortgages and most liens are public records at your county recorder, register of deeds or clerk. Many counties have free online search. Court judgments are usually searchable through your state's court website.

Does a home equity line show the balance in the records?

No. The recorded document shows the maximum amount, not what's currently owed. The borrower or the lender has the real balance, and a payoff letter is what the title company relies on at closing.

What if the problem is a judgment against my co-owner, not me?

In most states a judgment against one owner attaches only to that owner's share. If you're selling your own share, it generally shouldn't reduce your price, though it can complicate an eventual sale of the whole property.
