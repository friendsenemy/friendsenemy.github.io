Partition Lawsuits in Colorado: Commissioners Walk the Property First, and the Co-Owner Who Lives There Often Gets the First Chance to Buy

Colorado's partition statute is short, old and built around court-appointed commissioners who must look at the land and try to divide it before any sale. Colorado courts have also, as a matter of practice, let the co-owner who lives in the house buy it at the court's price. Both facts shape a Colorado case more than the filing itself does. Here's how the case actually runs, what it costs, and when you're better off settling or selling. Or ask us a question, free.

A partition action is the lawsuit any co-owner can bring to end co-ownership when the owners can't agree. Every state has one. What makes Colorado's version worth reading closely is how much of the process happens outside the courtroom, through commissioners the judge appoints, and how often the result is not an auction to a stranger but a buyout by the owner who's already living there.

🧑‍⚖️ Before Anything Is Sold, Colorado Sends Commissioners to the Property

Colorado's partition law sits in C.R.S. §§ 38-28-101 to 38-28-110, ten short sections. The heart of it is § 38-28-105. When the district court enters an order for partition, it appoints one or more disinterested commissioners. They take an oath to make the partition fairly and impartially, go and view the property, and write up a division assigning each owner a share. That written partition goes back to the judge for confirmation, and any party can object within the time the court sets.

Only if the commissioners report, and the court agrees, that the property can't be divided without "manifest prejudice" to someone's rights does § 38-28-107 allow a sale. So the statute's default order of operations is:

  1. 🗂️ A co-owner files in the district court for the county where the property is, and records a notice of lis pendens so buyers and lenders know about the case (Colorado Rule of Civil Procedure 105(f) covers lis pendens in actions affecting title).
  2. 📬 Every owner and anyone else with an interest is served. Under § 38-28-102 and § 38-28-103, the court can bring in everyone with a claim and resolve all of it in one case.
  3. ⚖️ The court decides the shares and orders partition.
  4. 🚶 Commissioners view the property and try to divide it (§ 38-28-105 and § 38-28-106).
  5. 🔨 If division would cause manifest prejudice, the court can order a sale (§ 38-28-107).
  6. 💰 The commissioners or sellers report back, the court confirms, and the money is distributed (§ 38-28-108).

For a single-family house on a city lot, the commissioners' answer is almost always that it can't be split, so in practice the case moves quickly toward a sale. The step still matters, because commissioners cost money, and because on acreage, mountain land or a property with two houses, Colorado judges take the division option seriously.

🏡 The Co-Owner Who Lives There Usually Gets the First Chance to Buy

Here's the Colorado practice that changes strategy for both sides. In a 2021 article for the Colorado Bar Association's journal, "Partition Comes of Age," the authors note that Colorado courts have consistently given the party living at the property the opportunity to purchase it at the price the court decrees. That isn't written into the statute; it's how Colorado judges have tended to exercise their equitable discretion.

What that means for you depends on which side of the front door you're on:

  • 🔑 If you live in the house: a partition case is not necessarily the end of your ownership. If you can raise the money, often through a refinance, you may be able to keep the house by paying the other owners the value of their shares, net of credits. Start talking to a lender early, because a buy-in window doesn't help if you can't close.
  • 🚪 If you've moved out: filing may not lead to a market sale at all. It may lead to the occupant buying you out at a court-set value. That's often a fine result, but understand that the value fight and the credits fight are where your money is decided.
  • 🧾 For both of you: an agreed appraisal early in the case narrows the biggest argument and saves fees.

📐 "Manifest Prejudice": the Two Ways a Sale Gets Ordered

Colorado, like most states, prefers dividing land over selling it. Under § 38-28-107, a sale is allowed only when partition can't be made without manifest prejudice to an interested party. Colorado case law, as summarized in the same bar journal article, recognizes two ways to show it:

🗺️ The land can't practically be split into fair pieces

Most houses

The physical characteristics of the property make it impracticable to divide into parts that match the owners' fractions. A three-bedroom house on a 6,000-square-foot lot in Thornton is the obvious example. So is a condominium unit.

📉 The whole is worth materially more than the parts

Acreage and ranch land

Even if the land could be cut up, the court can order a sale if the whole parcel is worth materially more than the sum of the divided pieces. Forty acres with one well and one access road might be worth far more as one piece than as two twenty-acre tracts, one of which has no water.

If you own a share of rural land in Colorado and want to keep part of it, that second test is where your evidence goes: show that a division can produce fair pieces without destroying value, and the commissioners may divide it rather than sell.

🔨 Public Sale by Default, a Broker Listing by Agreement

The statute's default is old-fashioned. Section 38-28-107 lets the court direct a sale "at public sale" on terms it fixes, with notice given the same way as for sales of real estate on execution. That's a sheriff-style auction, and auctions of houses rarely bring full market value.

In practice, Colorado parties usually avoid it. The bar journal article notes that most parties agree on a real estate broker and the terms of sale rather than pay for a commissioner-run sale. A judge can approve that agreement, and a listed sale with ordinary marketing usually brings a materially better price than a courthouse auction. If you're the one filing, propose a broker and a listing price early. If you're defending, don't refuse a listing out of spite; a lower auction price comes out of your share too.

🧾 The Accounting: Down Payments, Mortgage, Improvements and Rent

Partition in Colorado is an equitable proceeding, so the judge doesn't simply split the check by the fractions on the deed. The court adjusts for what each owner put in and took out. The Colorado cases discussed in the bar journal treat these items this way:

ItemHow it's usually treated
Down paymentCredited to the owner who paid it
Mortgage principal, taxes and other payments on liensCredited to the owner who paid more than their share; § 38-28-110 lets the court pay off or apportion liens
ImprovementsCredited at the amount the improvement increased the property's value, not what it cost
HOA duesCan count as payment of a potential lien such as an assessment
Rent from the co-owner living thereGenerally not owed unless there was an agreement or the other owner was ousted
Rent collected from a tenantMust be accounted for to the other owners

Two practical lessons follow. First, the $40,000 kitchen remodel may only earn a $20,000 credit if that's all it added to value. Second, an owner who moved out can't usually bill the one who stayed for rent just because they stayed. If you were locked out or told not to come back, that's different, and you should document it.

💸 Who Pays the Lawyers and the Commissioners

This is the part of Colorado's statute most people find surprising. Under C.R.S. § 38-28-109, the court fixes the pay of the commissioners and of whoever conducts the sale, and it may order that pay, along with costs, expenses and attorney's fees, paid out of the sale proceeds, or make any other order it thinks best. The statute doesn't set a formula or a cap. That gives the judge room to have the house pay for the case, to split fees by ownership share, or to put more of the cost on an owner whose conduct made the case longer.

Don't read that as "the lawsuit is free." Fees paid from proceeds still reduce every owner's check, and judges are less generous with fees spent fighting each other than with fees that moved the case to a sale. The most expensive Colorado partition cases are the ones where both owners litigate every credit.

🧮 A Greeley House, Three Ways

Two brothers inherited a house in Greeley from their mother and own it 50/50 as tenants in common. One brother lives there and has paid the taxes; the other moved to Denver. Repaired, the house would sell for about $380,000; in its current shape, perhaps $345,000.

  • 🏠 Carrying it during a case. Weld County tax on a house at this value runs very roughly $1,700 to $2,300 a year, insurance $1,500 to $2,400 a year, and utilities and upkeep fall on whoever lives there. Over a 12-month case, the household spends roughly $5,000 to $8,000 just to hold the house, plus each brother's lawyer.
  • ⚖️ Partition with a broker sale: $345,000 less about 7 percent in sale costs is about $321,000. Take out $20,000 in fees and commissioner costs and $300,000 remains, or $150,000 each before credits. The living-in brother's tax payments will shift some of that his way.
  • 🔑 Occupant buy-in: if the court sets value near $345,000, the brother who stays owes his brother about $172,500 less credits and his share of costs. He needs a lender to say yes.
  • 💵 Cash sale of the whole house, agreed before filing: at 60 to 80 percent of the $380,000 repaired value, $228,000 to $304,000. On this house we'd likely be in the $265,000 to $285,000 range, closed in weeks, with no fees to either brother's lawyer beyond a contract review.
  • 🧩 The Denver brother sells just his half: a share buyer would likely offer $90,000 to $120,000, because they inherit the case.

The honest read: if the brothers can agree on a broker, a listing beats both our offer and a contested case. Our offer makes sense when the house needs work the brothers won't pay for, when one of them needs out now, or when the fees on a contested case would eat the difference.

🚫 When to Skip Both the Lawsuit and Us

Partition is a tool for a real stalemate. If the other owner is willing to talk, a written buyout offer with an appraisal attached solves more Colorado co-ownership fights than a complaint does. If the property is part of a pending divorce, the divorce court handles it, not a separate partition case. If the co-owners are heirs and the estate is still open, the personal representative may have power to sell, and our guide to Colorado heirs who can't agree covers that route. And if the house is in good condition and everyone will sign a listing, list it. We're the better answer when the house needs significant repairs, a co-owner can't be found, or someone simply needs to be out within weeks.

📋 Before You File: This Week

  1. 📄 Pull the recorded deed and a title report so you know every owner and every lien.
  2. 🧾 Total up what each owner has paid for the down payment, mortgage principal, taxes, insurance, HOA dues and improvements, with receipts.
  3. 🏷️ Get an appraisal or broker opinion of value; both sides will need one, and agreeing on one saves money.
  4. ✉️ Send a written buyout offer with a deadline. A judge will see it later, and it often ends the dispute now.
  5. 🏦 If you live in the house, ask a lender whether you could refinance to buy out the others at that value.
  6. ⚖️ Interview a Colorado attorney who handles partition regularly, and ask how they'd propose to handle commissioners and the sale method in your judicial district.

✅ Bottom Line

A Colorado partition case is built around commissioners who must view the property and try to divide it, a sale only on a showing of manifest prejudice, and a judge with broad power to pay commissioners, costs and attorney's fees from the proceeds. In practice, the occupant often gets the chance to buy at the court's price, and most cases end with an agreed broker sale or a buyout rather than an auction. The money is decided in the accounting, so keep your receipts.

❓ Frequently Asked Questions

What statute governs partition in Colorado?

Colorado Revised Statutes 38-28-101 through 38-28-110. Any person with an interest in the property can file in district court, and the court can bring in everyone with a claim and resolve all title questions in one case.

What do partition commissioners do in Colorado?

Under C.R.S. 38-28-105, the court appoints one or more disinterested commissioners who view the property, divide it in writing, assign each owner a share and report back. Parties can object before the judge confirms the division.

When will a Colorado court order a house sold instead of divided?

When partition can't be made without manifest prejudice. Colorado courts find that when the land can't practically be divided into fair parts, or when the whole parcel is worth materially more than the sum of the divided pieces.

Does the co-owner living in the house get to buy it in a Colorado partition?

Often, yes. Colorado courts have consistently given the party residing at the property the opportunity to buy it at the price the court decrees. That comes from judicial practice rather than the statute, so it isn't guaranteed.

Does a Colorado partition sale have to be an auction?

The statute's default is a public sale with execution-style notice under C.R.S. 38-28-107. In practice most parties agree on a real estate broker and sale terms, and the court approves that instead.

Who pays attorney fees in a Colorado partition lawsuit?

C.R.S. 38-28-109 lets the court order commissioners' pay, costs, expenses and attorney's fees paid from the sale proceeds, or make any other order it finds best. There's no fixed formula, so the judge decides how the burden falls.

Can I charge my co-owner rent for living in the house in Colorado?

Usually not, unless you had an agreement or were ousted from the property. Rent collected from a third-party tenant is different and must be accounted for to all owners.

How are improvements credited in a Colorado partition?

By how much the improvement increased the property's value, not by what it cost. A remodel that cost $40,000 but added $20,000 in value usually earns about a $20,000 credit.

How long does a partition case take in Colorado?

An agreed broker sale can happen within about four to eight months of filing. A case with contested shares and credits commonly runs 12 to 24 months, and an appeal adds more time.

Is it better to sell my share than file for partition in Colorado?

Only if speed matters more than price. A share sale ends your involvement in weeks but pays a discount. Partition, or a listing the owners agree to, usually brings more if you can afford the time and fees.
