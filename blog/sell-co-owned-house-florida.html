Co-Own a Florida House? First Find Out What Florida Has Already Done to Your Deed

Florida law changes how some co-owners hold title without anyone signing anything. A deed that forgot the survivorship words is a tenancy in common. A married couple's deed becomes one the day the divorce is final. That label decides whether you can force a sale, who inherits, and even how the property tax bill is figured. Here's how to read yours and pick an exit. Or ask us, free.

By Dustin Ray, Founder of Pages of Purpose LLC. You share a Florida house with someone, and the arrangement has stopped working. Maybe you and an ex bought a place in Kissimmee while married. Maybe two brothers bought a duplex in St. Petersburg together, or you and a partner of ten years split up, or your mother put your name on her deed in Ocala and your sister's too. Now one of you wants out.

Before you talk about price, find your deed and read how it names the owners. In Florida, that wording, plus anything that has happened since, such as a divorce or a death, decides whether you can force a sale, what happens if someone dies, and how much the person living there pays in property tax. Florida also changes some of those labels automatically, so the deed may not mean what it seems to say.

🔄 How Florida Converts Some Deeds Automatically

The key statute is short. Section 689.15 of the Florida Statutes says survivorship between joint owners does not prevail in Florida, so a deed to two or more people creates a tenancy in common unless the deed expressly provides for survivorship. Estates by the entirety, the special form for married couples, are the one exception. And the same statute says that when a married couple holding by the entirety divorces, they become tenants in common.

That gives you two automatic conversions to check for:

  • A deed to "A and B as joint tenants" without survivorship words. Unless the deed adds language like "with full rights of survivorship," Florida treats A and B as tenants in common. If A dies, A's share goes to A's heirs or will beneficiaries, not to B.
  • A married couple's deed after divorce. Florida presumes married couples who take real estate together hold it by the entirety. The day the divorce becomes final, that becomes a tenancy in common, even if the divorce judgment never mentions the house. Each ex-spouse now owns an undivided half and can sell that half or ask a court to force a sale.

🧾 The Deed Wording Also Sets the Occupant's Tax Bill

Here's a Florida wrinkle that changes negotiations more than people expect. Under the Department of Revenue's rule on joint ownership (Rule 12D-7.012 of the Florida Administrative Code), a tenant in common who lives in the house can claim the homestead exemption only on their own share of the assessed value. A joint tenant with survivorship who is the only owner living there can claim the whole exemption. So two houses next door, with the same value and the same occupant, can carry different tax bills just because of a few words on the deed.

Then there's Save Our Homes. Once a Florida homestead is established, the assessed value of the homestead portion can rise each year by no more than 3% or the change in the Consumer Price Index, whichever is lower. For 2026 that cap was 2.7%. A co-owner who has lived in the house since 2012 may be paying tax on an assessed value far below today's market. When the property sells, the cap goes away and the new owner's taxes are figured on full market value.

That has two practical effects. First, the co-owner living in the house often has a real financial reason to stay: moving means giving up a capped assessment, though Florida's portability rules may let them carry part of that benefit to a new homestead within the deadline. Second, the out-of-possession owner may be paying taxes on their share at non-homestead rates. If you're negotiating with an occupant who won't budge, ask a Florida tax professional or the county property appraiser how much portability would carry over. Showing them the number sometimes moves the conversation.

💔 Ex-Spouses Who Are Still on the Deed

We see this one constantly in Florida. The divorce was finalized years ago, the judgment either gave one spouse the house "upon refinancing" that never happened or didn't address the house at all, and both names are still on title. One ex lives there. The other is still on the mortgage and wants out.

Because Section 689.15 turned the entireties estate into a tenancy in common, the ex who moved out is a co-owner like any other. The Florida Supreme Court held in Tullis v. Tullis (1978) that Florida's homestead protections don't stop one former spouse from forcing a partition and sale against the other. If the divorce judgment did address the house, though, the first step is usually to enforce that judgment in the family court rather than starting a new case. Bring the judgment and any marital settlement agreement to a Florida family law attorney before you do anything else.

One more Florida rule matters for married co-owners who aren't married to each other. If a co-owner lives in the house with a spouse, Florida's constitutional homestead provisions generally require that spouse to join in any deed of the co-owner's interest, even if the spouse isn't on title. Ask the title agent early whether a non-owner spouse's signature will be needed.

🏠 One Owner Lives There, the Other Pays: Who Owes Whom?

Florida follows the general rule that a co-owner living in the property doesn't owe rent to the others just for living there. A Florida appeals court summarized it in Goins v. Goins (2000): a co-tenant in sole possession who collects no rent from outsiders isn't accountable to the others unless the possession is adverse or the result of an ouster. Changing the locks and refusing entry can be an ouster. Simply living there usually isn't.

That rule shifts once the case reaches a partition accounting. If the occupant asks to be credited for the mortgage, taxes and insurance they paid, Florida courts can offset those credits by the reasonable rental value of their exclusive use. If the occupant rents rooms or the whole house to tenants, they must account to the other owners for the rent actually collected. Practically: if you're the one living there and paying everything, keep records. If you're the one locked out, put a request for access in writing and save the response.

⚖️ If It Comes to a Florida Partition Case

Any tenant in common or joint tenant can file a partition action under Chapter 64 of the Florida Statutes. Partition cases are equity cases heard by a circuit judge without a jury, filed in the county where the property sits. If the property can be divided, Section 64.061 has the court appoint three commissioners to divide it. For a single house that's rarely practical, so under Section 64.071 the court orders it sold at public auction to the highest bidder, usually through the clerk's online sale system.

Two Florida rules shape the cost. Under Section 64.081, every party is bound by the judgment to pay a share of the costs, including attorneys' fees, determined on equitable principles in proportion to that party's interest. In other words, the owner who resisted can end up sharing the cost of the lawyer who forced the sale. And for inherited property filed on or after July 1, 2020, Florida's Uniform Partition of Heirs Property Act (Sections 64.201 through 64.214) lets the owners who didn't ask for a sale buy out the one who did, and replaces the courthouse auction with an open-market sale through a licensed broker.

Expect an uncontested Florida partition to take four to eight months and a contested one twelve to twenty-four. Attorney fees commonly run $5,000 to $25,000 per side, with a court-appointed appraiser or special magistrate on top. Our guide to partition lawsuits explains the general process.

🔀 Your Florida Exit Options, Rated

List it together

Highest price if everyone cooperates

If the house is in good shape and every owner (and any required spouse) will sign, a listing usually brings the most money. Florida's buyer pool is deep, but in many areas insurance and inspection requirements have gotten stricter. An older roof or a dated electrical panel can scare off financed buyers whose insurers won't write a policy. Get an inspection done early so there are no surprises.

Buyout by the owner who lives there

Best when one person wants to stay

The occupant buys the other owner's share at an agreed value, refinancing if there's a mortgage. Remember Florida documentary stamp tax: the deed is generally taxed at 70 cents per $100 of consideration (60 cents in Miami-Dade for single-family homes), which includes any mortgage balance the buyer takes over. On a $150,000 buyout that's about $1,050.

Cash sale of the whole house

Fastest when repairs or insurance block a listing

If the roof is near the end of its life, there's water damage, or the house won't pass an insurer's four-point inspection, many financed buyers can't close. A cash buyer can. You give up some price for speed and certainty, and nobody has to fix anything first.

Sell only your share

Possible, but priced as a dispute

As a tenant in common you can deed your share without the others' consent. A buyer is buying into a disagreement and a likely partition case, so the price will be well below your percentage of the house's value. It mostly makes sense when you can't fund a partition case yourself.

Partition

The backstop that makes negotiation possible

Filing shows you're serious, and Section 64.081 means the other owner shares the bill. Many Florida cases settle once the complaint is served. If the case goes to a clerk's auction, though, the house may sell for less than a cooperative listing would bring.

💵 What Each Exit Nets on a Florida House

Consider a 1990s block house outside Tampa owned 50/50 by ex-spouses. Updated, it would sell for about $380,000. It needs a roof and some water-damage repairs, roughly $40,000, and the roof is old enough that many insurers won't cover it.

Carrying costs. Non-homestead property in much of Florida pays somewhere around 1.5% to 2% of taxable value a year once county, school, city and special-district millage are added up. At 1.8% on $380,000, that's about $6,840 a year, or $570 a month. A vacant or hard-to-insure Florida house can cost $250 to $500 a month to insure, if you can get a policy at all. Add about $175 for power, water and pest control and $150 for lawn and upkeep. Call it $1,150 to $1,400 a month while the owners argue.

List as-is. With the roof issue, a financed buyer may not get insurance, so the realistic as-is buyer pool shrinks. Say a buyer pays $320,000 after a 90-day listing. Subtract about 8% in commission and closing costs ($25,600) and three months of carrying costs ($3,800). Net: about $290,600, or $145,300 each.

Cash sale. Cash buyers usually pay about 60% to 80% of after-repair value. Here, an offer around $255,000, or 67% of $380,000, closing in two to three weeks with no repairs, is realistic. Net: about $252,000 after minor closing costs, or $126,000 each.

Partition. After a contested year in circuit court and a clerk's auction at $300,000, subtract attorney fees, which the judge splits by interest under Section 64.081 (say $30,000 total), plus costs and twelve months of carrying costs. Each owner may net around $120,000 to $125,000, a year later.

Listing wins on paper by about $19,000 per owner, if a buyer's insurer will write the policy and both owners sign. The cash sale wins when the insurance problem is real, when one owner can't keep paying, or when the relationship makes a months-long listing unworkable.

📋 What To Do This Week

  1. Get a copy of the recorded deed from the county clerk's official records search and read exactly how the owners are named.
  2. If any owner has divorced since the deed was signed, pull the final judgment and any marital settlement agreement.
  3. Look up the property on the county property appraiser's site: who holds the homestead exemption, and how far the assessed value sits below market.
  4. Gather what each owner has paid toward mortgage, taxes, insurance and repairs.
  5. Get an insurance quote or a four-point inspection so you know whether a financed buyer can close.
  6. Send the other owner a written proposal with a deadline: list together, buyout, or cash sale.
  7. If the deadline passes, consult a Florida real estate attorney about a Chapter 64 partition.

✅ Bottom Line

In Florida, read the deed before you read the market. Missing survivorship words and a finished divorce both leave you as tenants in common, which means either owner can sell their share or force a sale, and each owner's heirs inherit their share. The same wording affects the occupant's homestead exemption, and Save Our Homes can give them a strong tax reason to stay put. Use that knowledge in the negotiation. List if everyone will sign and the house can be insured. Sell for cash if it can't, or if waiting costs more than the discount. Ask us if you want an honest read on which one fits.

❓ Frequently Asked Questions

Can my co-owner force me to sell a house in Florida?

If you hold title as tenants in common or joint tenants, yes. Any co-owner can file a partition action under Chapter 64 of the Florida Statutes, and if the house can't be divided the court orders it sold. The main exception is a married couple holding as tenants by the entirety, which can't be partitioned while the marriage lasts.

Our deed says joint tenants. Do we have survivorship in Florida?

Only if the deed expressly says so. Under Florida Statutes Section 689.15, a deed to two or more people creates a tenancy in common unless it expressly provides for a right of survivorship. Without those words, each owner's share passes to their own heirs or will beneficiaries.

What happens to a Florida house owned by the entirety after divorce?

Section 689.15 says that when a married couple holding by the entirety divorces, they become tenants in common. That happens automatically, even if the divorce judgment doesn't mention the house, and either ex-spouse can then sell their half or seek partition.

Can my ex-spouse who lives in the house stop a partition because it's homestead?

Generally no. In Tullis v. Tullis (1978), the Florida Supreme Court held that homestead protections don't prevent one former spouse from forcing a partition and sale against the other. If your divorce judgment addressed the house, though, enforcing it in family court is usually the first step.

My co-owner lives in the Florida house rent-free. Do they owe me rent?

Not just for living there. Florida courts hold that a co-owner in sole possession isn't accountable for rent unless they ousted the others or collected rent from third parties. In a partition accounting, however, the occupant's rental value can offset any credits they claim for paying the mortgage, taxes or insurance.

Why is my co-owner's property tax bill so much lower than market value suggests?

Two reasons. The occupant may hold a homestead exemption, and Save Our Homes caps annual increases in a homestead's assessed value at 3% or the CPI change, whichever is lower (2.7% for 2026). When the house sells, the cap ends and the buyer is taxed on full market value. For tenants in common, the exemption covers only the occupant's share.

Who pays attorney fees in a Florida partition case?

Under Florida Statutes Section 64.081, every party is bound by the judgment to pay a share of the costs, including attorneys' fees, determined on equitable principles in proportion to their interest. The co-owner who opposed the sale can end up sharing the cost of the lawyer who forced it.

Can I sell my share of a co-owned Florida house without the other owner?

Yes, if you are a tenant in common or a joint tenant. You can deed your undivided interest to a buyer without consent, but expect a price well below your percentage of the house's value. If your co-owner's spouse lives there, ask the title agent whether homestead rules require any extra signature.

Do we pay documentary stamp tax if one co-owner buys the other out in Florida?

Generally yes. Florida documentary stamp tax on deeds is 70 cents per $100 of consideration, or 60 cents in Miami-Dade for single-family homes, and the consideration usually includes any mortgage balance the buying owner assumes. A $150,000 buyout would typically carry about $1,050 in stamps.

Should co-owners list a Florida house or sell it for cash?

If the house is insurable, in decent shape and every owner will sign, listing usually nets more. A cash sale makes sense when an old roof or other damage blocks insurance for financed buyers, when one owner can't keep paying the carrying costs, or when the owners can't cooperate through a months-long listing.
