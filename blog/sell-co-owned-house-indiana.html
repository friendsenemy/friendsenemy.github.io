Selling a Co-Owned House in Indiana: The Statute Gives You 60 Days, Then It Sells the House

Indiana removed the holdout's leverage. Under IC 32-17-4-2.5 a partition court appoints an appraiser within 30 days, orders mediation within 45, and warns everyone the property will be sold if they have not agreed within 60. So the Indiana question is never whether the house sells — it is who buys it, at what price, and who gets paid back first. Here is how to win those three. Or ask us a question, free.

In most states, a co-owner who refuses to sell has leverage. The other owners have to file a partition case, prove the house cannot be fairly divided, wait on commissioners, and hope a judge orders a sale. The holdout can stall for a year or two and often wins a better deal just by waiting.

Indiana took that leverage away. The partition statute Indiana rewrote in 2012 and amended since — IC 32-17-4-2.5 — does not ask whether the property should be sold. It sets a schedule that ends in a sale unless everyone agrees on something else first. Appraiser appointed within 30 days of the court getting jurisdiction. Case referred to mediation within 45 days. And in the order sending it to mediation, the court must tell the parties outright that the property will be sold if they cannot reach an agreement within 60 days.

So in Indiana the question is never really "can they make me sell." It is who ends up owning the house, at what price, and who gets paid back first. The rest of this guide is about winning those three questions, because that is where Indiana law actually gives a co-owner something to work with.

🏁 Indiana Has No Middle Gear

Read IC 32-17-4-2.5 in order and the design is obvious.

  • Who can file. Under IC 32-17-4-1, any joint tenant or tenant in common can compel partition — in their own right or as an executor or trustee — and if a deceased co-owner's estate needs the sale, the administrator or executor can file too.
  • Title search up front. IC 32-17-4-2 requires the petition to describe the premises and the parties' rights and titles, and requires whoever files to have a title search done and file the results with the court.
  • Appraisal within 30 days of the court acquiring jurisdiction over all interested parties, by a licensed real estate appraiser the court appoints, filed with the court — unless every party waives it.
  • Mediation within 45 days, under the Indiana rules of alternative dispute resolution, and mediation cannot begin until the appraisal is on file (unless the appraisal was waived).
  • Sixty days to agree. The referral order tells the parties the property will be sold if they do not settle inside 60 days, and that they may agree on the method of sale — otherwise it may go to public auction or to the sheriff.
  • Then the sale. Within 30 days after the mediator reports failure (or the parties file an agreed method), the court orders the property sold by the agreed method, or orders the parties to pick an auctioneer. Fail to pick one within 30 days and the court orders the sheriff to sell it the way property is sold at execution under IC 34-55-6.

Notice what is missing: commissioners walking the property, a hearing on whether the house could be split down the middle, an in-kind preference. Indiana kept partition in kind on the books for unusual cases — IC 32-17-4-23 still lets an owner holding both an undivided fee interest and a life estate compel partition and have the fee set off — but for an ordinary co-owned house, the statute is a countdown to a sale with a mediation in the middle of it.

🧾 The Statute Pays Back the Owner Who Paid the Taxes

This is the provision Indiana co-owners should know by heart, and almost nobody does.

Under IC 32-17-4-2.5(j), any person who has paid a tax or special assessment on the property is entitled to pro rata reimbursement from the proceeds of the sale — before the remaining money is divided among the owners by ownership percentage. Subsection (i) does the same for whoever paid for the title search required by IC 32-17-4-2 or the title or lien search required in an estate under IC 29-1-17-11. Subsection (l) then says the court divides what is left, after those reimbursements, the amounts paid to lienholders, and the reasonable expenses of the sale, among the owners in proportion to their interests.

If you are the sibling who has quietly paid the property tax bill for six years on a house four people own, that is not a favor you did for free. It is a statutory claim on the top of the money. The catch is proof: you need the treasurer's receipts, the canceled checks, the ditch or sewer assessment statements. Nobody reconstructs six years of payments for you at the mediation table.

The flip side is worth saying plainly too. Subsection (k) lets any person advertise the sale at their own expense but gives them no reimbursement for it, and nothing in the statute reimburses an owner for repairs, mowing, insurance, or the mortgage. Taxes and assessments are specifically protected; everything else is an equitable argument you have to make.

🙋 If You Want the House, Plan to Be the Buyer

Because Indiana's statute ends in a sale, the owner who wants to keep the property has to stop thinking like a defendant and start thinking like a bidder. Two subsections make that realistic.

First, IC 32-17-4-2.5(m): if a party who owns an interest in the property becomes the successful purchaser — whether through an agreed settlement or at the auction — that person gets full credit based on the percentage of their interest in the property before the purchase. An owner with a one-quarter interest bidding $200,000 is not writing a $200,000 check. They are funding the other owners' three-quarters, roughly $150,000 before costs and reimbursements. That credit is what makes a co-owner competitive against an investor bidding cash.

Second, subsection (d)(2) and (g)(1): the parties may agree on the method of sale, and if they do, the court orders the sale by the method they picked. An agreement that one co-owner buys the others out at the appraised value is a method of sale. You do not have to lose the house to end the case.

Practical sequence if that is your goal: get your lender pre-qualified before mediation, bring your tax receipts, know the appraised number, and offer the appraised value less your own share and your reimbursements. In a Delaware County or Allen County mediation, that offer is usually better than what the other owners would net at a sheriff's sale, and they know it.

🏷️ The Clause That Turns an Auction Into a Listing

Indiana added something in 2016 that most states' partition statutes still lack. Under IC 32-17-4-2.5(n), if the court has ordered the property sold at auction, then at any time before the auction all the parties can inform the court in writing that they want to sell through a real estate professional, that they have jointly selected one, and that they have agreed on a listing price. The court must then rescind the auction order and let the property be listed. If it has not sold by the end of the listing agreement, any party can petition and the court orders the auction back on.

That is the single biggest money lever in an Indiana partition case. A listed sale with photographs, a lockbox and 30 days of market exposure routinely brings 10% to 25% more than an auction on the courthouse steps or a sheriff's sale under IC 34-55-6. It costs nothing but a signature from every party — and because every owner's share of the proceeds goes up, it is the rare thing in these cases that everyone can actually agree to.

⚠️ Everyone has to sign, and the window closes. Subsection (n) requires all parties to tell the court in writing, with a jointly selected professional and an agreed listing price, and it only works before the property is sold at auction. If one owner will not sign, or the auction happens first, Indiana law gives nobody a second chance at the listing route.

💍 Married Co-Owners and the One-Spouse Power of Attorney

Indiana still recognizes tenancy by the entireties between spouses, and that changes who can be forced into anything. Under IC 32-17-3-1, a written contract in which a husband and wife purchase real estate, or lease it with an option to purchase, creates an estate by the entireties, and neither party's interest is severable during the marriage — unless the contract expressly creates a tenancy in common or its tenor shows that was the intent. On the death of either spouse, the survivor is treated as having owned the whole from the start. If the couple divorces while such a contract is in effect, IC 32-17-3-2 gives them the contract interest and the equity in equal shares.

For a co-ownership that includes a married couple, the practical effect is that the couple is one block. A single spouse cannot sell out from under the other, and a creditor of one spouse generally cannot reach entireties property to satisfy that spouse's individual debt.

Two mechanics worth knowing from IC 32-17-3-4: a joint deed by both spouses is sufficient to convey any interest either or both of them hold, whether they hold it as tenants in common, joint tenants or tenants by the entireties. And an executed, recorded power of attorney from one spouse to the other authorizing conveyance lets the spouse holding the power convey the interest by making the deed individually. That is the clean fix when one spouse is deployed, hospitalized, incarcerated or simply out of state at closing — and it has to be recorded, not just signed. 📜 Why So Many Indiana Houses Have Three Owners

Indiana's Transfer on Death Property Act, IC 32-17-14, lets an owner record a transfer-on-death deed naming beneficiaries who take the property automatically at death, outside probate. It is cheap, it avoids a court file, and Indiana families use it constantly.

It also manufactures co-ownership. A parent records a TOD deed naming three children; the parent dies; three adults now own undivided interests in one house, with no will to interpret, no personal representative to make decisions, and no instructions about who gets to live there or who pays the taxes. A TOD deed transfers the asset but not the agreement. Within a year, one child is living there, one wants to sell, one is unreachable, and somebody is paying the tax bill alone.

If probate was opened, the route can be shorter: IC 32-17-4-1 lets the administrator or executor file for partition when the estate's sale of the land is necessary, and the estate's own title or lien search under IC 29-1-17-11 is reimbursable from the proceeds. Indiana also allows unsupervised administration where the will authorizes it or the heirs agree, and a small-estate affidavit procedure for modest estates — currently estates with a gross value of $100,000 or less — which can move a house out of an estate without a full supervised probate. Ask an Indiana probate attorney which one your file qualifies for before you file anything in the partition court. 🧮 The 1% Cap Becomes 2% When Nobody Lives There

Indiana caps property tax bills by how the property is used. The Department of Local Government Finance states it plainly: taxes are capped at 1% of gross assessed value for homestead property, 2% for other residential property and agricultural land, and 3% for other real and personal property. Those caps are constitutional, and the credit that enforces them is applied class by class on the bill.

Here is what that means for co-owners. While a parent lived in the house, it was a homestead: 1% cap, plus the homestead deductions that reduce the taxable value underneath the cap. Once the house is vacant family property that nobody occupies as their principal residence, it is "other residential" — the cap doubles to 2% and the homestead deductions come off. On a $180,000 house, the cap alone moves from about $1,800 to about $3,600 a year, and the loss of the deductions can push the actual bill most of the way there.

Indiana's 2025 property tax law changed how homestead deductions are calculated for bills going forward, so do not take a number off an old bill and assume it still applies. Call the county auditor, ask what deductions are currently on the parcel and what happens when the occupying owner is gone, and get the answer before you decide to hold the house for another year. On the Muncie example below, the tax swing alone is the difference between waiting being cheap and waiting being expensive.

🔗 What One Indiana Co-Owner Can Do Alone

Without the others' signatures, a tenant in common in Indiana can:

  • Sell or mortgage their own undivided interest. A buyer takes that fractional share and steps into the same stuck co-ownership, which is why share buyers discount heavily.
  • File for partition under IC 32-17-4-1 and start the 45-day/60-day clock.
  • Pay the taxes and preserve a pro rata reimbursement claim under subsection (j).
  • Occupy the property, if no agreement or court order says otherwise — though an occupying co-owner can face an accounting for rents received from third parties.

What one co-owner cannot do alone: sell the whole house, give a buyer insurable title to the whole, evict another co-owner, or sign away a co-owner's interest. And a spouse holding property as tenants by the entireties cannot sever it during the marriage at all. 🧭 Four Routes for Indiana Co-Owners

🤝 Buyout at the appraised value, credited for your share

Best if you want to stay

Use the court's appraisal, subsection (m)'s full credit for your own percentage, and your tax reimbursement. Settle it at mediation as the agreed method of sale and skip the auction entirely.

🏷️ Convert the auction to a listing under subsection (n)

Biggest shared win

All parties, in writing, one jointly selected broker, one agreed price. Usually 10–25% more than an auction, which raises every owner's share. Must happen before the property sells at auction.

📄 Agree on a method before anyone files

Cheapest

A written family agreement — appraiser, price, deadline, who pays what, who gets reimbursed — avoids the filing fee, the court-appointed appraiser, the mediator and the title search. Everything the statute does, you can do voluntarily and faster.

💵 Sell the house or your own interest for cash

Fastest and most certain

📋 Six Steps, One Week

  1. Pull the deed from the county recorder and read the vesting language. Tenants in common, joint tenants with survivorship, tenancy by the entireties and a TOD deed under IC 32-17-14 all lead to different answers.
  2. Gather every tax and assessment receipt. Under IC 32-17-4-2.5(j) they are reimbursable from the proceeds before the shares are divided, and the treasurer's payment history is the proof.
  3. Call the county auditor and ask what deductions are on the parcel now, and what the bill becomes if no owner occupies it. The 1% to 2% cap change is the real cost of waiting.
  4. Find out whether a probate was opened, and whether the estate can act. If an executor or administrator needs the sale, IC 32-17-4-1 lets them file.
  5. Get a value you trust — a broker's opinion and a cash offer, so you know both ends of the range before anyone appraises anything.
  6. Put one written proposal in front of the other owners with a method of sale and a deadline. Indiana's statute is going to force a method in 60 days of mediation anyway; choosing it yourselves is cheaper and pays everyone more.

✅ Bottom Line

Indiana does not reward waiting. IC 32-17-4-2.5 appoints an appraiser in 30 days, sends the case to mediation in 45, warns everyone the property will be sold if they do not agree in 60, and then sells it — by an agreed method, by auction, or by the sheriff. A co-owner who plans to stall is planning to be a spectator at a sale.

What the statute does give you is money and a choice. Taxes and assessments you paid come back pro rata off the top. A co-owner who buys gets full credit for their own percentage. And every party together can convert an auction into a listed sale, which is almost always the highest number on the table. Use those three things before the court uses the default.

❓ Questions Indiana Co-Owners Ask Us

Can one co-owner force the sale of a house in Indiana?

In practice, yes. Under IC 32-17-4-1 any joint tenant or tenant in common can file to compel partition, and IC 32-17-4-2.5 then runs a schedule that ends in a sale: an appraiser appointed within 30 days, mediation within 45 days, and an order that tells the parties the property will be sold if they do not agree within 60 days. Indiana does not require proof that the house cannot be divided.

Do I get my money back if I have been paying the property taxes alone?

From a court sale, yes. IC 32-17-4-2.5(j) entitles any person who paid a tax or special assessment on the property to pro rata reimbursement from the sale proceeds before the balance is divided among the owners. Subsection (i) does the same for whoever paid for the required title search. In a private sale or buyout you only get it if the agreement says so, so put it in writing.

If I buy the house at the partition sale, do I pay full price?

No. IC 32-17-4-2.5(m) gives a party who owns an interest in the property and becomes the successful purchaser, by agreed settlement or at auction, full credit based on the percentage of their interest before the purchase. An owner with a one-quarter interest is effectively funding the other three-quarters, which is what makes a co-owner competitive against an outside cash bidder.

Can we list an Indiana house with a real estate agent instead of letting it go to auction?

Yes, if everyone signs. Under IC 32-17-4-2.5(n), at any time before the property is sold at auction, all parties may inform the court in writing that they want to sell through a real estate professional, that they have jointly selected one, and that they have agreed on a listing price; the court must then rescind the auction order. If it does not sell by the end of the listing, any party can ask the court to order the auction.

Who sells the property if we cannot agree on an auctioneer?

The sheriff. Under IC 32-17-4-2.5(g)(2), if the parties fail to select an auctioneer within 30 days of the court's order to do so, the court orders the sheriff to sell the property in the same manner property is sold at execution under IC 34-55-6. That is usually the lowest-price outcome available in an Indiana partition case.

Does an Indiana partition sale wipe out liens on the property?

Mostly. Under IC 32-17-4-2.5(h), the court notifies lienholders and others with an interest identified in the required title or lien search, and the property must be sold free and clear of all liens and special assessments except prescriptive easements, easements of record and irrevocable licenses — with any secured sum satisfied out of the sale proceeds.

Can one spouse sell a house Indiana spouses own together?

Not alone, if it is held by the entireties. IC 32-17-3-1 provides that a written contract in which spouses purchase real estate creates an estate by the entireties and that neither party's interest is severable during the marriage, unless a tenancy in common was expressly created or intended. IC 32-17-3-4 allows one spouse to convey the interest individually when the other has given a recorded power of attorney authorizing it.

How does a transfer-on-death deed create co-ownership in Indiana?

Indiana's Transfer on Death Property Act, IC 32-17-14, lets an owner record a deed naming beneficiaries who take the property at death without probate. When a parent names three children, all three become undivided owners the day the parent dies — with no will, no personal representative and no agreement about who lives there or pays the taxes. It is a common source of stuck Indiana co-ownerships.

Will the property tax bill go up if nobody lives in the house?

Usually. Indiana's Department of Local Government Finance explains that bills are capped at 1% of gross assessed value for homestead property, 2% for other residential property and agricultural land, and 3% for other property. A house that stops being an owner's principal residence loses homestead treatment and its deductions, so the cap doubles from 1% to 2%. Confirm the current deductions with your county auditor.

Is a cash sale better than an Indiana partition case?

Only sometimes. If every owner will sign and the house is sound, an agreed listing nets the most, and if one owner can get a mortgage, a buyout under subsection (m) usually beats a cash offer for everyone. A cash sale makes sense when repairs and carrying costs cannot be funded, the non-homestead tax cap is running, an owner cannot be located, or one owner wants out of the argument now.
