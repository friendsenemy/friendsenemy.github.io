Partition Lawsuits in Arkansas: The Co-Owner Who Files Gets Their Lawyer Paid From Everyone's Share

Arkansas has a fee rule most co-owners never hear about until they're served: when a court orders a house divided or sold, it allows a reasonable fee to the attorney who brought the case, and every owner pays a share of it in proportion to their interest, up to $40,000. That changes the math on whether to file, how to respond, and when to settle. Here's how an Arkansas partition case actually runs. Or ask us a question, free.

You co-own a house or a piece of family land in Arkansas, and one of the owners wants out while another wants to stay. Maybe a sibling lives in Mom's house and won't pay rent. Maybe four cousins own 80 acres in Lee County and one of them got an offer from a timber company. Sooner or later someone says the word "partition," which means asking the circuit court to divide the property or sell it and split the money.

Most partition guides spend their first pages on what partition is. This one starts with the Arkansas rule that changes everyone's math, because it decides whether filing is a cheap threat or an expensive one, and who ends up paying for it.

💼 In Arkansas, Everyone Helps Pay the Filing Owner's Lawyer

Under Ark. Code § 18-60-419, when a court enters judgment for partition in kind or for a sale and division of the proceeds, it "shall allow a reasonable fee to the attorney bringing the suit." The fee is taxed as part of the court costs and paid "pro rata as the other costs are paid according to the respective interests of the parties." In plain terms: if your brother owns half, you own a quarter and a cousin owns a quarter, and your brother's lawyer files, the court can order the lawyer's fee paid out of the sale money half by your brother, a quarter by you and a quarter by your cousin.

The statute puts three limits on that:

  • 🤝 Common-benefit work only. The court considers only services "of common benefit to all parties," such as the petition, service, getting the judgment and running the sale. It assesses no fee for work that helps only one party, like preparing and trying a contested title issue.
  • 🧢 A hard cap. The fee taxed as costs can't exceed $40,000 in total compensation and costs.
  • 🚫 No fee for a lawyer with a stake. If the attorney asking for the fee has an interest in the property, no fee is awarded.

The statute also says these limits don't cap what a lawyer can charge their own client. Your brother may still owe his lawyer more than the court shifts to the group, and if you hire your own lawyer to fight the case, that bill is yours.

⚠️ The warning that matters most: in Arkansas, refusing to deal doesn't keep your money out of the fight. Once a co-owner files and the court orders partition, part of their lawyer's common-benefit fee comes out of your share of the sale. Delay that only runs up common-benefit work costs you too. If a sale is inevitable, an early settlement, a buyout, or a sale of your share usually leaves more in your pocket than litigating to the end.

🧮 Running the Fee Math Before Anyone Files

The fee rule cuts differently depending on which seat you're in.

📝 If you're the owner thinking about filing

Leverage, with limits

Your lawyer's common-benefit work is shared by everyone, so your out-of-pocket exposure is lower than in a state where each side pays its own way. But you only get that sharing if the court enters a judgment of partition, and fees for fighting over title or accusations between owners stay yours. Ask a prospective lawyer how they split common-benefit time from adversarial time.

📬 If you've been served

Settle the sale, fight the accounting

A single house almost never gets divided in kind, so arguing that it shouldn't be sold rarely works. Your money is usually better spent on the parts that change your check: credits for taxes, insurance and repairs you paid, rent owed by an owner who lived there, and making sure the sale method gets a fair price.

🚪 If you just want out

Sell your interest

You can sell your undivided share before or during the case. A buyer steps into your position in the lawsuit, and you're done paying carrying costs and any lawyer. You'll take a discount from your share of market value, so compare it to your realistic net after the case.

👥 Who Can Bring an Arkansas Partition Case, and Against What

Arkansas's petition statute, § 18-60-401, is broad. Anyone with an interest in land held in joint tenancy, in common, in coparceny, "as assigned or unassigned dower" or curtesy, or subject to someone else's life estate can file a written petition in circuit court. The petition describes the property, names everyone with an interest and their shares, and asks for division, or a sale if division can't be made "without great prejudice to the owners." Everyone with an interest who didn't join gets summoned.

Four Arkansas limits are worth knowing:

  • 💔 Divorced spouses and the homestead. Former spouses who held as tenants by the entirety can petition after the divorce, except when the property is a homestead occupied by either of them.
  • 🌲 Outside buyers of big parcels wait three years. Under § 18-60-404, someone who buys an undivided interest in a parcel of at least 10 acres and isn't related to another owner within the fourth degree can't sue for partition for three years, unless they own 50 percent or more.
  • 📜 A will can block it. Under § 18-60-413, no partition or sale of land devised by a will may be made contrary to the testator's expressed intention. If Grandpa's will said the farm stays together until the youngest grandchild turns 25, read it before filing.
  • 🔎 Missing owners can be served by publication. The statute allows constructive service, which is how cases with a cousin nobody has heard from in 20 years still move forward.

🏛️ From Petition to Deed: How the Circuit Court Case Moves

  1. Petition and service. Filed in the circuit court of the county where the land is. Owners are served or, if they can't be found, notified by publication.
  2. Answer. Defendants respond. This is when credits, title questions and any heirs property act request get raised.
  3. Judgment of partition. The court determines each owner's share and orders the property partitioned.
  4. Commissioners. Under § 18-60-414 the court may appoint not fewer than three nor more than five commissioners who live in the county. They report whether the land can be divided fairly. Under § 18-60-424, the court can skip commissioners and order a sale directly if the evidence shows there's no need for them, which is common with a single house.
  5. Sale. If division would cause great prejudice, § 18-60-420 lets the court order the property sold at public auction to the highest bidder, or by a negotiated sale on terms the court sets, "including without limitation by engaging a licensed real estate broker." Distinct farms or lots are sold separately.
  6. Who can't buy. Section 18-60-421 bars a commissioner, or anyone acting for one, from buying, and bars a guardian from buying except for the ward. A sale that breaks the rule is void. The co-owners themselves are not on that list and can bid.
  7. Report, confirmation and distribution. The sale is reported to the court, confirmed, a deed issues, and the proceeds are paid out by share after costs, including the attorney fee allowed under § 18-60-419.

Ask for a broker sale if you're a defendant who expects the house to be sold anyway. A listed house with photos and showings usually brings more than a courthouse-steps auction, and the statute gives the judge that option.

🌳 Inherited Family Land: The 2015 Heirs Property Act Comes First

Arkansas adopted the Uniform Partition of Heirs Property Act in 2015, at Ark. Code § 18-60-1001 and the sections that follow. When the court finds the property is "heirs property," generally land with no binding co-ownership agreement where a significant share is held by relatives or came down through a relative, the act changes the order of events:

  • 📐 The court determines the property's value, usually by appraisal.
  • 💰 The owners who didn't ask for a sale get a chance to buy out the owner or owners who did, at that value.
  • ✂️ If nobody buys, the court looks harder at dividing the land before ordering a sale, which matters for acreage more than for a house lot.
  • 🏷️ If a sale is ordered, the default is an open-market sale through a broker rather than an auction.

For the sibling who wants to keep the family place, that's real protection. For the sibling who wants out, it may simply mean being bought out at the appraised price, which can be a perfectly good result. Our guide to what to do when heirs can't agree covers the family side of this.

While the case runs, someone keeps paying the house's bills. Arkansas assesses property at 20 percent of market value. In Fort Smith, the 2025 millage is 57.5 mills, so a house with a $180,000 assessor's value carries about $2,070 a year in property tax, or $173 a month, without a homestead credit if no owner lives there. Vacant-dwelling insurance often adds $100 to $200 a month, and utilities, lawn care and small repairs $120 to $250. Who paid those bills, and whether an owner lived there rent-free, is exactly what the court sorts out before it divides the money.

💵 A Fort Smith House, Three Ways

Three siblings inherited a house in Fort Smith equally. Updated, it would bring about $210,000. As it stands, with original windows and a tired kitchen, it would list for about $175,000. One sibling lives there; another files for partition.

  • 📈 Settle and list it together: $175,000 less about 7 percent in commission and closing costs leaves roughly $162,750, or about $54,250 each, minus any credit the court or the siblings agree the occupant owes the others.
  • ⚖️ Litigate to a court-ordered sale: if a broker sale brings the same $175,000 a year later, take off the sale costs, the filing attorney's common-benefit fee (say $9,000 to $15,000, shared by thirds), each defendant's own lawyer, and a year of taxes and insurance, about $3,300 to $4,500. Each sibling might net $45,000 to $50,000, later and after a lot of stress.
  • 💵 Sell one-third to a cash buyer now: a third of a house in active litigation might bring $30,000 to $40,000. That's less on paper, but it's in hand in weeks, with no lawyer bill and no more carrying costs.

For comparison, a cash offer on the whole house, if all three agreed, would run 60 to 80 percent of the $210,000 after-repair value, about $126,000 to $168,000. On this house that's below the listing number, so if the siblings can agree, they should list it.

✅ The Arkansas Partition Takeaway

An Arkansas partition case is a reliable way to end co-ownership, and the state's fee rule means the owner who files shares the cost of the common work with everyone else, up to a $40,000 cap. That makes a filing more likely and a long fight more expensive for every owner. The 2015 heirs property act gives family members a buyout first, and the court can order a broker sale instead of an auction.

The cheapest partition is usually the one settled before judgment. If your co-owners will talk, list or buy each other out. If they won't, and you want out, ask us what your share would bring. We'll tell you if waiting for the court looks better.

❓ Frequently Asked Questions

Who pays attorney fees in an Arkansas partition lawsuit?

When the court orders partition in kind or a sale, Ark. Code § 18-60-419 requires it to allow a reasonable fee to the attorney who brought the suit, taxed as costs and paid by all owners in proportion to their interests. Each side still pays its own lawyer for contested, one-sided work.

Is there a cap on the partition attorney fee in Arkansas?

Yes. The fee assessed and taxed as costs under § 18-60-419 cannot exceed $40,000 in total compensation and costs, and no fee is awarded if the attorney has an interest in the property. The cap doesn't limit what a lawyer can charge their own client.

Which Arkansas court hears a partition case?

The circuit court in the county where the land is located. The owner who wants partition files a written petition describing the property and every owner's interest, and all owners who didn't join the petition are summoned.

How many commissioners does an Arkansas court appoint in a partition?

Under § 18-60-414, the court may appoint not fewer than three nor more than five commissioners who live in the county. Under § 18-60-424, it can order a sale without commissioners if the evidence shows they aren't needed, which is common for a single house.

Does an Arkansas partition sale have to be an auction?

No. Under § 18-60-420, when the land can't be divided without great prejudice, the court may order a public auction or a negotiated sale on terms it sets, including by engaging a licensed real estate broker. Defendants who expect a sale often ask for a broker sale.

Can a co-owner buy the house at an Arkansas partition sale?

Generally yes. Section 18-60-421 bars commissioners, people acting for them and guardians (except for their ward) from buying, and makes such sales void. It does not bar the co-owners themselves from bidding.

Has Arkansas adopted the Uniform Partition of Heirs Property Act?

Yes, in 2015, at Ark. Code § 18-60-1001 and following. For qualifying family land, the court determines value, offers the other relatives a chance to buy out the owners who want a sale, considers dividing the land, and prefers an open-market sale over an auction.

Can a will stop a partition of Arkansas land?

It can. Under § 18-60-413, no partition or sale of land devised by a will may be made contrary to the testator's intention as expressed in the will. If the will addresses keeping the property together, have an Arkansas lawyer read it before anyone files.

How long does a partition lawsuit take in Arkansas?

A simple case with all owners found and one house often reaches a sale in about five to nine months. Missing owners served by publication, an heirs property buyout window, or contested credits and title issues can push it to a year or two.

Is it better to sell my share than to go through an Arkansas partition case?

Sometimes. Selling your share gets you out in weeks with no lawyer bill or carrying costs, but at a discount. If the owners can agree to list the house, or you have large credits for taxes and repairs you paid, finishing or settling the case may leave you with more.
