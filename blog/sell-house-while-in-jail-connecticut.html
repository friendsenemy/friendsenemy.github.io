Selling a Connecticut House While Incarcerated: The State's Cost-of-Incarceration Claim, the Two-Witness Power of Attorney and What Happens to the Money

Connecticut is one of the few states with a law letting it recover the cost of someone's incarceration from their property. Reforms in 2022 and 2024 narrowed it a lot, but it still matters when a house is turned into cash. This guide covers what the state can and can't reach, how to sign a valid power of attorney from a DOC facility, and how a sale works through the town land records.

By Dustin Ray, Founder of Pages of Purpose LLC. Not legal advice. Laws differ by state, and Connecticut's incarceration-cost statutes have changed twice since 2022. Talk to an attorney licensed in Connecticut, where the property is, before anyone signs a power of attorney, a sale contract or a deed.

The question families in Connecticut ask us most isn't how to get a signature out of a prison. It's whether the state will take the money. They've heard, usually secondhand, that Connecticut charges inmates for their own incarceration. That's partly true. Connecticut law does give the state a claim for the cost of a person's incarceration, and reforms in 2022 and 2024 narrowed it a great deal. Whether it touches a home sale depends on the offense, the equity and the timing. This guide starts there, because it can change whether and when to sell.

For the basics that apply everywhere, see the national guide to selling a house while incarcerated. What follows is Connecticut-specific.

💵 Connecticut Can Bill an Inmate for the Cost of the Stay

Under Conn. Gen. Stat. 18-85a, the State of Connecticut has a claim against a person for the unreimbursed cost of their incarceration, and any property the person owns may be used to satisfy it, subject to exceptions. The Attorney General enforces the claim by suing in Hartford Superior Court, generally within two years after the person's release or death, unless property was fraudulently concealed.

Two related sections reach money that comes later:

  • 🎁 Inheritances (18-85b(b)). If the person inherits within 20 years of release, the state has a lien for the lesser of its costs or 50 percent of the inheritance. Since 2024 the first $50,000 of an inheritance is exempt, except for people convicted of the most serious offenses listed in the statute.
  • ⚖️ Lawsuit awards (18-85b(a)). The lien on lawsuit proceeds now applies only to people convicted of those same serious offenses.
  • 🕯️ Estates (18-85c). If the person dies within 20 years of release, the state can claim against the estate.

For a family deciding about a house, the point is that turning a home into a bank balance can change how exposed that value is. That doesn't mean you shouldn't sell. It means you should know the rules first.

🛡️ What the State's Claim Can't Reach

Section 18-85a(b) lists property the state can't use to satisfy the claim. The ones that matter for a homeowner:

  • 🏠 Exempt property under 52-352b, which includes Connecticut's homestead exemption of up to $250,000 of equity (fair market value minus liens). If the house is the person's homestead and the equity is under that number, the house itself is protected from this claim.
  • 💰 Up to $50,000 of other assets. Public Act 22-118 added this carve-out in 2022, and it applies to costs incurred before that date too. It doesn't apply to people incarcerated for capital felony, murder with special circumstances, the offense in 53a-54c, or the sexual assault offenses listed in the statute.
  • 🧹 Erased convictions. Since July 1, 2024, the claim ends for time served on crimes that have been erased (18-85a(c)).
  • 🛠️ Wages from designated DOC job programs, and most property acquired after release, other than the inheritances, lawsuit awards and estate claims described above.

⚠️ Most important warning: the homestead exemption protects a house. Whether it still protects the cash after the house is sold is not something to assume. Before a Connecticut owner who is inside, or recently released, sells a house with significant equity, ask a Connecticut attorney how the proceeds will be held and whether the state's claim could reach them. Ten minutes of advice before closing is worth far more than the conversation afterward.

⏱️ Timing: Sell Now, Sell Later, or Hold the Proceeds Carefully

This is where Connecticut differs from its neighbors. In most states the only timing questions are carrying costs and foreclosure. Here the two-year window after release, the offense category and the size of the equity all matter too. Three common situations:

  • 🟢 Modest equity, non-excluded offense. Equity under the homestead figure and other assets under $50,000. The claim is unlikely to reach much, and the usual factors, such as vacancy and repairs, drive the decision.
  • 🟡 Large equity. Equity well above $250,000 is not fully covered by the homestead exemption even before a sale. An attorney should look at the numbers before anyone lists.
  • 🔴 Excluded offense. No $50,000 carve-out, and the inheritance and lawsuit liens still apply. Get legal advice before converting a protected house into unprotected cash.

None of this changes the bills. A vacant house still costs money every month, and a mortgage in default still heads toward foreclosure. If the lender is already sending notices, read our guide on selling to stop a foreclosure, because waiting for perfect tax planning can cost the whole house.

✍️ Two Witnesses and a Notary: Connecticut's Stricter Power of Attorney

Connecticut adopted the Uniform Power of Attorney Act effective October 1, 2016 (Chapter 15c of the General Statutes). Section 1-350d requires the power of attorney to be signed by the principal and witnessed by two witnesses. A notary's acknowledgment makes the signature presumed genuine. For selling land, section 47-5 requires the power to be executed, acknowledged and witnessed the way a deed is, and deeds in Connecticut need two witnesses and an acknowledgment. In practice, for a home sale, plan on two witnesses and a notary.

That's more than many states ask for, and it's the most common reason a Connecticut power of attorney signed in a facility fails. A notary alone isn't enough. Arrange the two witnesses at the same time.

  • 📄 Use the statutory short form in section 1-352. Real property authority is incorporated by reference, and title examiners know the form.
  • 🗃️ Record it with the deed. Under section 47-10, when a deed is signed under a power of attorney, the power is recorded with the deed on the town's land records, unless it's already recorded in that town and the deed refers to it.
  • ✒️ Signature form. Section 47-5(c) shows how the agent signs, with the owner's name followed by the agent's signature as agent.

🏢 One Department, No County Jails

Connecticut is one of a handful of states with no county jails. The Department of Correction runs both the facilities that hold people awaiting trial and the prisons for sentenced people, a system unified in 1968. That simplifies one thing: whether the person was arrested last week or is serving a long sentence, you're dealing with the same department.

We couldn't find a published DOC directive on notary services, so don't assume a staff notary is available. Ask the person's counselor or the facility's records office how to arrange a notary visit, and say up front that you also need two witnesses. Many families do this through the attorney who drafted the power of attorney, who brings a notary and a second witness on a legal visit.

🏛️ The Probate Court Route: A Voluntary Conservator

If the owner is willing but a power of attorney is impractical, Connecticut has an option many states don't. Under section 45a-646, a person can ask the probate court to appoint a voluntary conservator, and the court does not find the person incapable. The court must see the respondent in person, which takes arranging when the respondent is in a DOC facility.

An involuntary conservatorship is different. It requires clear and convincing proof that the person can't manage their affairs because of a mental, emotional or physical condition, and incarceration alone isn't that. Either way, section 45a-656b bars a conservator from selling real property unless the probate court finds, after a hearing, that the sale is necessary or that the person agrees. Probate filing fees for general petitions run about $250, and attorney fees come on top. A power of attorney with two witnesses is faster and cheaper whenever it's possible.

🏘️ Town Land Records, Spouses and the Conveyance Tax

Connecticut has no county government, so deeds and powers of attorney are recorded on the land records of the town where the property is. The town clerk's office is where the closer checks title and records the documents.

Connecticut sellers pay a conveyance tax under section 12-494. For a residential dwelling the state portion is 0.75 percent of the price up to $800,000, 1.25 percent on the part from $800,000 to $2.5 million, and 2.25 percent above that. The municipal tax is 0.25 percent, and certain designated towns can add up to another 0.25 percent. Ask your closing attorney which rate your town charges.

Spouses: Connecticut doesn't have the non-owner-spouse signature rule found in states like Minnesota or Massachusetts. A spouse's statutory rights in property are mainly at death, under section 45a-436. If both spouses are on the deed, both sign, and a lender or title insurer may still ask a non-owner spouse to sign a release. If co-owners other than a spouse are involved, see can a co-owner sell without the others. Connecticut also adopted the Uniform Partition of Heirs Property Act (sections 52-503f to 52-503r), which matters if the house was inherited by several relatives.

🧮 A Bridgeport Colonial, by the Numbers

Say a house in Bridgeport's North End would be worth $300,000 fully repaired. It needs a boiler, windows and bathroom work, about $40,000. The owner is serving three more years, the house is empty, and the equity is well under the homestead figure.

Carrying costs. Connecticut assesses property at 70 percent of market value in most towns, and Bridgeport's mill rate for fiscal 2026-27 is 27.95. On a $300,000 house that's $210,000 assessed and about $5,870 a year, close to $490 a month. Statewide, the Tax Foundation puts Connecticut's effective rate on owner-occupied homes at 1.54 percent, among the higher rates in the country. Add vacant-home insurance at $100 to $200 a month and heat and utilities of $150 to $250, because an unheated house in a Connecticut winter means burst pipes. Holding the house empty easily costs $750 to $950 a month before any mortgage.

Selling costs. On a $220,000 sale, the state conveyance tax is $1,650 and the municipal tax $550, both paid by the seller. A power of attorney drafted by a Connecticut attorney typically costs a few hundred dollars. A conservatorship costs more and takes longer.

The cash offer. Cash buyers usually pay 60 to 80 percent of repaired value. Here that's $180,000 to $240,000. With $40,000 of work, a fair offer would land around $205,000 to $225,000. A retail listing as-is might bring $250,000 or so, less commission and several more months of holding costs. If the house were in good shape, listing would likely net more, and you should do that.

  1. Get a copy of the deed from the town clerk and note who is on title and whether a mortgage is recorded.
  2. Estimate the equity: likely sale price minus the mortgage payoff and any liens.
  3. If the equity is large or the offense might be on the excluded list, book a consultation with a Connecticut attorney about section 18-85a before listing.

✅ Bottom Line in Connecticut

Connecticut adds a question most states don't: what happens to the money. The state has a claim for incarceration costs, but exempt property, including up to $250,000 of homestead equity, is excluded, most people keep another $50,000 of assets, and erased convictions are off the table. For a house with modest equity, the claim rarely changes much. For a house with large equity or an excluded offense, get legal advice before you sell. The mechanics are stricter than in most states: two witnesses and a notary on the power of attorney, recorded with the deed on the town land records, signed in a DOC facility, since Connecticut has no county jails. A house in good shape should be listed. A vacant one needing work, or one facing foreclosure, is where a cash buyer helps.

❓ Connecticut Incarceration Costs, Power of Attorney and Home Sale FAQ

Can the State of Connecticut take money from selling an inmate's house?

It can make a claim. Under Conn. Gen. Stat. 18-85a the state has a claim for unreimbursed incarceration costs, and an inmate's property may be used to satisfy it, except exempt property and, for most offenses, up to 50,000 dollars of other assets. The Attorney General enforces the claim in court.

Is a Connecticut home protected from the cost-of-incarceration claim?

Property exempt under 52-352b is excluded, and that includes the homestead exemption of up to 250,000 dollars of equity. Whether that protection follows the cash after the house is sold is a question to put to a Connecticut attorney before closing.

Who does not get the 50,000 dollar protection from the incarceration-cost claim?

People incarcerated for capital felony, murder with special circumstances, the offense in 53a-54c, or the sexual assault offenses listed in 18-85a. For them the general 50,000 dollar carve-out added in 2022 does not apply.

How long does Connecticut have to pursue the incarceration-cost claim?

The Attorney General generally must bring the action within two years after the person's release or death, unless property was fraudulently concealed. Separate rules reach certain inheritances and lawsuit awards for up to 20 years after release.

Does a Connecticut power of attorney need witnesses to sell a house?

Yes. Conn. Gen. Stat. 1-350d requires two witnesses, and section 47-5 requires a power used to convey land to be executed, acknowledged and witnessed like a deed. Plan on two witnesses and a notary.

Does the power of attorney have to be recorded in Connecticut?

Yes, when an agent signs the deed. Section 47-10 says the power of attorney is recorded with the deed on the land records of the town where the property is, unless it is already recorded there and the deed refers to it.

Are there county jails in Connecticut?

No. The Department of Correction runs both the jails that hold people awaiting trial and the prisons for sentenced people, so every signing is arranged through a DOC facility.

Can a Connecticut probate court appoint someone to sell an inmate's house?

Under 45a-646 a person can ask the probate court for a voluntary conservator, and the court does not find the person incapable. A conservator can sell real property only after a hearing under 45a-656b, so it is slower than a power of attorney.

Who pays the conveyance tax when selling a house in Connecticut?

The seller. The state rate on a residential sale is 0.75 percent of the price up to 800,000 dollars, plus a municipal tax of 0.25 percent, which some designated towns can raise by another 0.25 percent.

Does an erased conviction still count toward the incarceration-cost claim?

No. Since July 1, 2024, section 18-85a(c) ends the state's claim for time served on crimes that have been erased.
