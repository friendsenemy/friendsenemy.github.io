Prison Doesn't Pause the Mortgage. Here's the Clock the House Is Actually On.

The bill keeps coming on the first of the month whether the borrower is home, in county jail, or three years into a prison term. Federal servicing rules give a family about four months before foreclosure can even begin, and what you do inside that window decides whether the equity survives.

By Dustin Ray, Founder of Pages of Purpose LLC. Not legal advice — mortgage servicing rules change, foreclosure law differs from state to state, and every loan has its own terms. Before you sign or file anything, talk to an attorney licensed in the state where the property is.

🤝 Need a buyer, not just answers? Pages of Purpose specializes in buying houses when an owner or co-owner is in jail or prison. See how we handle these sales.

Nothing about an arrest changes a promissory note. There is no federal or state program that suspends mortgage payments because a borrower is locked up, no automatic forbearance, and no rule that makes the lender wait until release. The note says the payment is due, and if it doesn't arrive, the loan goes delinquent on the same schedule it would for anyone else.

What families usually don't know is that the countdown from a missed payment to a foreclosure filing is written into federal law, and it's longer than panic suggests. For most home loans, the servicer has to wait until the loan is more than 120 days past due before it can take the first legal step. That gap is the most valuable thing you have. This guide is about how to spend it: getting the servicer to talk to you, deciding whether the house can realistically be kept, and selling it while there's still equity to sell if it can't.

⏱️ Day 1 to Day 120: What the Servicer Can and Can't Do

Most mortgage servicers are covered by Regulation X, the rules the Consumer Financial Protection Bureau wrote under the Real Estate Settlement Procedures Act (RESPA). Three dates in those rules shape the first four months after a missed payment.

  • 📞 Day 36. Under 12 CFR 1024.39(a), the servicer must make good-faith efforts to reach the borrower live by the 36th day of delinquency, and to tell them about loss mitigation options when appropriate. When the borrower is in a cell, those calls go to a disconnected phone or to whoever is answering it at home.
  • ✉️ Day 45. Under 1024.39(b), a written early intervention notice must go out by the 45th day, listing the servicer's contact person and the kinds of help that may be available. Save it. It names who to send paperwork to.
  • ⚖️ Day 120. Under 12 CFR 1024.41(f)(1), the servicer may not make "the first notice or filing required by applicable law" for a judicial or non-judicial foreclosure until the loan is more than 120 days delinquent. The exceptions are narrow: a foreclosure based on violating a due-on-sale clause, or the servicer joining another lienholder's foreclosure.

Even small servicers, the ones handling 5,000 or fewer loans and exempt from much of Regulation X, still have to honor the 120-day wait; 1024.41(j) carves that rule back in for them.

A second protection sits on top of the first. If a complete loss mitigation application reaches the servicer before that first filing, 1024.41(f)(2) says the servicer can't file until it has evaluated the application and the borrower has been denied (with appeal rights run out), rejected every offer, or failed to perform on an agreement. If the complete application arrives after the filing but more than 37 days before a scheduled sale, 1024.41(g) bars the servicer from moving for judgment or holding the sale until the same conditions are met. The servicer has 30 days to evaluate a complete application received more than 37 days before a sale.

⚠️ "Complete" is the word that matters. A phone call or a half-filled form doesn't stop anything. The foreclosure protections attach only to an application with every document the servicer asked for. When the borrower is inside, getting pay stubs, a signed hardship letter and a signed tax-return authorization out of a facility can eat three or four weeks of the 120 days. Start that paperwork in the first month, not the fourth.

One more thing the 120-day window is not: a promise. Late fees post after the grace period in the note (usually 15 days), the credit reporting starts at 30 days, and arrears grow by a full payment every month. The rule delays the lawsuit or the notice of sale. It doesn't freeze the debt.

And no, the Servicemembers Civil Relief Act doesn't help. Families sometimes hear about the federal law that stops foreclosure on people who can't appear because they're away, but the SCRA protects active-duty military members and certain dependents. Incarceration isn't military service, and no comparable federal shield exists for inmates.

🔐 Getting the Servicer to Talk to Anyone Who Isn't the Borrower

The first wall a spouse, parent or adult child hits is privacy. A servicer will usually confirm nothing about a loan to someone who isn't on it. That's not stubbornness; it's how they avoid giving account details to a stranger. You get past it one of two ways.

Third-party authorization

Almost every servicer has a one-page form, often called a third-party authorization, that lets the borrower name a person the servicer may discuss the account with. It's the quickest fix. Download it from the servicer's website or ask for it by mail, send it in to the borrower with a stamped return envelope, and have it mailed straight back to the servicer. Some servicers want it notarized; many don't. Its limit: it lets the servicer talk to you, but it doesn't let you sign a modification, a short-sale approval or a deed.

A durable power of attorney

A properly executed financial power of attorney lets the agent act, not just listen. Regulation X's official commentary says a loss mitigation application, an information request or a notice of error "submitted by an agent of the borrower" counts as submitted by the borrower, and the servicer may take reasonable steps to confirm the agent's authority. In practice, that means sending a copy of the POA with everything. If a sale is coming, the same POA, executed with the formalities your state requires for recording, can let the agent sign the deed. Our guide to selling a house while incarcerated covers how the POA gets signed inside.

Use the written-request rules

Once you have authority, put requests in writing to the servicer's designated address for information requests (it's on the monthly statement or website). Under 12 CFR 1024.36, the servicer must acknowledge a written information request within five business days and generally answer within 30 business days, or within 10 business days if you're asking who owns the loan. Ask for three things in one letter: the current payoff and reinstatement amounts, the name of the investor (Fannie Mae, Freddie Mac, FHA, VA, a bank's own portfolio), and a list of every document a complete loss mitigation application needs. The investor answer tells you which rulebook the servicer has to follow.

Why "successor in interest" probably isn't you, yet

Regulation X has a special status called "successor in interest," and a confirmed successor is treated as a borrower for nearly all servicing protections even if they never signed the note. But the definition in 12 CFR 1024.31 turns on a transfer of ownership: inheriting after a death, a transfer that makes a spouse or child an owner, a divorce decree, or a move into a living trust. Being married to someone who went to prison, or living in their house, doesn't make you a successor. A recorded deed from the borrower to you might, which is the next section's question.

🏠 Is It Still Their "Principal Residence"? The Quiet Question That Changes Everything

Here's the detail almost no one tells families. The early intervention rule, the continuity-of-contact rule and the loss mitigation protections in 1024.39 through 1024.41 apply only to a mortgage on property that is the borrower's principal residence, under 1024.30(c)(2). The CFPB's commentary adds that if the property stops being the borrower's principal residence, those procedures stop applying, that the answer depends on the facts and state law, and that a vacant property can still be a principal residence.

A servicer is not supposed to treat an arrest as a change of residence, and a borrower serving a short sentence whose spouse and children still live in the house has a strong case that it's still home. The case gets weaker when the house sits empty, the borrower is serving a long sentence, the mail is forwarded and the utilities are off. If a servicer argues the property is no longer a principal residence, the 120-day wait and the protections around a complete application can be contested rather than assumed.

The practical points: keep the house occupied by family if you can, keep the borrower's mail coming to it, and say in writing to the servicer that the borrower intends to return. Be truthful about it. If no one is going to live there for years, don't build a strategy around rules that may not cover you; build one around the state foreclosure timeline below.

💍 Deeding the House to a Spouse or Child: What Garn-St Germain Covers

Families often ask whether the inmate can simply sign the house over to a wife, husband or adult child who's still paying. Most mortgages have a due-on-sale clause that lets the lender demand full payment if the house is transferred. The Garn-St Germain Act, 12 U.S.C. § 1701j-3(d), blocks lenders from enforcing that clause for certain transfers on homes with fewer than five units, including "a transfer where the spouse or children of the borrower become an owner of the property" and a transfer into a living trust in which the borrower remains a beneficiary.

So the deed itself usually won't trigger an acceleration. Note one wrinkle: the OCC's implementing regulation, 12 CFR 191.5(b), frames the spouse-and-child exemption around a transferee "who occupies or will occupy the property." A spouse living in the house fits comfortably. A child who doesn't plan to move in is a weaker fit.

Here's what the transfer does not do:

  • It doesn't take the borrower off the loan. The inmate still owes the debt, and the late payments still hit their credit.
  • It doesn't stop a foreclosure for nonpayment. Garn-St Germain only stops acceleration because of the transfer.
  • It doesn't make the new owner liable on the note unless they formally assume it.

What it can do is make the new owner a potential successor in interest. Once the servicer confirms that status, 1024.30(d) says that person is treated as a borrower for Regulation X purposes: they can get account statements, apply for loss mitigation in their own right, and get answers without a POA for every call. For a spouse who intends to keep the house and can afford it, that is real leverage. For a family that's going to sell anyway, it's usually an unnecessary extra deed, and it can create gift-tax filing questions and, in a few situations, complications with the criminal case or a later creditor claim. Ask a lawyer before anyone records it.

🧾 Forbearance, Modification, Partial Claims: The Honest Odds

Every investor has its own toolbox, and none of them lists incarceration as a hardship by name. The current Fannie Mae/Freddie Mac Mortgage Assistance Application (Form 710) lists unemployment, reduction in income, divorce, disability, death and a handful of others, plus "Other." A borrower who lost a paycheck because they're in prison fits "reduction in income" or "Other." The question isn't whether the hardship qualifies. It's whether any option makes the numbers work.

The honest reading: forbearance buys time to bridge a gap. A 60-day county jail stay, a case that's likely to end in probation, or a borrower who'll be back at work in six months can be bridged. A five-year sentence with no other income in the household can't, because every option ends with someone needing to make a payment. When a servicer approves a forbearance for a borrower who has no realistic way to repay, the family has only postponed the decision and added arrears.

Where it can work is when a spouse or partner still has income. A modification evaluated on household income, especially once that person is a confirmed successor, sometimes produces a payment they can carry. Run that analysis before deciding to sell.

🗺️ After Day 120: How Fast Each State Moves

Once the 120 days pass and loss mitigation is exhausted, state law takes over, and the difference between states is enormous. Our foreclosure guide has the full timeline chart; here's what matters specifically when the owner is locked up.

Non-judicial states: no lawsuit to answer

In Texas, Georgia, California and other states that use deeds of trust, the house is sold by a trustee outside court. In Texas, Property Code § 51.002 requires at least 20 days to cure before acceleration on a residence and at least 21 days' notice before a first-Tuesday sale. The notices go to the borrower's last known address, which means the house where nobody may be opening the mail. A family can easily learn about the sale after it happens. If you're in a non-judicial state, the person with the POA should be watching the mailbox and the county's foreclosure postings every week.

Judicial states: the inmate gets sued

In Florida, New York, Ohio, Pennsylvania and other judicial states, the lender files a lawsuit and has to serve the borrower, which can happen at the facility. Then the clock is a court deadline, often about 20 days in Florida to file a response. An inmate who can't reach a lawyer and ignores the papers is headed for a default judgment. Judicial states move more slowly overall (Florida commonly six months to a year or longer, New York often well over a year), which gives more time to sell, but only if someone answers the complaint or arranges a lawyer to.

Either way, the borrower is entitled to the notices. Make sure the facility address and the agent's address are on file with the servicer in writing so nothing important lands only at an empty house.

🛡️ The Bills That Don't Show Up on the Mortgage Statement

Many families focus on the principal and interest and miss the costs that quietly destroy equity during an incarceration.

⚠️ Check the vacancy clause today. Standard homeowners policies typically exclude vandalism and broken-glass losses once a home has been vacant more than 60 consecutive days, and some carriers cancel or decline to renew. If the inmate lived alone, the house may already be past that line. Call the agent, tell them the truth, and get a vacant-dwelling policy if needed. A fire or burst pipe in an uninsured house can wipe out every dollar of equity.
  • 🏦 Escrowed taxes and insurance. If the loan has an escrow account, the servicer keeps paying the property tax and insurance bills even when payments stop, and adds those advances to what's owed. Under 12 CFR 1024.17(k)(5), a servicer with an escrow account generally must keep paying the borrower's own policy rather than buying force-placed coverage, as long as the policy is still in force.
  • 📄 Force-placed insurance. If the policy lapses, 12 CFR 1024.37 requires two written notices before the servicer can charge for lender-placed coverage: the first at least 45 days before the charge, then a reminder. That coverage protects the lender, not the contents, and often costs several times a normal premium.
  • 🏛️ Property tax without escrow. Unpaid taxes become a lien ahead of the mortgage. Effective rates range from under 0.3% of value in Hawaii to around 2% in New Jersey and Illinois, so a $250,000 house carries roughly $60 to $420 a month.
  • 🏘️ HOA dues. Assessments keep accruing and can be enforced by lien and, in many states, foreclosure. In states with a "super-priority" HOA lien (Nevada and Colorado among them), a slice of unpaid dues can outrank the mortgage.

A realistic monthly carrying cost on a vacant $250,000 house with a $1,400 principal-and-interest payment and a 1.2% tax rate: about $250 in taxes, $150 to $250 for vacant-dwelling insurance, $100 to $150 for minimal utilities, and $100 for lawn and upkeep. That's $2,000 to $2,150 a month, or about $26,000 a year, before late fees and any lawyer.

💵 Selling Before the Auction: Three Exits Compared

If the numbers say the house can't be kept, the next goal is to protect the equity. A foreclosure auction is the worst place for it: bidders pay well below market, fees and interest pile up first, and whatever surplus exists has to be claimed through a process that's hard to run from prison. A sale any time before the auction can pay off the loan, arrears and fees in full and send the rest to the owner.

📈 List it with an agent

Best when there's time and someone outside

Highest price, but it needs an agent willing to work with a POA, someone to handle showings and repairs, and usually 60 to 120 days. In a non-judicial state with a sale already scheduled, there may not be enough runway.

💰 Sell for cash, as-is

Best when the clock is short or the house is rough

Cash buyers typically pay 60% to 80% of the after-repair value, and can close in two to four weeks once the signing authority is in place. You give up price in exchange for certainty and speed.

📉 Short sale or deed in lieu

Only when there's no equity

If the house is worth less than what's owed, the lender has to approve a short sale for less than the payoff, or take the house back with a deed in lieu. Both avoid the auction, but both need lender approval, both take time, and a deed in lieu generally needs clean title with no junior liens. Ask in writing whether any deficiency will be waived.

A worked example

A house would be worth $280,000 fixed up and about $250,000 as-is. The owner owes $150,000, is six months behind on a $1,700 payment ($10,200), and about $2,000 in late fees and inspection charges have posted. Payoff: about $162,000.

  • Cash sale at 70% of after-repair value: $196,000 minus the $162,000 payoff leaves about $34,000 to the owner, with the buyer covering closing costs.
  • Listing as-is at $250,000: minus about 5.5% commission ($13,750), $5,000 in seller closing costs, the payoff, and three more months of carrying costs and interest (about $6,500) leaves roughly $62,000.
  • Foreclosure auction: another $5,000 to $15,000 in fees and interest first, then whatever bidders pay. Any surplus has to be claimed, often by petition, and can take months to reach the owner.

On these numbers, listing wins by about $29,000, and if there's a reliable person outside and a few months before a sale date, that's the move. Cash wins when there's no one to manage a listing, the sale date is close, the house needs work a retail buyer won't accept, or the payoff is so near the value that a slow listing would eat the margin. Run both, in writing, before choosing.

🔒 Where the Equity Goes After Closing

Sale proceeds belong to the owner on title, but they may not stay in the owner's hands. Several claims can reach them first.

  • Federal restitution and fines. Under 18 U.S.C. § 3613(c), a federal restitution order is a lien on all of the defendant's property as if it were a federal tax lien, and it lasts 20 years. If notice of it has been filed, the title company will require it be paid from the sale. Separately, 18 U.S.C. § 3664(n) says a person who owes restitution and receives "substantial resources" during incarceration must apply them to the restitution still owed.
  • Child support. Federal law requires states to make overdue support a lien on the obligor's real and personal property, and title searches routinely catch recorded support liens.
  • State restitution, court costs and judgments. Recorded judgments attach to real estate in most states and get paid at closing.
  • Inmate trust accounts. Many prison systems take a percentage of deposits for fees and restitution, and the Bureau of Prisons' Inmate Financial Responsibility Program does the same for federal inmates. Depositing a large check to a commissary account is usually the wrong move.

The proceeds should go to an account in the owner's name, managed by the agent under the POA and spent only for the owner's benefit. That's not a way to hide money from a lawful claim; a court order or statute that reaches the funds still reaches them, and pretending otherwise invites new trouble. Talk to the defense lawyer about restitution before closing. Our federal prison guide covers federal liens in more detail.

📋 What To Do This Week

  1. Find the last mortgage statement. Note the servicer, loan number, due date, payment, and whether taxes and insurance are escrowed.
  2. Get authority on paper. Mail the servicer's third-party authorization to the borrower now. Start a durable POA in parallel if a sale or a modification is possible.
  3. Count the days. Figure out how many days delinquent the loan is today. If it's past 90, assume the 120-day mark is close and act on everything below this week.
  4. Send one written information request. Ask for payoff, reinstatement amount, investor, and the full list of loss mitigation documents.
  5. Call the insurance agent. Confirm the policy is active and whether the vacancy clause is a problem.
  6. Decide keep or sell honestly. If someone can make the payment within a year, apply for loss mitigation. If not, get a real agent's opinion of value and a written cash offer, and compare the net.
  7. Check for liens. Ask a title company or the defense lawyer about restitution, child support and judgments before you count on the equity.

✅ Bottom Line

Prison changes nothing about what's owed and everything about who can deal with it. The federal 120-day wait, the protection for a complete application, and Garn-St Germain's spouse-and-child exemption are real tools, but each one has a condition: written authority, a complete file, an occupied home, a family member who can carry the payment. If those conditions are met, fight to keep the house. If they aren't, sell before the foreclosure sale, list it when there's time and a capable person outside, and take a cash offer only when the clock or the condition makes listing unrealistic. ❓ Frequently Asked Questions

Do mortgage payments stop or pause when the borrower goes to prison?

No. No federal or state rule suspends a mortgage because the borrower is incarcerated. Payments stay due on the original schedule, late fees post after the grace period, and the loan becomes delinquent like any other. A servicer may agree to a forbearance, but only if someone applies and the investor's rules allow it.

How long before the lender can start foreclosure on an incarcerated borrower's house?

For most home loans, Regulation X (12 CFR 1024.41(f)) bars the servicer from making the first foreclosure notice or filing until the loan is more than 120 days delinquent. After that, state law controls: a non-judicial state like Texas can reach a sale within weeks, while judicial states like Florida or New York usually take many months.

Can I call the mortgage company for my husband or son who is in prison?

Only with written authority. Most servicers have a third-party authorization form the borrower can sign so the servicer can discuss the account with you. To sign applications, approvals or a deed for the borrower, you need a valid durable power of attorney; Regulation X treats requests and applications from a borrower's agent as coming from the borrower.

Does the Servicemembers Civil Relief Act or any other law protect inmates from foreclosure?

No. The SCRA protects active-duty military members and certain dependents, not people who are incarcerated. There is no federal law that pauses foreclosure for inmates. The protections that do apply are the ordinary ones every borrower gets: the 120-day wait, loss mitigation rules, and state foreclosure procedures.

Will the bank call the loan due if the inmate deeds the house to a spouse or child?

Generally no. The Garn-St Germain Act (12 U.S.C. 1701j-3(d)) bars lenders from enforcing a due-on-sale clause on a transfer that makes the borrower's spouse or children owners of a home with fewer than five units. The transfer doesn't remove the borrower from the loan or stop a foreclosure for missed payments, and the federal regulation ties the exemption to a transferee who occupies the home.

Does incarceration count as a hardship for mortgage forbearance?

It isn't listed by name. The Fannie Mae/Freddie Mac Mortgage Assistance Application lists hardships such as reduction in income and Other, which an incarcerated borrower's lost income can fit. The harder question is repayment: Fannie Mae caps forbearance at 12 months cumulative, so a long sentence with no household income rarely ends in a workable plan.

Does homeowners insurance cover a house that is empty because the owner is in prison?

Often only partly. Standard homeowners policies typically exclude vandalism and glass breakage once the home has been vacant more than 60 consecutive days, and some insurers cancel or refuse to renew. Tell the insurer the truth and buy a vacant-dwelling policy if no one is living there, or the lender may add expensive force-placed coverage.

Can an inmate be served with a foreclosure lawsuit in prison?

Yes. In judicial foreclosure states the lender must sue and serve the borrower, and service can happen at the facility. Response deadlines are short, about 20 days in Florida, and ignoring the papers usually leads to a default judgment. In non-judicial states there's no lawsuit, so notices may go only to the property address.

If the house sells, can restitution or child support take the proceeds?

Yes, often. A federal restitution order is a lien on all of the defendant's property for 20 years under 18 U.S.C. 3613(c), and 18 U.S.C. 3664(n) requires substantial resources received during incarceration to be applied to restitution. Recorded child support liens and court judgments are usually paid at closing before the owner gets anything.

Is a short sale or deed in lieu better than letting the house go to foreclosure?

When there is no equity, usually yes, because both avoid an auction and the fees that come with it. Both need lender approval and take time, and a deed in lieu typically requires clean title. When there is equity, a regular sale or a cash sale before the auction is better, because the owner keeps what's left after the payoff.
