Selling a Co-Owned Portland House When the Other Owner Won't: Oregon Reads Your Deed Literally

Oregon abolished joint tenancy. So the three or four words after your names on the deed (nothing at all, "with right of survivorship," or "as spouses") decide whether you can sell your share, force a sale, or neither. And if it ends in court, Oregon's partition statute still sends the house to a public auction run by referees. Here's how that shapes a smart exit from a Portland house you own with someone else.

By Dustin Ray, Founder of Pages of Purpose LLC. Not legal advice. Oregon property and partition law is technical, Multnomah County practice changes, and laws differ by state. Before you file, sign, or send a demand letter, talk to an attorney licensed in Oregon, where the property is.

You own a house in Portland with someone else: a brother, an ex, a former partner, a friend you bought with in 2015, or cousins you inherited it with. You want out. They don't, or they won't answer. Maybe they live in it rent-free. Maybe it sits empty on a street in Lents or St. Johns while the water bill keeps coming.

Most online advice says one co-owner can always force a sale through a partition lawsuit. In Oregon that's often true, but not always, and even when it is, how an Oregon partition sale actually happens should change how you approach the other owner. Start with the deed.

📜 Start With the Exact Words on Your Oregon Deed

Oregon is unusual. Under ORS 93.180, a conveyance to two or more people creates a tenancy in common unless it clearly and expressly says otherwise. Oregon also abolished joint tenancy: a deed that says only "as joint tenants" is treated as a tenancy in common unless it also shows an intent to create survivorship. A conveyance to two people married to each other creates a tenancy by the entirety unless it says otherwise.

Pull the recorded deed from Multnomah County Records (or Washington or Clackamas County, if the house is in Beaverton, Hillsboro, Milwaukie or Lake Oswego) and find the words right after the owners' names. They'll put you in one of three positions.

🔗 If your deed has a right of survivorship

Oregon REALTORS® guidance on Oregon co-ownership puts it plainly: with a right of survivorship, neither owner can end it on their own; both have to agree to convert to a tenancy in common. That's a real constraint. If your deed has survivorship language and the other owner won't cooperate, a buyer for "your share" is buying something that could vanish if you die first. Expect few takers and a low price. Have an Oregon real estate attorney read the deed and advise whether a partition case is realistic before you spend money on one.

💍 If you're married to the other owner

A tenancy by the entirety can't be partitioned or conveyed by one spouse alone, and a creditor of just one spouse generally can't force a sale. The exit is the divorce. When an Oregon judgment of dissolution is entered, the entirety tenancy becomes a tenancy in common, and the divorce court usually decides who keeps or sells the house anyway. If that's you, our guide to selling a house during a divorce is the better starting point.

🔨 Oregon Partition Still Ends in a Referee's Public Auction

If you're tenants in common, ORS 105.205 lets any co-owner bring a suit for partition in circuit court (Multnomah County Circuit Court for a Portland house) and ask for a sale of the property. What happens next is the part to plan around.

  • 🏘️ Division is the starting point. If the court doesn't order a sale, it appoints three referees to physically divide the property. For a single house on a Portland lot, that rarely works, so the court can order a sale when division "cannot be made without great prejudice to the owners."
  • 📣 The sale is an auction. Under ORS 105.280, the referees sell by public auction to the highest bidder, conducted like an execution sale. The referees report the sale, and the court confirms it before the deed issues.
  • ⚖️ Costs come off the top. Under ORS 105.405, reasonable costs, including attorney fees for work done for the common benefit, are shared in proportion to each owner's interest and become a lien on their shares. Sale proceeds pay costs first, then liens, then the owners.
  • 💰 A buyout escape hatch. ORS 105.210 lets the court, if the property can be neither divided nor sold without great prejudice, value each interest and allow one owner to pay off another.

Here's why this matters for Portland in particular. More than 20 states have adopted the Uniform Partition of Heirs Property Act or similar rules that push court-ordered sales toward a broker listing at appraised value. Oregon hasn't, as of this writing. The Oregon Law Commission has been studying a heirs-property partition proposal, but the statutes on the books still call for a referee's auction. Auctions of occupied, unrepaired houses with a cloud of litigation over them seldom bring full market value.

⚠️ Treat partition as leverage, not as the plan. For most Portland co-owners, the most valuable thing a partition filing does is get the other owner to the table. Owners who ride the case all the way to a referee's auction typically end up splitting a lower price after both sides' attorneys and the referees are paid. Get a written settlement offer on the table before, or right after, you file.

🤝 The Negotiated Exits That Usually Beat the Auction

🏡 Agree to list it together

Best when you can get both signatures

If the other owner will sign a listing agreement, even reluctantly, a Portland agent selling a reasonably maintained house on the open market will almost always net both of you more than any cash buyer or auction. Agree in writing on the list price, price reductions, who pays which repairs, and how credits (taxes or mortgage paid by one owner) come out of the proceeds.

💵 Buyout by the owner who wants to stay

Best when one owner lives there and can refinance

Use a single appraisal both sides accept, subtract a share of the selling costs the buyout avoids, and credit whoever carried more of the mortgage, taxes and repairs. The staying owner refinances or brings cash. This is what ORS 105.210 does in court, done faster and cheaper by agreement.

🏷️ Sell the whole house for cash, as-is

Best when the house needs work or the other owner just wants it over

Older Portland houses often carry knob-and-tube wiring, an oil tank in the yard, a sewer lateral at the end of its life, or an unpermitted basement unit. Some co-owners can't agree to spend money on any of that. A cash sale skips repairs and can close in a few weeks. Expect to give up price for that convenience.

🧾 Sell only your share

Best when the other owner won't negotiate and you're tenants in common

Your undivided interest is yours to sell. The buyer steps into your shoes, including the dispute and the right to seek partition, so the price is well below your proportional slice of the market value. It gets you out without a lawsuit in your name.

Take a 1920s bungalow in outer Southeast that would sell for about $550,000 fully updated. It needs about $60,000 of work: an electrical upgrade, a sewer lateral, a decommissioned oil tank and a kitchen. Two siblings each own half as tenants in common.

🧾 Portland Costs That Pile Up While You Wait

Oregon's property tax system softens one cost. Under Measure 50, tax is charged on assessed value, which is capped and generally grows no more than 3% a year, so long-held Portland houses often have an assessed value well below market. Unlike in some states, a sale doesn't reset the assessment. A buyer inherits the capped value, which helps resale. Typical Portland bills run around 1% to 1.2% of market value, though the rate varies by levy code area.

  • 🏛️ Property tax: roughly $450-$550 a month on a $550,000 house, split by ownership share unless one owner is carrying it.
  • 💧 Water, sewer and stormwater: Portland's combined utility bills are among the higher ones in the country, and fixed charges continue on a vacant house. Budget $120-$200 a month.
  • 🛡️ Vacant-home insurance: standard homeowner's coverage often lapses after a house sits empty for a stretch; a vacant policy commonly costs $150-$300 a month.
  • 🔒 Security and upkeep: boarding, yard care, and winter freeze protection, $100-$250 a month. Vacant houses in some Portland neighborhoods attract break-ins and squatters fast.

That's roughly $800 to $1,300 a month, or $10,000 to $15,000 a year, before any attorney fees. A contested Oregon partition commonly costs each side $10,000 to $30,000 or more in legal fees, part of which may be shifted to the shared pot under ORS 105.405.

✈️ If one co-owner lives out of state

Under ORS 314.258, when a nonresident individual sells Oregon real estate, the escrow agent generally must withhold for Oregon income tax the lowest of 4% of the price, 8% of the gain, or the net proceeds. Exemptions apply, including sales of $100,000 or less, sales qualifying for the federal home-sale exclusion, and a written affirmation that the seller is unlikely to owe Oregon tax. It's a credit against Oregon tax, not a penalty, but it surprises co-owners who moved away. One more Oregon point: Oregon's constitution has barred new real estate transfer taxes since 2012, so a Multnomah County sale carries no county transfer tax. Washington County's older one still applies there.

🏠 When the Other Owner Lives in the House

This is the most common Portland version of the problem. One co-owner lives there, pays little or nothing, and has no reason to sell. As a tenant in common, each owner has a right to occupy the whole house, so the occupying owner usually isn't trespassing just by living there. What you can do:

  • 📨 Send a written demand to share the house, rent your share, or agree on a sale. A refusal to let you in can change the legal picture.
  • 📒 Keep records of every tax, insurance and mortgage payment you make. In an accounting at partition, those payments can be credited.
  • 🧾 Price a buyout using an appraisal, then offer it. An occupying owner who can refinance often prefers that to an auction.

📋 What To Do This Week

  1. Get the recorded deed and read the words after the owners' names. That decides almost everything above.
  2. Pull the Multnomah County tax record for real market value, assessed value, and whether taxes are current.
  3. Check the mortgage: who's on the loan (not just the deed), and whether it's current.
  4. Total what each owner has paid since you took title: mortgage, taxes, insurance, repairs.
  5. Get one appraisal or broker opinion both owners can see.
  6. Make a written offer to the other owner: list together, buy out, or sell together for cash, with numbers for each.
  7. Only then talk to an Oregon attorney about partition, if the other owner won't engage.

✅ Bottom Line

In Oregon, your deed's exact wording decides your options: a tenancy in common can be sold or partitioned, a right of survivorship can't be undone alone, and a tenancy by the entirety waits for the divorce court. If partition is open to you, remember that Oregon's statute still ends in a referee's public auction, so a negotiated listing, buyout, or sale nearly always leaves both owners with more.

If you and the other owner can sign together and the house is in decent shape, list it in Portland's market. ❓ Frequently Asked Questions

Can one co-owner force the sale of a house in Oregon?

If the owners hold as tenants in common, generally yes. Any co-owner can file a partition suit under ORS 105.205 and ask the court to order a sale when the property can't be divided without great prejudice to the owners. Spouses holding as tenants by the entirety can't force a partition while they are married.

Does Oregon still have joint tenancy?

No. ORS 93.180 abolished joint tenancy in real property. A deed that says only joint tenants creates a tenancy in common unless it also clearly expresses a right of survivorship.

Can I sell my share of a Portland house without the other owner?

If you are a tenant in common, yes. You can sell your undivided interest to anyone, and the buyer takes your place as co-owner. Expect a price well below your proportional share of market value. If the deed includes a right of survivorship or you hold as tenants by the entirety, selling alone is restricted or impractical.

How is a partition sale conducted in Oregon?

Under ORS 105.280, referees appointed by the court sell the property by public auction to the highest bidder, in the manner of an execution sale. The referees report the sale and the court must confirm it before the conveyance.

Has Oregon adopted the Uniform Partition of Heirs Property Act?

Not as of this writing. The Oregon Law Commission has been studying a heirs property partition proposal, but the partition statutes in ORS chapter 105 still provide for referees and a public auction.

Who pays the attorney fees in an Oregon partition case?

Under ORS 105.405, reasonable costs, including attorney fees for services performed for the common benefit, are shared by the parties in proportion to their interests and become a lien on their shares. Fees for one side's own disputes are usually that side's responsibility.

My co-owner lives in the house and won't pay rent. What can I do in Portland?

A tenant in common generally has the right to occupy the whole property, so living there alone isn't automatically wrongful. Send a written demand to share use, rent your share, or sell; keep records of the expenses you pay; and if that fails, partition lets the court account for payments and occupancy when dividing proceeds.

What happens to a tenancy by the entirety after an Oregon divorce?

A judgment of divorce or annulment converts it into a tenancy in common. Each former spouse then holds a share that can be sold, and the divorce judgment usually decides who keeps or sells the house.

Will Oregon withhold taxes if a co-owner lives out of state?

Often. ORS 314.258 generally requires the escrow agent to withhold the lowest of 4 percent of the price, 8 percent of the gain, or the net proceeds when a nonresident sells Oregon real estate, unless an exemption applies, such as a sale of 100,000 dollars or less.

Is listing or a cash sale better for a co-owned Portland house?

If both owners will sign and the house is in reasonable condition, listing on the open market usually nets more. A cash sale fits when the house needs major work, the owners can't agree on repairs or pricing, or speed and certainty matter more than the last dollar.
