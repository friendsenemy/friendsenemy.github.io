Heirs Can't Agree on a Georgia House? Georgia's Heirs Property Law Gives the Family a Buyout First

In Georgia, a sibling who wants out of an inherited house can't simply file suit and send it to the courthouse steps. Since 2013, Georgia's version of the Uniform Partition of Heirs Property Act has given the heirs who want to keep a family house an appraisal and a window to buy the others out first. Here is how that law, the probate court's sale rules and Year's Support fit together, and when listing beats a cash offer. Or ask us a question, free.

The Georgia version of this story usually starts a long time ago. A grandparent bought a house in Albany, Augusta, Savannah or a small town outside Macon, died without a will, and nobody ever opened an estate. The children shared it informally. Some of them died too. Today the title is split among eight or twelve cousins, a few of whom have never seen the place, one of whom lives in it, and one of whom wants to sell.

Georgia has a name for that situation, heirs property, and a law written specifically for it. The law changes the most important question in an heirs dispute. In many states the question is "can I force a sale?" In Georgia, once a court finds the house is heirs property, the question becomes "will anyone in the family buy out the people who want to sell, at an appraised price, inside the statutory window?" Only if the answer is no does a sale come into view.

🌳 Georgia's Heirs Property Law Puts a Family Buyout Ahead of Any Sale

Georgia enacted the Uniform Partition of Heirs Property Act in 2012 (House Bill 744), effective January 1, 2013, and codified it at O.C.G.A. 44-6-180 through 44-6-189.1. Nevada was the first state to adopt the uniform act; Georgia followed the next year, ahead of most of the country. The law exists because heirs property is common here: a study of ten Georgia counties outside metro Atlanta, cited by the Georgia Heirs Property Law Center, found heirs property made up 11% to 25% of the acreage in individual counties, worth about $2.15 billion in assessed value.

Here is what the act does once someone files a partition case in superior court:

  1. 🔍 The court must decide first whether it's heirs property. Under 44-6-181, the act applies when there's no binding agreement among the co-owners about partition, at least one co-owner inherited from a relative, and family members together hold a large enough share (the definition in 44-6-180 uses 20% thresholds). In Faison v. Faison (2018), a trial court's ruling was reversed because it skipped that determination.
  2. 🧾 A neutral appraisal. Under 44-6-184, the court orders an appraisal by a disinterested Georgia-licensed appraiser unless all co-owners agree on the value. The parties get notice within 10 days and 30 days to object, and the court then holds a valuation hearing.
  3. 🙋 A 45-day buyout window. Under 44-6-185, co-owners who did not ask for a sale have 45 days after notice of the value to elect to buy the interests of those who did, at the appraised value times each seller's fractional share. Payment comes on a schedule the court sets, at least 60 days after notice.
  4. 🧭 Only then, partition or sale. If no one buys, the court looks at whether the land can be physically divided, and if it orders a sale, the uniform act's default is an open-market sale rather than a courthouse auction.

What this means in practice: the cousin who wants cash does not get a fire-sale auction, and the cousin who lives in the house gets a real chance to keep it, if they can raise the money. It also means a Georgia partition of heirs property takes longer than a simple partition between two business partners. Appraisal, objections, a valuation hearing, a 45-day election, a payment period and then a listing period can easily stretch past a year.

The warning that matters most: if you live in an inherited Georgia house and a relative files for partition, don't ignore the papers and don't assume the house is safe because "it's family land." The protections in the heirs property act only help you if you show up, ask the court to apply it, and are ready to elect within the 45 days. A co-owner who misses the window can lose the right to buy.

🔑 Who Holds Title Right Now: The Heirs or an Administrator?

Before anyone can talk about selling, Georgia families need to know who has legal control. The answer depends on whether an estate has been opened.

When someone dies without a will, O.C.G.A. 53-2-7 vests title to their real estate in the heirs immediately, "subject to divestment by the appointment of an administrator." So in that grandmother's house nobody probated, the cousins already own it, in shares set by Georgia's intestacy rules. But the moment the probate court appoints an administrator, title moves to the administrator, who gets the right to possess the whole estate and can even recover possession from heirs, until the administrator assents to returning it.

That has two practical consequences for a family that disagrees:

  • 🏛️ Opening an estate changes the balance of power. A sibling who can't get everyone to sign a deed can petition the probate court to be appointed administrator. Once appointed, they hold title for the estate. The fight over who should be administrator is often the real fight.
  • 📜 Without an estate, every heir must sign. If nobody opens an estate, a buyer and a title insurer will want a deed from every heir, plus proof of who the heirs are. One missing cousin is enough to stop a sale.

🤝 Georgia's Shortcuts Only Work When Everyone Agrees

Georgia has two efficient ways to deal with an inherited house, and both require unanimity:

  • 📄 "No administration necessary." Under O.C.G.A. 53-2-40, when there's no will and no personal representative, an heir can petition for an order that no administration is necessary. The petition must show the estate owes no debts (or that creditors consent or will be served) and that the heirs have agreed on how to divide the estate among themselves. The resulting order, under 53-2-41, confirms that title vests in the heirs according to their shares or their filed agreement. If even one heir won't agree to the division, this route is closed and the family is headed toward a full administration.
  • 🖋️ A grant of powers. Under 53-7-1(b), the heirs (or beneficiaries under a will) may by unanimous consent authorize the probate court to grant the personal representative the powers listed in 53-12-261, including the power to sell property at a public or private sale without court approval. The grant happens only after a citation is published and no objection is filed, or any objection is withdrawn or dismissed.

A will can also grant those powers directly; many Georgia wills incorporate 53-12-261 by reference. If the will does, an executor can usually list and sell the house without asking the heirs. If the will doesn't, or there's no will, the family either gets everyone's consent or goes the slower route below.

📨 Selling Over an Objection: The Probate Court Petition to Sell

An administrator without 53-12-261 powers can still sell the house. Under O.C.G.A. 53-8-13, the personal representative petitions the probate court, describing the property, the purpose of the sale, and the proposed price and terms, and naming every heir or beneficiary. The court issues a citation, the heirs are served, and anyone can file an objection. After a hearing on any objection, the court decides whether to allow the sale. The representative then reports the sale back to the court in a return.

Three Georgia details are worth knowing:

  • ⚖️ An objection gets a hearing, not a veto. The court can approve the sale over a sibling's objection if it's in the estate's interest.
  • 🔗 Liens can move to the money. The court can transfer liens from the property to the sale proceeds, which helps when the house has tax or judgment liens.
  • 📝 No warranty deed. Under 53-8-14, a personal representative can't bind the estate to any warranty, so the buyer receives a fiduciary's deed and relies on title insurance.

Probate court costs are set statewide by O.C.G.A. 15-9-60, which since January 1, 2025 charges $175 per petition and $75 per motion, response, citation or caveat. The filing fees are modest. The real cost of a contested sale is the attorney time and the months it adds, typically several months for service, objections and a hearing.

👩‍👧 Year's Support Can Put the House Ahead of Every Heir

Georgia has one more rule that surprises families from other states. Under O.C.G.A. 53-3-1, a surviving spouse and minor children are entitled to Year's Support: property set apart for their support for twelve months, with or without a will. The statute makes it "preferred before all other debts or demands," with limited exceptions. The spouse or children apply in probate court, describing the property they want.

Year's Support matters in an heirs dispute because the property applied for can be the house itself. Under 53-3-7, if nobody objects, the court sets apart what was requested. If someone objects, the court sets apart an amount sufficient to maintain the family's previous standard of living, considering their other resources, the estate's ability to pay its debts and other relevant factors, and the applicant has to prove the need. A stepmother who receives the house as Year's Support takes it out of the pool the adult children expected to share.

For children of an earlier marriage, the lesson is to read the probate file early. A Year's Support petition has a citation and an objection period, and objecting is how you get the court to weigh the family's actual needs. For a surviving spouse who wants to stay, Year's Support is often more powerful than any argument about shares.

🧮 Without a Will: The Spouse Never Gets Less Than a Third

When there's no will, O.C.G.A. 53-2-1 gives a spouse with no children the whole estate. With children, the spouse shares equally with them, but the spouse's portion is never less than one-third. A widow with five children takes one-third, and the children split two-thirds. Children of a parent who died earlier take that parent's share. In multi-generation heirs property, those fractions compound quickly, and some cousins can end up with interests of 1/48 or smaller, which is one reason Georgia families often can't find or persuade every owner.

Georgia also has no estate or inheritance tax for deaths after July 1, 2014, so unlike some neighboring states, the heirs aren't racing a state tax deadline.

🗺️ Two Courthouses, Two Different Fights

Georgia splits inherited-house disputes between two courts, and knowing which one you're in tells you a lot:

  • 🏛️ Probate court handles administration, Year's Support, "no administration necessary" petitions, grants of powers and petitions to sell under 53-8-13. Every county has one.
  • ⚖️ Superior court handles partition. Under O.C.G.A. 44-6-160, any co-owner can petition the superior court in the county where the land is. The heirs property act lives here too.

A sale through the probate court is usually faster and cheaper while the estate is open. A partition in superior court is usually slower but can be brought years after the death by any co-owner, and it is where the heirs property buyout applies. Families sometimes end up in both: an administrator sells through probate court, while a co-owner who already received a deed files partition in superior court. 🛠️ Practical Ways Georgia Families Get Unstuck

Map the heirs before you argue about the house

Do this first

For old heirs property, build a family tree back to the person on the deed, with every death, marriage and child. Until you know who owns what fraction, you don't know who must sign, who could elect to buy, or how small some interests are. Many disputes shrink once the family sees the real numbers.

Negotiate a buyout at an agreed appraisal

Often the best outcome

Georgia's act already tells everyone what a court would likely do: appraise the house and let the stay-put heirs buy out the sellers. Doing that voluntarily, with one appraisal everyone accepts and deeds from the selling heirs, saves a year of litigation and the fees that come with it.

Open an estate and let the probate court decide

When the death is recent

If no estate exists and the family can't agree, petitioning for letters of administration gives one person authority, a court to supervise them, and a path to a court-approved sale under 53-8-13. It works best when the family's dispute is about control and timing rather than about whether to keep the house.

Sell your own interest

Fast, at a discount

Each heir can generally sell or deed their own undivided interest. The buyer steps into the family dispute, including any later heirs property buyout at the appraised value, so offers for fractional interests are well below a proportional share. It suits an heir who needs money now and doesn't want a role in a two-year case.

💰 What a Georgia Standoff Costs, Worked Out

Consider a brick ranch in a Georgia county seat that would be worth about $220,000 fixed up. It needs an HVAC system, a roof and updated electrical: about $35,000 of work.

Monthly cost of holding it (check your county tax commissioner's bill; these are illustrations):

  • 🧾 Property tax. Georgia assesses homes at 40% of fair market value, and the Department of Revenue reports average combined county and city millage of about 30 mills. On $220,000, that's $88,000 assessed × 0.030 = about $2,640 a year, or roughly $220 a month. The standard $2,000 homestead exemption requires the owner to live there on January 1, so it typically drops off once a deceased parent's house sits empty or is held by heirs who live elsewhere.
  • 🛡️ Vacant-dwelling insurance: about $100 to $200 a month, since standard policies often exclude long vacancies.
  • 💡 Utilities to keep the air on through a Georgia summer and prevent mold: $100 to $175 a month.
  • 🌿 Yard, pest control and small repairs: $75 to $150 a month.

Total: roughly $495 to $745 a month, or $6,000 to $9,000 a year. A contested partition under the heirs property act, with an appraisal, a valuation hearing and attorneys for multiple sides, can add $10,000 to $30,000 or more in combined fees over its life, and the court can allocate costs among the owners.

What a cash buyer pays. Cash offers typically land around 60% to 80% of after-repair value. With $35,000 of work, a fair offer here might be about $145,000 to $160,000, call it $150,000 (68%). Listing the house as-is to retail buyers might bring $185,000 to $200,000 if every heir signs, less about 6% in selling costs, or roughly $175,000 to $188,000 net.

So if your family can agree, list it; the difference is real. The math moves toward a cash sale when the alternative is an 18-month partition case: eighteen months at $600 a month is about $10,800, plus fees, plus the risk that the house deteriorates while the family waits.

🚫 When a Cash Buyer Is the Wrong Call

We'd rather lose a deal than sell a Georgia family the wrong outcome. Don't sell to a cash buyer if all the heirs will sign and the house is in livable condition; list it. Don't sell if someone in the family wants the house and can finance a buyout at an appraised price; Georgia's law was designed for exactly that family, and keeping the home may matter more than the money. And if you're the heir living in the house and a cousin has filed partition, talk to a Georgia attorney or the Georgia Heirs Property Law Center before you talk to any buyer, including us.

📋 What To Do This Week

  1. Pull the current deed from the county clerk of superior court's real estate records and confirm whose name is on it.
  2. Check the probate court in that county for any estate, Year's Support petition or "no administration necessary" order.
  3. Write out the family tree from the person on the deed to today, including every heir who has died and who their heirs are.
  4. Ask each living heir in writing whether they want to keep the house, sell it, or sell their own share.
  5. Get one as-is value opinion so the conversation starts with a number.
  6. Talk to a Georgia probate or real estate attorney about whether to open an estate, petition for powers, or file partition.

✅ Bottom Line

Georgia gives inherited houses an unusual order of operations. Title starts with the heirs, moves to an administrator if one is appointed, and can be claimed ahead of everyone through Year's Support. When co-owners end up in partition, the heirs property act puts a neutral appraisal and a 45-day family buyout in front of any sale. Families that know those steps can usually negotiate the result a court would reach, without the year it takes to get there.

If every heir agrees and the house is sound, list it. If the title needs a probate case, the house needs serious work, or one heir needs to get out now, ask us, and we'll tell you honestly which group you're in.

❓ Frequently Asked Questions

What is heirs property in Georgia?

Heirs property is land passed down without a will or a clear transfer, so that it ends up owned by several relatives at once. Georgia's Uniform Partition of Heirs Property Act, O.C.G.A. 44-6-180 and following, defines it for partition cases: there's no binding agreement among the co-owners about partition, at least one co-owner inherited from a relative, and family members hold enough of the ownership to meet the statute's 20% thresholds.

Can one heir force the sale of an inherited house in Georgia?

An heir who is a co-owner can file for partition in superior court under O.C.G.A. 44-6-160. If the court finds the house is heirs property, it must first order an appraisal and give the co-owners who don't want a sale 45 days to buy out the ones who do, at the appraised value. Only if nobody buys does the court move to division or a sale, and the act favors an open-market sale.

When did Georgia adopt the Uniform Partition of Heirs Property Act?

Georgia enacted it in 2012 through House Bill 744, effective January 1, 2013. It is codified at O.C.G.A. 44-6-180 through 44-6-189.1. Nevada adopted the uniform act first, in 2011.

Who owns an inherited Georgia house before probate?

If there's no will, O.C.G.A. 53-2-7 vests title in the heirs at the moment of death, subject to divestment if the probate court appoints an administrator. Once an administrator is appointed, title and the right to possess the property move to the administrator until they assent to returning it to the heirs.

Can a Georgia administrator sell the house if an heir objects?

Yes, with court permission. Under O.C.G.A. 53-8-13, the administrator petitions the probate court with the proposed price and terms, every heir is served with a citation, and objections are heard. The court can approve the sale over an objection. If all heirs instead consent unanimously, the court can grant the administrator powers under 53-12-261 to sell without further court approval.

What does 'no administration necessary' mean in Georgia?

It is a probate court order under O.C.G.A. 53-2-40 that lets heirs skip a full administration when there's no will, the estate's debts are paid or the creditors consent, and the heirs have agreed on how to divide the estate. If one heir won't agree to the division, the family can't use it.

What is Year's Support in Georgia, and can it include the house?

Year's Support, under O.C.G.A. 53-3-1, lets a surviving spouse and minor children ask the probate court to set apart property for twelve months of support, ahead of most other debts and claims. It can include the house. If an heir objects, the court sets apart what is needed to maintain the family's standard of living, considering their other resources and the estate's ability to pay its debts.

How much does a surviving spouse inherit in Georgia without a will?

Under O.C.G.A. 53-2-1, a spouse with no children inherits everything. With children, the spouse shares equally with them, but never takes less than one-third of the estate.

What are the probate court filing fees in Georgia?

O.C.G.A. 15-9-60 sets uniform probate court fees statewide. Since January 1, 2025, estate matters carry a $175 fee per petition and $75 per motion, response, citation or caveat, plus costs such as publication and certified copies.

Should Georgia heirs list the house or sell it for cash?

If every heir will sign and the house is in reasonable shape, listing usually nets the most. A cash sale makes more sense when the house needs major repairs, the title requires a probate case to clear, the alternative is a partition case that may run a year or two, or one heir wants to sell their interest now.
