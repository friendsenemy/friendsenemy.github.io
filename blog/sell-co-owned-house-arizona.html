Can One Owner Sell a Jointly Owned House in Arizona? Married Owners Need Two Signatures, Everyone Else Needs None

Arizona gives two opposite answers to the same question. If you and your co-owner are married and the house is community property, state law requires both spouses to join any sale or mortgage, even of a half. If you aren't married, you can sell your own share, or record a one-page affidavit ending survivorship, without asking anyone. Here's how to tell which rule you're under, what each one lets you do, and when listing the whole house beats every other option. Questions are free.

You own an Arizona house with someone else, and you want out. Maybe it's a sibling you bought a rental with, a former partner who stayed in the house, a friend from an investment that went flat, or a spouse in a marriage that's ending. You want to know whether you can sell without their signature.

Arizona answers that question differently depending on one fact that has nothing to do with the deed's fine print: whether you're married to the other owner. Start there.

💍 Married to Your Co-Owner? Arizona Requires Both Signatures

Arizona is a community property state. A house bought during the marriage with marital money is presumed to be community property no matter whose name is on the loan, and A.R.S. § 25-214 governs who can deal with it. Each spouse generally has equal power over community property and can act alone on most of it, but subsection C carves out the big exception: joinder of both spouses is required for "any transaction for the acquisition, disposition or encumbrance of an interest in real property" (other than an unpatented mining claim or a lease under a year).

For a married co-owner, that means you can't sell the house alone, you can't sell "your half" alone, and you can't mortgage your interest alone. Title companies in Arizona know this rule cold and will ask for both signatures on community real estate. If your spouse won't sign, the path to a sale runs through a divorce or legal separation, where the court divides the community equitably under A.R.S. § 25-318, or through an agreement you both sign.

There is one more wrinkle for a marriage that's ending. In every Arizona divorce or legal separation, A.R.S. § 25-315 has the clerk issue a preliminary injunction, binding the filing spouse when the petition is filed and the other spouse once served or notified. Among other things, it bars both spouses from selling, transferring, encumbering or concealing joint, common or community property, except in the usual course of business, for the necessities of life or for reasonable court and attorney fees, without written consent or the court's permission. So a spouse who tries to sell or borrow against the house mid-divorce is violating a court order, not just a norm. Our guide to selling a house during a divorce covers how couples agree on a sale in that setting.

⚠️ The warning that matters most: don't assume the "community property" label only applies if the deed says so. A house bought during the marriage is presumed community even if only one spouse is on title, and a buyer's title company will usually require the other spouse to sign or disclaim. Find out how the house was acquired before you sign a listing agreement or a purchase contract.

🧍 Not Married? You Can Act Alone on Your Own Share

For unmarried co-owners, Arizona law starts from the opposite place. Under A.R.S. § 33-431(A), a grant of real property to two or more people creates a tenancy in common unless the deed expressly says "joint tenancy with right of survivorship" (spouses are treated separately). As a tenant in common, you own an undivided share that you can sell, give away, mortgage or leave by will without your co-owner's permission. You can't sell the whole house, and a buyer of your share steps into your shoes as a co-owner with the same rights you had.

Joint tenants with right of survivorship have one more tool, and it's an Arizona specialty.

📝 The One-Page Affidavit That Ends Survivorship

In many states, breaking a joint tenancy means deeding your interest to a third party or to yourself. Arizona offers a simpler route. Under A.R.S. § 33-431(E), any joint tenant can record, with the county recorder, an "affidavit terminating right of survivorship," signed under oath, stating the intent to end survivorship and identifying the instrument that created it, its recording date and reference, and the legal description. Recording it ends that owner's survivorship right. If there are more than two joint tenants, the affidavit only removes the person who signs it; survivorship continues among the others.

Why it matters: if you and a former partner bought a Tempe condo as joint tenants and you die first, your share would pass to them automatically, not to your children. Recording the affidavit turns your share into an ordinary tenancy-in-common interest that passes under your will or to your heirs. It does not by itself sell anything or force a sale, and it can't be used to take more than your share. A parallel provision in § 33-431(D) lets either spouse terminate the survivorship feature of "community property with right of survivorship," while expressly preserving each spouse's community interest; it doesn't let one spouse sell.

⚖️ When the Other Owner Won't Sell: Arizona Partition

An unmarried co-owner who can't get agreement can ask the superior court in the county where the property sits to partition it. The procedure is in A.R.S. § 12-1211 and the sections that follow. The court decides each owner's share, appoints commissioners to divide the land, and, if the commissioners report under § 12-1218 that the property can't be fairly divided, orders it sold and the proceeds split. A single-family house in Phoenix or Tucson almost always ends in a sale, not a division.

Plan on 9 to 18 months for a contested case and $10,000 to $40,000 or more in legal fees per side, with commissioners' fees and sale costs paid from the proceeds. Arizona courts can also sort out who paid what during co-ownership, so keep records of mortgage payments, property tax, insurance and repairs.

If the house came to you and your co-owners by inheritance, Arizona's 2024 heirs property act (A.R.S. §§ 12-3401 to 12-3412) can add a court appraisal, a 45-day window for owners who didn't ask for a sale to buy out those who did, and a preference for an open-market listing over an auction. The act is written to work with partitions requested in probate under § 14-3911; ask your lawyer how it applies to your case. For the national picture, see our partition lawsuit explainer.

🧭 Five Situations, Five Moves

💔 Married, divorce filed

Sell by agreement or through the decree

The preliminary injunction and the joinder rule both stand in the way of a solo sale. A signed agreement to list or to sell to a cash buyer, approved in the divorce, is the fastest path; otherwise the decree will order a sale or award the house to one spouse with an equalization payment.

🤝 Unmarried, the other owner wants the house

Negotiate a buyout

Agree on an appraiser, net out the mortgage and any credits, and have the buyer refinance in their own name. It's cheaper than any other route if their credit and income can carry it.

🏡 Unmarried, both willing to sell but can't agree on price

List with a price-drop schedule

Write the listing price and a 30-day reduction schedule into a short agreement before you hire the agent. Most "won't sell" fights are really "won't agree on the number" fights.

🚪 Unmarried, the other owner won't engage at all

Sell your share or file partition

A share sale gets you out quickly at a discount; partition gets you full value minus legal costs and time. A written buyout offer with a deadline often moves people before either is needed.

🕊️ Joint tenants, worried about who gets your share if you die

Record the termination affidavit

Ending survivorship under § 33-431(E) is quick and inexpensive and doesn't require the other owner's cooperation. Pair it with a will so your share goes where you intend.

💵 The Numbers on a Tucson House

Consider a 1980s three-bedroom in Tucson that would be worth about $340,000 after updates, owned 50/50 by two unmarried former partners. One lives there and pays nothing toward the mortgage; the other wants out.

Holding costs. Pima County property taxes on an owner-occupied home commonly run around 0.8 to 1 percent of market value, roughly $2,700 to $3,400 a year here, or about $225 to $285 a month. With insurance at $100 to $200 a month, a mortgage payment that one owner may be covering alone, summer electricity of $150 to $300 and basic upkeep, the house can easily cost $500 to $900 a month on top of the mortgage.

Route 1, a cooperative listing. $25,000 in paint, flooring and HVAC service, a sale at $335,000, and about $22,000 in commission and closing costs. Net before the mortgage payoff: about $288,000, or $144,000 each.

Route 2, partition. If the court orders a sale and the house sells as-is for $300,000, with commissioners' and sale costs of about $20,000 and legal fees of $15,000 to $30,000 per side, each owner might net $110,000 to $125,000 before the payoff, a year or more later.

Route 3, a cash sale of the whole house. Cash buyers usually pay about 60 to 80 percent of after-repair value: here $204,000 to $272,000, and around $240,000 to $255,000 for this condition. No repairs, no commission, closing in a few weeks once both owners sign. About $120,000 to $127,000 each before the payoff.

Route 4, selling only your half. A buyer of a 50 percent share in an occupied house is buying a lawsuit, so the price is steeply discounted from $170,000, often to well under half of it. It's the fastest exit when the other owner won't sign anything, and the worst-priced one.

✅ Bottom Line

In Arizona, "can one owner sell?" depends first on marriage. Spouses holding community real estate must both sign any sale or mortgage, and a divorce petition adds an injunction on top. Unmarried co-owners can sell their own shares, and joint tenants can end survivorship alone by recording an affidavit. To sell the whole house, you still need everyone's signature or a partition order, so a written offer and a listing plan are usually the first moves.

❓ Frequently Asked Questions

Can one spouse sell a jointly owned house in Arizona?

Not if it's community real estate. A.R.S. 25-214(C) requires both spouses to join any transaction to acquire, sell or encumber an interest in real property, so one spouse can't sell or mortgage the house, or a half of it, alone.

Can I sell my half of a house I own with my unmarried partner in Arizona?

Yes, if you hold as tenants in common or joint tenants. You can sell your undivided share without the other owner's consent, though you can't sell the whole house, and the buyer becomes a co-owner with the remaining owner.

How do I end a joint tenancy with right of survivorship in Arizona?

A.R.S. 33-431(E) lets any joint tenant record an 'affidavit terminating right of survivorship' with the county recorder, signed under oath and describing the original instrument and the property. With more than two joint tenants, it only removes the signer's survivorship.

What does an Arizona deed create if it just names two unmarried buyers?

A tenancy in common. Under A.R.S. 33-431(A), a grant to two or more people creates an estate in common unless the deed expressly declares a joint tenancy with right of survivorship.

Can my spouse sell or refinance our Arizona house during the divorce?

Generally not without your written consent or the court's permission. A.R.S. 25-315 requires a preliminary injunction in every divorce, effective against the filing spouse at filing and the other spouse on service or notice, that bars selling, transferring or encumbering joint, common or community property outside narrow exceptions.

Is a house in only my name community property in Arizona?

It can be. A house bought during the marriage with community funds is presumed community property regardless of whose name is on title, and title companies will usually require the other spouse to sign or disclaim before a sale.

How does a partition lawsuit work in Arizona?

An owner files in the superior court of the county where the property is under A.R.S. 12-1211 and following. The court sets each owner's share and appoints commissioners; if they report the property can't be fairly divided, the court orders a sale under 12-1218 and splits the proceeds.

Does Arizona's heirs property act apply to co-owned houses?

It applies to heirs property, generally land held as tenants in common where someone got title from a relative and relatives hold a large share. Arizona's version, A.R.S. 12-3401 to 12-3412, adds a court appraisal and a 45-day buyout election; it is written to work with probate partitions under 14-3911.

How long does an Arizona partition case take?

A contested case commonly takes 9 to 18 months from filing to a sale and distribution, longer if ownership shares or credits are disputed. An owner who doesn't respond can shorten it considerably.

Is a cash sale or a listing better for Arizona co-owners who disagree?

If both owners will sign and the house is in decent shape, listing usually nets the most. A cash sale makes more sense when the house needs work nobody will fund, one owner won't participate, or the owners need a fast, certain closing.
