Partition Lawsuits in Florida: The Owner Who Fights Helps Pay for It

In most lawsuits each side pays its own lawyer. Florida partition is different: the judge can split the attorney fees among all the owners according to their shares, including the owner who opposed the sale. That one rule changes the math for everyone, especially the co-owner deciding whether to resist. Here's how Florida partition works, who can be forced into it, and how to avoid it. Or ask us, free.

By Dustin Ray, Founder of Pages of Purpose LLC. Not legal advice — laws differ by state, Florida homestead and partition law are full of exceptions, and judges decide fee awards case by case. Talk to an attorney licensed in Florida, where the property is, before you file, respond, or settle.

If you co-own a house in Florida and the owners can't agree, any one of you can file a partition action asking the court to divide the property or sell it and split the proceeds. If someone has filed against you, or you're thinking about filing, the first thing to understand isn't the procedure. It's the bill, and who ends up paying it.

💸 Florida Can Make the Holdout Share the Plaintiff's Legal Bill

Section 64.081 of the Florida Statutes says that every party is bound by the partition judgment to pay a share of the costs, and it expressly includes attorneys' fees in those costs. The share is set on equitable principles, in proportion to each party's interest. In practice, Florida judges commonly award the fees of the attorney whose work benefited all the owners, typically the one who brought the case and produced the sale, and spread that award across the owners by percentage.

Think about what that means for a 50/50 house. Your brother files for partition. You fight it for a year, then the court orders a sale. Your brother's lawyer billed $24,000. Unless the judge finds a reason to do otherwise, you may be ordered to pay half of that out of your sale proceeds, on top of whatever you paid your own lawyer. Resisting didn't just cost you your own fees; it may have increased the fees you share.

The rule cuts the other way too. Judges look at equity, and a plaintiff who runs up fees on pointless motions, or who files without trying to settle, can see the award trimmed. A defendant with a real defense, such as a claim that the property can't be partitioned at all, isn't penalized for raising it. But a co-owner who simply refuses to sell a house that plainly has to be sold is, in most cases, paying for both sides of the argument.

If you've been served: before you hire a litigator to resist, ask a Florida real estate attorney one question: "Is there a legal reason this property can't be partitioned?" If the answer is no, every month of opposition likely costs you twice, once for your own lawyer and again through your share of the plaintiff's.

🧭 Which Florida Owners Can Be Forced Into a Sale, and Which Can't

Section 64.031 lets any joint tenant, tenant in common or coparcener file. That covers most co-owners, but Florida has several important exceptions and twists:

  • Married couples holding by the entirety can't be partitioned. While the marriage lasts, neither spouse can force a sale of entireties property. Divorce changes that: under Section 689.15, former spouses become tenants in common, and either can then file. Our page on selling a co-owned house in Florida covers how those automatic conversions work.
  • Homestead doesn't block partition between co-owners. The Florida Supreme Court held in Tullis v. Tullis (1978) that the constitution's homestead protections allow partition and forced sale when one owner sues another. Living in the house as your homestead doesn't stop your co-owner.
  • A surviving spouse's life estate usually does. When a married Florida homeowner dies leaving a spouse and children, Section 732.401 generally gives the spouse a life estate in the homestead and the children the remainder. Remainder owners have no present right to possess, so they can't partition against the spouse while the life estate lasts.
  • But the spouse's election changes the picture. The same statute lets the surviving spouse elect, within six months after the death, to take an undivided one-half interest as a tenant in common instead. Once that election is made, the spouse and the children are co-owners, and either side can file for partition. Whether a stepparent elected, and when, can decide whether a Florida partition case is even possible.

🏛️ How a Florida Partition Case Moves, Step by Step

  1. Complaint and lis pendens. The plaintiff files in circuit court in the county where the land lies. Section 64.041 requires the complaint to describe the property and state each owner's interest. A notice of lis pendens is usually recorded under Section 48.23, which clouds title so no one can quietly sell or refinance.
  2. Response. Each defendant generally has 20 days after service to respond. Florida treats partition as an equity case, so a judge, not a jury, decides it.
  3. Interests and accounting. The court confirms who owns what and takes evidence on credits: who paid the mortgage, taxes and insurance, and what rental value an occupying owner enjoyed.
  4. Division or sale. If the property can be divided, Section 64.061 has the court appoint three commissioners to make the division. When division would prejudice the owners, which is the usual finding for a single house, Section 64.071 lets the court order a public auction to the highest bidder. Most Florida clerks run these sales online.
  5. Distribution. After the sale, the court pays costs and the fee awards, applies each owner's credits and charges, and distributes what's left by ownership share.

An uncontested Florida case can finish in four to eight months. A contested one, with an evidentiary hearing on the accounting, often takes twelve to twenty-four. Our general guide to partition lawsuits explains how other states compare.

🧬 Inherited Houses Filed After July 1, 2020

Florida adopted the Uniform Partition of Heirs Property Act, codified as Sections 64.201 through 64.214, for partition actions filed on or after July 1, 2020. It covers property held by tenants in common with no binding partition agreement, where an owner acquired title from a relative and related owners hold a large enough share (one of the tests is 20% or more of the interests).

When it applies, three things change. The court determines the property's value, usually through an appraisal. Under Section 64.207, the owners who didn't ask for a sale can buy out the interests of those who did at that value. And if a sale is still needed, Section 64.210 requires an open-market sale through a licensed broker in most cases, not a courthouse auction. For heirs who want to keep the family home, that's significant protection. For heirs who want out, it usually means a better price but a longer case.

🧮 The Accounting That Happens Before Anyone Gets a Check

Florida partition isn't just a sale; it's a settling of accounts among the owners. Three rules drive most of the money:

Credits for carrying costs. An owner who paid more than their share of the mortgage, property taxes or insurance can ask for a credit from the others' shares. Keep bank statements, not just memories.

Rental value as an offset. A co-owner living in the house usually doesn't owe rent simply for being there; Florida courts require an ouster or third-party rent for that. But when the occupant claims credits for paying the expenses, the court can offset those credits by the reasonable rental value of their exclusive use. Many occupants who expect a big credit end up close to even.

Actual rent collected. If an owner rented the house to tenants, they must account to the others for the rent received, minus legitimate expenses.

🔀 Exits That Usually Beat a Clerk's Auction

Joint listing with an agent

Best price on a sound house

Owners can agree, even after filing, to list the house and dismiss the case at closing. On a house that's insurable and in decent shape, this beats an auction by a wide margin and keeps the fee award small.

Buyout at an agreed value

Best when one owner wants to stay

The owner who wants the house pays the others their share, with credits for what each paid. Budget for documentary stamp tax on the deed, generally 70 cents per $100 of the price paid.

Cash sale with the case dismissed

Best when the house won't finance or time is short

A house with an old roof, water damage or code problems may not qualify for a financed buyer's insurance. A cash buyer can close in two to four weeks, the lis pendens is released, and the fee fight ends.

Selling your interest to step out

Last resort if you can't afford the case

A tenant in common can sell their undivided interest at any time. The buyer joins the case in your place. You'll get less than your fraction of the house's value, but you stop paying fees and carrying costs. Our guide to selling a share of an inherited Florida house explains how those shares are priced.

💵 Clerk's Auction or Settlement? The Numbers on an Orlando-Area House

Three adult children inherited a 1980s house in Pine Hills, west of Orlando, in equal thirds after their mother died; no surviving spouse. Repaired, it would bring about $340,000. It needs a roof and interior work, about $45,000. One child filed for partition after the others stopped responding.

Carrying costs. With no homestead exemption, a non-homestead house in much of Florida pays somewhere around 1.5% to 2% of taxable value each year. At 1.8% on $340,000, that's about $6,120 a year, or $510 a month. Insurance on a vacant Florida house with an aging roof often runs $250 to $500 a month if a carrier will write it. Utilities and lawn care add about $300. Total: roughly $1,100 to $1,300 a month.

Contested case ending in a clerk's auction. After fifteen months, the house sells online for $230,000. Auction bidders price in the roof, the lack of inspections and the occupancy risk. The plaintiff's fees of $22,000 are awarded and split three ways under Section 64.081; the two defendants also spent $12,000 each on their own lawyers. Add about $17,000 in carrying costs and $3,000 in court and sale costs. Net to divide: about $188,000, or $62,700 each, before the defendants subtract their own fees. Each defendant ends up near $50,700.

Settlement and cash sale in month two. Cash buyers usually pay about 60% to 80% of after-repair value. A realistic offer here is around $235,000, or 69%, closing in three weeks. Subtract about $6,000 in combined legal costs to dismiss and $2,400 in carrying costs. Net: about $226,600, or $75,500 each.

Settlement and a listing. If the three agree to list as-is and a buyer pays $270,000 after four months, net after 8% sale costs ($21,600), $6,000 in legal costs and $4,800 in carrying costs is about $237,600, or $79,200 each.

Even without any money from a cash buyer in the picture, settling early is worth $25,000 or more to each sibling compared with fighting to an auction. The listing is the best result if the house can be insured and all three can cooperate for four months.

🙅 When Listing Beats Selling to a Cash Buyer

If the roof and systems are in decent shape, the house can be insured, and the owners will cooperate long enough to list it, list it. A retail buyer will pay more than we will. If one owner wants to keep the house and can refinance, a buyout beats any sale. A cash sale makes sense when the house can't pass a four-point inspection, when an owner can't keep paying carrying costs, or when a fast, certain close is worth more than the last few thousand dollars.

📋 What To Do This Week

  1. If you've been served, write down the service date. Your response is generally due 20 days later.
  2. Pull the deed and any probate records. Confirm whether there's an entireties estate, a life estate under Section 732.401, or a recorded election by a surviving spouse.
  3. Gather proof of every mortgage, tax and insurance payment you've made, and note whether anyone lived in or rented out the house.
  4. Check whether the house can be insured: get a four-point inspection or an insurance quote.
  5. Get an as-is value and an after-repair value from a local agent.
  6. Ask a Florida real estate attorney whether you have a genuine defense. If not, focus on the accounting and on settlement.
  7. Put a written settlement proposal on the table: buyout, joint listing, or cash sale, each with a deadline.

✅ Bottom Line

Florida partition rewards cooperation and charges for resistance. Because Section 64.081 lets the judge spread attorney fees across all the owners by share, a co-owner who fights a sale with no real defense often pays for both lawyers. Check first whether partition is even available: entireties property, a surviving spouse's life estate, and the 2020 heirs property law all change the answer. Then settle the accounting and choose the exit, whether a listing, a buyout or a cash sale, before a clerk's auction chooses it for you. If you'd like an honest read on your situation, ask us.

❓ Frequently Asked Questions

Who pays the attorney fees in a Florida partition lawsuit?

Under Florida Statutes Section 64.081, every party is bound to pay a share of the costs, including attorneys' fees, set on equitable principles in proportion to their interest. Judges commonly award the fees of the attorney whose work benefited all owners and divide that award by ownership share, so an owner who opposed the sale can end up sharing the plaintiff's legal bill.

Can I stop my co-owner from forcing a partition in Florida?

Only with a legal reason. Florida courts generally must partition property held by tenants in common or joint tenants. Real defenses include an entireties estate between spouses who are still married, a surviving spouse's life estate, a binding agreement not to partition, or a dispute over who actually owns the property.

Does Florida homestead protection prevent a partition sale?

Not between co-owners. In Tullis v. Tullis (1978), the Florida Supreme Court held that homestead provisions allow partition and forced sale when one owner sues another. Homestead protects against most creditors, not against a fellow owner's partition action.

Can the children force a sale if their stepparent has a life estate in the Florida house?

Generally not. Under Section 732.401, a surviving spouse usually receives a life estate in the homestead, and the children hold the remainder with no present right to possess. However, if the spouse elected within six months after the death to take a one-half interest as a tenant in common instead, the spouse and children are co-owners and partition becomes possible.

Has Florida adopted the Uniform Partition of Heirs Property Act?

Yes. Florida's version is in Sections 64.201 through 64.214 and applies to partition actions filed on or after July 1, 2020. For qualifying inherited property, it provides a court-determined value, a buyout right for owners who didn't request a sale, and generally an open-market sale through a licensed broker instead of an auction.

How is a house sold in a Florida partition case?

If the property can't be divided without prejudice to the owners, Section 64.071 allows the court to order a public auction to the highest bidder, usually run online by the county clerk. For heirs property covered by the 2020 act, the sale is generally an open-market listing with a broker instead.

How long does a partition lawsuit take in Florida?

An uncontested case often finishes in four to eight months. A contested case with an evidentiary hearing on credits and rental value commonly takes twelve to twenty-four months, longer if there is an appeal or a dispute over who owns what.

Does the co-owner living in the house get credit for paying the mortgage in a Florida partition?

They can ask for credits for paying more than their share of the mortgage, taxes and insurance. But Florida courts can offset those credits by the reasonable rental value of the occupant's exclusive use of the house, which often reduces or eliminates the net credit.

How long do I have to respond to a partition complaint in Florida?

Generally 20 days after you are served. A partition complaint is usually accompanied by a recorded lis pendens, so the property can't be sold or refinanced without addressing the case. Contact a Florida real estate attorney promptly, even if you plan to settle.

Is it better to settle a Florida partition case or let it go to auction?

Settling is usually better for everyone. A clerk's auction tends to bring a lower price than a listing, and fee awards under Section 64.081 shrink each owner's share. A negotiated listing, buyout or cash sale typically leaves each owner with more money, sooner.
