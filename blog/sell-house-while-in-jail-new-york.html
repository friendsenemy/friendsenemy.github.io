Selling a House From a New York Jail or Prison: Do It Before Sentencing if You Can

New York is one of the few states that still writes a prison sentence's civil consequences into statute. A state prison sentence suspends the person's civil rights for the term, a life sentence makes them “civilly dead,” and once there's a conviction for a serious crime, sale money over $10,000 can set off a notice to the state's Office of Victim Services. None of that takes the house away. It does change who can sign, who has to be told, and how long the family should wait. Or ask us a question, free.

By Dustin Ray, Founder of Pages of Purpose LLC. Not legal advice. New York's civil rights, crime victim and power of attorney statutes are technical, the Department of Corrections changes its directives, and laws differ by state. Before anyone signs a power of attorney, deed or contract for an owner who is locked up, talk to an attorney licensed in New York, where the property is.

Most families who reach us about a New York house ask the wrong first question. They ask whether the owner is allowed to sell. The owner almost always is. New York abolished forfeiture of property on conviction long ago: Civil Rights Law section 79-b says a conviction for any crime does not work a forfeiture of any property, real or personal, or of any right or interest in it. The house stays in the owner's name whether they're on Rikers Island awaiting trial or serving a long term upstate.

The better first question is: where is this person in the criminal case right now? Unlike most states, New York attaches different legal labels to an owner at each stage, and each label changes the paperwork. A person in a county jail who hasn't been sentenced is, in the eyes of the Civil Rights Law, the same as anyone else. A person sentenced to a state correctional facility has their civil rights suspended for the term. A person sentenced to life is declared civilly dead. And once a conviction for one of a long list of serious crimes is entered, a buyer paying that person more than $10,000 may have to send written notice to the state. The practical upshot: if a sale is going to happen, the weeks before sentencing are often the cleanest window the family will get.

⚖️ The Sentence Decides What the Owner Can Sign

Here is what the statutes actually say, in plain words.

Civil Rights Law section 79 covers a sentence of imprisonment in a state correctional institution for any term less than life. It "suspends, during the term of the sentence, all the civil rights, and all private trusts, authority, or powers of, or held by, the person sentenced." Amendments in 1973 carved out the right to bring and prosecute lawsuits, after courts found the old blanket rule unconstitutional. What the statute doesn't do is list which everyday acts count as a suspended "civil right." It says nothing directly about signing a deed or a power of attorney for one's own home.

Section 79-a covers life sentences: the person "is thereafter deemed civilly dead," with exceptions for marriage and for bringing lawsuits. It expressly excludes the old indeterminate sentence of one day to life, but on its face it reaches other sentences whose maximum is life. New York is one of a small handful of states that still has a civil death statute on the books.

Section 79-b is the counterweight: no forfeiture of property. So the owner keeps the house. The open question is whether, during a state sentence or a life term, they can personally exercise the power to transfer it.

In our experience the answer for owners serving a sentence shorter than life is usually yes in practice, and sales close on deeds and powers of attorney signed inside. But it's a question a title underwriter is entitled to ask, and some do. For a lifer, expect real resistance and a lawyer's involvement from the start. Two things worth knowing:

  • 🗓️ A power of attorney signed before sentencing was signed by someone whose rights were fully intact. That removes the question entirely for the signing itself. It's the strongest reason New York families should move early.
  • 📜 A certificate of relief from disabilities under Article 23 of the Correction Law can lift automatic forfeitures and disabilities that come with a conviction, and section 79-a expressly says a lifer can still receive one. Ask the defense lawyer whether one was issued at sentencing, or whether one can be sought.

⚠️ The window that matters: if the case hasn't reached sentencing yet, talk to the defense lawyer this week about getting a New York power of attorney signed now. A document signed while the owner's rights are untouched is far easier to defend later than one signed after a state sentence begins, and it can be used for months or years without another trip inside.

💰 The Son of Sam Notice: Sale Money Over $10,000

New York's "Son of Sam" law, Executive Law section 632-a, started as a rule about criminals profiting from book deals. It was expanded to cover "funds of a convicted person," and the definition is broad: all funds and property received from any source by a person convicted of a specified crime, other than child support and earned income, while they're in a state prison, a local jail or a federal facility, and for some time after release.

"Specified crime" covers a violent felony offense, any class B felony, any felony in the first degree, certain grand larceny and stolen-property offenses, crimes that bar merit time, and equivalent offenses in other jurisdictions with a New York connection. Many people serving state time were convicted of at least one of these.

Here's what that means for a house sale:

  • 💵 The payer has the duty. Anyone who knowingly pays or agrees to pay funds of a convicted person, where the payments are worth more than $10,000 combined, must give written notice to the Office of Victim Services as soon as practicable. In a sale, that's the buyer and whoever disburses the money at closing. If we're the buyer, we send that notice. A buyer who tells you they'll skip it is telling you something about how they do business.
  • 📬 The office tells the victims. Known crime victims get notice, and the statute gives them three years from discovery of the funds to sue, even if their normal deadline has passed.
  • 🧊 The money can be frozen. Once a victim files, or tells the office they're about to, the office can seek attachment, an injunction or a receiver to keep the money from being spent.
  • 🏦 The facility reports too. If more than $10,000 is deposited into the person's facility account, the superintendent or sheriff must also notify the office.

Before conviction, these rules about funds of a convicted person don't apply, because there's no conviction yet. That's part of why the pre-sentencing window matters. It is not a reason to rush money out of reach. A transfer designed to keep money from people with claims can be undone under New York's voidable transactions law, and moving proceeds into a relative's account to hide them can create new legal trouble. The honest plan is to sell when it makes sense, give whatever notices are required, and let the defense or civil lawyer advise on what the victim's claims really are.

Restitution is a separate issue. A New York restitution order can be enforced like a civil judgment, and a docketed judgment is a lien on real property in that county. A title search will find it, and it gets paid from the proceeds at closing.

🗂️ When the Person Inside Is Someone Else's Agent or Executor

This is the New York trap that catches families who aren't even selling the incarcerated person's house. Section 79 suspends not only the person's civil rights but "all private trusts, authority, or powers" held by them. If your brother is named as agent on your mother's power of attorney, or as executor in your father's will, a state prison sentence suspends that authority for the length of the sentence.

For an estate, that matters in Surrogate's Court. Separately from section 79, the Surrogate's Court Procedure Act lets the court, in its discretion, declare someone convicted of a felony ineligible to serve as a fiduciary when the crime could harm the estate, with embezzlement and misappropriation given as examples. In practice the family usually petitions for letters naming the successor executor in the will, or, with no will, another distributee. The incarcerated person doesn't lose their inheritance, but the estate's sale goes ahead without them acting as fiduciary. Note that their share of the proceeds is "funds from any source," so the $10,000 notice rule can apply when the estate distributes it. Our guides on a house with no will and heirs who can't agree cover the rest of that process.

For a power of attorney where the person inside is the agent, look for a named successor agent. The New York form has a place for one. If there isn't one and the principal still has capacity, the simplest fix is a new power of attorney naming someone else.

🖋️ The 2021 Statutory Short Form, Signed Inside a DOCCS Facility

New York's power of attorney law lives in General Obligations Law article 5, title 15. The statutory short form is in section 5-1513, and the execution rules are in section 5-1501B. A major overhaul took effect on June 13, 2021. The parts that matter for a sale from prison:

  • ✅ "Substantially conforming" now counts. Before 2021, small wording errors could sink a New York power of attorney. Now a form that substantially conforms to the statute is valid, and insubstantial variations in the "Caution to the Principal" and "Important Information for the Agent" sections don't defeat it. The separate statutory gifts rider was also eliminated; gifting authority goes in the form's modifications section.
  • ✍️ Notary plus two witnesses. The principal signs and dates it, and the signature is acknowledged before a notary the way a deed would be. Two witnesses must also sign in the principal's presence. Neither witness may be named as an agent or as someone allowed to receive gifts. The notary may serve as one of the two witnesses, so you need at least one more qualified person in the room.
  • 🔏 The agent signs too. The agent signs the form in front of their own notary, on the outside, and the power of attorney takes effect for that agent on the date of the agent's acknowledgment. A gap between the principal's signing and the agent's doesn't invalidate it.
  • 🏠 Initial the real estate authority. The form lists subjects of authority. Real estate transactions must be granted, and it's worth adding a modification that names the property address and authorizes the agent to sell it, sign the deed and the transfer tax forms, and receive the proceeds.

Two more provisions help families. A third party in New York can't refuse a properly executed statutory short form without reasonable cause, and an older form signed under the law in effect at the time remains good. And a title company asked to accept the form may request an agent's sworn certification of facts or an attorney's opinion, which is the usual source of a week or two of back-and-forth.

The DOCCS side. DOCCS Directive 4483 governs law libraries and notary services in state prisons. It requires each facility to schedule notary access so people in general population can reach a notary within 72 hours of asking, not counting weekends and holidays. People in special housing usually face more limits. The facility notary handles the acknowledgment, and New York's statutory notary fee for an acknowledgment is a couple of dollars. What the directive doesn't solve is the second witness. Ask the facility, through the law library or the person's assigned counselor, who can witness, and confirm the agent won't be one of them. Then send the form in already filled out, with the property address in the modifications section, so nothing is left blank.

Recording. Real Property Law section 294 lets a power to convey real property be recorded like a deed. In practice your title insurer will require the original, or a certified copy, to be recorded with the deed: at the County Clerk outside New York City, and through ACRIS with the City Register in the five boroughs. Keep the original safe. Losing it is the single most common reason a New York closing slips.

💍 Married Owners: Entireties Ownership Is Thinner Protection Here

When spouses take title together in New York, the law presumes a tenancy by the entirety unless the deed says otherwise. Two consequences matter. First, neither spouse can sell the house alone, so the spouse outside needs the incarcerated spouse's signature on the deed or a power of attorney naming someone to sign it. Being married doesn't let you sign for them; our spouse-in-jail guide walks through the deed scenarios.

Second, and this is where New York differs from Pennsylvania and Virginia: entireties ownership here doesn't fully shield the house from one spouse's creditors. New York's highest court has allowed a judgment creditor of one spouse to reach that spouse's interest, including the right of survivorship, while leaving the other spouse's rights intact. Restitution docketed as a judgment and civil claims by victims can therefore attach to the incarcerated spouse's side. When the house sells, those claims tend to come out of that spouse's share of the money at closing. Talk to a lawyer about how the proceeds will be split before the contract is signed, not after.

🛡️ The Homestead Exemption Needs Someone Living There

New York's homestead exemption, CPLR 5206, protects equity in a principal residence from money judgments, and the amount depends on the county. The Department of Financial Services adjusts it every three years. The amounts effective April 1, 2024 are:

  • 📍 $204,825 in Kings, Queens, New York, Bronx, Richmond, Nassau, Suffolk, Rockland, Westchester and Putnam Counties.
  • 📍 $170,700 in Dutchess, Albany, Columbia, Orange, Saratoga and Ulster Counties.
  • 📍 $102,400 in every other county.

The catch for an incarcerated owner is in the statute's wording. The protection covers property "owned and occupied as a principal residence," and it says the exemption ceases when the property stops being occupied as a residence, with a narrow exception for damage or destruction of the dwelling. If a spouse and children still live in the house, the picture is different from a house that has sat empty since the arrest. Once the house becomes a check, ask a lawyer what protection, if any, follows the money. Don't assume the homestead number travels with it.

⏳ A Six-Year Foreclosure Is Still a Losing Clock

New York foreclosures go through the courts, and they are famously slow. Lenders must send a 90-day pre-foreclosure notice before filing on a home loan, and the court must hold a mandatory settlement conference early in the case. ATTOM's mid-2026 report put New York's average at 2,007 days from first filing to completed foreclosure for homes foreclosed in the second quarter of 2026, among the longest in the country.

Families sometimes hear "five or six years" and relax. Don't. Interest, late charges, the lender's legal fees and any property taxes or insurance the lender advances all keep being added to the debt the whole time. A house with $70,000 of equity at the first missed payment can have much less by the time a referee's sale is scheduled, and an empty house collects code violations, break-ins and frozen pipes along the way. The long timeline is useful only as room to sell on your own terms. If a case has already been filed, our guide to selling to stop a foreclosure explains the options.

🧾 Transfer Tax and Closing Costs, Upstate and in the City

New York is an attorney-closing state: each side normally has its own lawyer, and the seller's lawyer prepares the deed and the transfer tax returns. Expect these seller-side costs:

  • 🏛️ State real estate transfer tax: $2 per $500 of the price, or 0.4%, customarily paid by the seller.
  • 🏙️ New York City Real Property Transfer Tax: for a one- to three-family house, condo or co-op in the five boroughs, 1% of the price at $500,000 or less and 1.425% above that, also usually the seller's.
  • 💎 "Mansion tax": 1% on residential sales of $1 million or more, rising on a sliding scale above $2 million in the city. It's paid by the buyer, but it shapes offers at those prices.
  • ⚖️ Seller's attorney: roughly $1,000 to $2,500 upstate and $2,000 to $4,000 in the city for a standard sale. Drafting a power of attorney with property-specific modifications usually adds $300 to $750.

So a $700,000 house in Queens carries about $9,975 in city transfer tax plus $2,800 in state transfer tax, close to $12,800 before the broker or the lawyer. The same math on a $180,000 house in Utica is $720.

🔀 Five Ways Forward, Judged Honestly

Get the power of attorney signed now, then decide

Do this first in almost every case

Whatever happens with the house, someone outside needs authority to deal with the lender, the insurer, the tax collector and a buyer. A New York statutory short form signed before sentencing is the cleanest version. Signed later inside a DOCCS facility, it still usually works. It costs a few hundred dollars and one well-planned visit, and it keeps every other option open.

List it on the open market through the agent

Best price when the house is sound

If the house is in decent shape, insurable, and the mortgage isn't deep in default, an agent acting under a recorded power of attorney can hire a listing broker and sell it like anyone else. On a house a lender will finance, this usually nets more than any cash offer, including ours. The costs are time, a commission, and someone willing to manage showings, inspections and the buyer's attorney for two to four months.

Sell for cash, as-is

When time, condition or title fights the listing

Cash makes sense when the house needs work a retail buyer's lender won't accept, when it's vacant through a Rochester or Buffalo winter, when the foreclosure is past the settlement conference, or when liens and judgments make a retail buyer's attorney nervous. Expect 60% to 80% of the repaired value. That discount is real money, and you should only accept it when the alternative costs more.

Have the owner sign the deed inside at closing

Workable for one quick, simple deal

Skipping the power of attorney and having the owner sign a deed inside can work for a buyer who's ready to close and a title company that has seen it before. But every correction, every revised transfer tax form, every lender payoff question has to go back through the facility. With 72-hour notary scheduling at best, one error can cost two weeks.

Hold the house until release

Only with a paid mortgage and a plan

If the mortgage is paid off, a family member lives there and pays the taxes, and the sentence is short, keeping the house can be the right answer. Be clear-eyed about it otherwise. An empty upstate house costs money every month, and a state sentence means any power the owner holds over other people's affairs is suspended in the meantime.

💵 A Worked Example: A Vacant House in Monroe County

Say the owner is serving a four-year state sentence. The house is a 1950s ranch in a town outside Rochester, empty since the arrest. Repaired, similar houses sell for about $240,000. It needs a roof, a furnace and repairs to a bathroom where a pipe froze: about $30,000 of work. As it sits, it's worth maybe $205,000 to a retail buyer who can get financing. The owner's sister holds a power of attorney signed before sentencing.

Holding it costs about $900 a month. Monroe County's average effective property tax rate is roughly 2.36% of market value, so on $205,000 that's about $4,840 a year, or $403 a month. Vacant-home insurance runs about $150 to $250 a month. Keeping the heat on so the pipes don't freeze again, plus electricity, averages around $200 a month over the year. Snow removal, lawn care and weekly checks run another $100 to $150. That's before any mortgage payment, and before a single code violation.

Listing it as-is at $205,000, after a 5% commission ($10,250), state transfer tax ($820), the seller's attorney ($1,500), about $4,000 of repair credits after inspection, and five months of carrying costs ($4,500), nets roughly $183,900 before the mortgage payoff.

A cash sale at $168,000, which is 70% of the repaired value, closing in three to four weeks, minus state transfer tax ($672) and the seller's attorney ($1,500), nets about $165,800 before the payoff.

Listing wins by about $18,000. If the sister has the time and the house can pass a lender's appraisal, she should list it, and we'd tell her that. The math flips if the furnace failure means the house can't be insured or financed, if the mortgage has been in default long enough that a referee's sale is on the calendar, or if a judgment on the title scares off retail buyers. And in any route, if the owner's conviction is for a specified crime, the buyer's notice to the Office of Victim Services comes along with the sale; it doesn't change the price, but it can change how soon the money can be used.

📋 What To Do This Week

  1. Find out exactly where the owner is in the case: arraigned and awaiting trial, awaiting sentence, or sentenced. Use the DOCCS incarcerated lookup or call the jail. If sentencing hasn't happened, call the defense lawyer about signing a power of attorney before it does.
  2. Ask the defense lawyer what the conviction is, or is likely to be, and whether it's a "specified crime" under Executive Law section 632-a. Ask about restitution and whether a certificate of relief from disabilities was issued or can be sought.
  3. Pull the deed from the County Clerk, or from ACRIS in New York City. Note whether a spouse is on title and whether any judgments or liens are docketed against the owner.
  4. Check whether the person inside is an agent on anyone else's power of attorney or an executor in anyone's will. If so, line up the successor now.
  5. Prepare the statutory short form with the real estate authority granted and the property named in the modifications. Have the title company you'll use review the draft before anyone sends it in.
  6. Through the facility's law library, request notary access and ask who can serve as the second witness. Then send the completed form with instructions, and arrange for the agent to sign before their own notary once it comes back.
  7. Keep the house insured. Tell the carrier it's vacant, and buy a vacancy policy if needed. Get one listing agent's opinion and one cash offer, so you're choosing between real numbers.

✅ Bottom Line

In New York, the owner's stage in the criminal case shapes the sale more than the house does. Before sentencing, the owner signs like anyone else and the victim-notice rules for funds of a convicted person don't yet apply. After a state sentence, civil rights are suspended for the term, any authority they hold over other people's affairs is suspended with them, and sale money over $10,000 may require notice to the Office of Victim Services. After a life sentence, expect a lawyer and a skeptical title company. In none of those cases does the state take the house.

So get the power of attorney done early, sign it with a notary and two proper witnesses, and record it with the deed. If the house is sound and the family has time, list it. If it's empty, cold, behind on the mortgage or tangled in judgments, ask us for a cash number and compare it honestly against the listing math above.

❓ Frequently Asked Questions

Does a New York prison sentence take away the right to own or sell a house?

No. Civil Rights Law section 79-b says a conviction does not work a forfeiture of any property. The house stays the owner's. What changes is the owner's legal status: a state prison sentence of less than life suspends civil rights for the term, and a life sentence makes the person civilly dead under section 79-a. Most sales still close through a power of attorney or a deed signed inside, but the title company should confirm its requirements first.

What does civilly dead mean for someone serving life in New York?

Civil Rights Law section 79-a says a person sentenced to life is deemed civilly dead, with exceptions for marriage and for bringing lawsuits. It does not take their property, but it raises serious doubt about whether they can sign a deed or power of attorney themselves. Expect a title insurer to want a lawyer's opinion or a court's involvement, and ask whether a certificate of relief from disabilities is available.

Does New York's Son of Sam law apply to money from selling a house?

It can. Executive Law section 632-a covers funds of a convicted person from any source, other than child support and earned income, when the person was convicted of a specified crime such as a violent felony or a class B felony. Anyone who knowingly pays such a person more than $10,000 must give written notice to the Office of Victim Services, which notifies known victims. Victims then have three years from discovery to sue.

Is it better to sell before sentencing in New York?

Often, yes. Before sentencing, the Civil Rights Law doesn't limit the owner's rights and the funds-of-a-convicted-person notice rule doesn't yet apply because there's no conviction. A power of attorney signed then is the easiest kind to defend. That isn't a license to hide money from victims or creditors; transfers designed to defeat claims can be undone.

Can my brother in state prison still act as executor of our parent's estate?

Usually not during the sentence. Section 79 suspends all private trusts, authority and powers held by a person serving a state sentence of less than life, and the Surrogate's Court can also find a person convicted of a felony ineligible when the crime could harm the estate. The successor executor named in the will, or another distributee, typically petitions for letters. Your brother still inherits his share.

How many witnesses does a New York power of attorney need?

Two, plus a notary. Under General Obligations Law section 5-1501B, the principal's signature must be acknowledged before a notary and witnessed by two people who are not named as agents or as permitted gift recipients. The notary may count as one of the two witnesses, so inside a facility you need at least one more qualified person present.

Can a DOCCS facility notary notarize a power of attorney for a house sale?

Yes. DOCCS Directive 4483 covers notary services in state prisons and requires facilities to give people in general population reasonable access to a notary within 72 hours of a request, excluding weekends and holidays. Arrange the second witness ahead of time and send the form in complete, with the property named in the modifications section.

Does the agent have to sign the New York power of attorney too?

Yes. The agent must sign and date the form with the signature acknowledged before a notary, and the power of attorney takes effect for that agent on the date of that acknowledgment. The agent can sign weeks after the principal; a lapse of time between the two signatures doesn't invalidate it.

Does the New York homestead exemption protect a house while the owner is in prison?

Possibly not. CPLR 5206 protects $204,825, $170,700 or $102,400 of equity depending on the county, as adjusted April 1, 2024, but only for property owned and occupied as a principal residence, and the statute says the protection ends when the home stops being occupied. If a spouse or children still live there, the analysis is different. Ask a New York lawyer before relying on it.

Who pays transfer tax when a New York house is sold?

The seller customarily pays the New York State transfer tax of $2 per $500 of the price, or 0.4%. In New York City the seller also usually pays the city's Real Property Transfer Tax, 1% for homes sold at $500,000 or less and 1.425% above that. Buyers of homes priced at $1 million or more pay the separate mansion tax.
