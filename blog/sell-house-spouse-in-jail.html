Your Husband or Wife Is in Jail. Being Married Doesn't Let You Sign for Them.

There is no spouse exception to a deed. What matters is whose name is on the title, what your state says about married couples and the family home, and whether your spouse will sign anything at all. Sort those three things out and you'll know within a day which road you're on.

By Dustin Ray, Founder of Pages of Purpose LLC. Not legal advice — laws differ by state, and spousal property rules differ more than most. Before you sign or file anything, talk to an attorney licensed in the state where the property is.

🤝 Need a buyer, not just answers? Pages of Purpose specializes in buying houses when an owner or co-owner is in jail or prison. See how we handle these sales.

Most families who land here are asking some version of one question: my husband just went to jail, can I sell the house? Many assume a wife can act for her husband because they're married. Banks, title companies and county recorders don't work that way. Marriage gives you no authority to sign your spouse's name to anything. Only a valid power of attorney, a court order, or your spouse's own signature does that.

What marriage can do is the opposite. In many states it gives your spouse a say over a house that has only your name on it. So the answer runs both directions, and you can be surprised either way.

🪪 The Myth: "I'm Their Spouse, So I Can Sign"

Sign your husband's name on a deed, even with his blessing over a jail phone, and you've created a forged instrument. A title company will catch it, or it'll surface years later when someone tries to sell or refinance. Verbal permission is worthless here. The same goes for a letter he mails you saying it's fine. A deed needs a signature that was notarized, and the notary has to have seen the signer.

The lawful routes are short. Your spouse signs the deed inside, in front of a notary. Your spouse signs a power of attorney naming you, and you then sign for them, with the POA recorded alongside the deed. Or a court gives you authority, which is slower and rarer than people hope.

📜 Four Deeds, Four Different Answers

Pull the deed before you do anything else. It's public record at the county recorder or land records office, and most counties have it online. Then find your situation below.

Both names on the deed

Your spouse must sign, or give a POA

You can't sell the whole house alone. In states with tenancy by the entirety, you can't even sell your half alone, because neither spouse owns a separate half. Our Pennsylvania guide shows how that plays out. The practical task here is one notarized POA, and the rest of the sale runs normally.

Only your spouse's name on the deed

Your signature alone does nothing

You have no power to sell, even if you've paid the mortgage for fifteen years. Your spouse signs or gives a POA, and you may also need to sign to release your own marital rights. If you put money in and your name isn't on title, talk to a lawyer before any money moves, because that's a claim you'd want protected.

Only your name on the deed, common-law state

Often possible, but check for joinder

You may be able to sell by yourself, but many states still make a non-owner spouse sign. Ohio's dower right is the classic example; our Ohio guide explains it. Florida requires the spouse to join in a sale of the homestead no matter whose name is on it. A title company will tell you what they require in a five-minute call.

Only your name on the deed, community-property state

Assume two signatures until proven otherwise

Nine states use community property: Arizona, California, Idaho, Louisiana, Nevada, New Mexico, Texas, Washington and Wisconsin. A house bought during the marriage is presumed to belong to both spouses, whatever the deed says. California's Family Code section 1102 says both spouses must join, personally or through an authorized agent, to sell community real property. Texas Family Code section 5.001 goes further for homesteads: neither spouse may sell the homestead without the other joining, whether it's community or separate property.

🚫 Before You Ask: Is There a No-Contact Order?

⚠️ Read this first if the charge involves you. If the case is a domestic violence charge, or you're a listed victim or witness, there may be a no-contact order. A letter, a message through a relative, or a phone call asking your spouse to sign house papers can violate it. That can mean new charges for them and real trouble in their case. Don't reach out yourself. Have your lawyer contact their defense lawyer, or ask the court to allow contact limited to the property.

It's an easy thing to miss when you're focused on notaries. A good family-law or criminal attorney can usually set up a lawyer-to-lawyer exchange of documents, which keeps everyone on the right side of the order.

🙅 When They Won't Sign, or Can't

A POA requires a willing signer who understands what they're signing. If your spouse refuses, nobody can make them, short of a court. Your options then depend on the state, and they're narrower than people expect.

  • A court order authorizing the sale. California's Family Code section 1101(e) lets a court order a transaction when a spouse's consent can't be obtained and the transaction serves the community. Our California jail guide covers how that works.
  • Guardianship-based routes. Texas Family Code section 5.003 lets the competent spouse sell a community homestead alone, but only after the other spouse has been judicially declared incapacitated. Being locked up isn't incapacity. Someone who's competent and in prison doesn't qualify, and courts won't stretch the word.
  • Divorce or legal separation. A divorce court can divide the property or order it sold, and it can sign off when one party won't. It's the most common fix when the marriage is ending anyway. See our guide to selling during a divorce.
  • Partition, if you're co-owners but not protected as spouses. After divorce you're usually tenants in common, and either co-owner can ask a court to force a sale. See also what one co-owner can and can't do alone.

Expect any court route to run several months and cost several thousand dollars, often $3,000 to $10,000 with a contested hearing. So it's worth one honest try at getting a signature first. Spouses who refuse in week one sometimes change their minds once they see the foreclosure letter.

For most couples the POA wins easily. It's a single trip for the notary, and from then on you can deal with the title company, the lender and the insurer directly. Ask that it cover titled vehicles and bank accounts too, because the question "can I sell his truck?" usually follows the house question. A car in your spouse's name has the same rule as the house: their signature or their agent's.

🧾 Their Legal Trouble May Follow the House

Three things can land on the title because of the criminal case, and a title search will find them.

  • A bail or bond lien. If the house was pledged for bond, that lien has to be released before closing. It usually comes off when the case ends or the bond is exonerated, and it's worth calling the bondsman this week.
  • Restitution and fines. Depending on the state, these can become judgments that attach to your spouse's interest. Money you'd expect to split may go to those first. Tenancy by the entirety can shield a house from one spouse's separate debts in some states, which is a reason not to break that title casually.
  • Attorney fee liens. Some defense lawyers take a deed of trust on the home as payment. If you signed one, it'll show up.

Also keep in mind that your own name may not be on the mortgage even if it's on the deed, and that the servicer won't talk to you without authorization. If you're not on the loan, a POA also gets you standing to call about a hardship plan. Foreclosure timelines keep running no matter what's going on in court; see our guide to stopping foreclosure by selling.

💵 What the Choice Looks Like in Dollars

Say you own a house worth $260,000 fixed up, in fair condition, with $120,000 left on the mortgage. Income dropped when your husband was arrested, and you're carrying it alone.

  • Monthly cost to hold it: mortgage $1,150, property tax around $220 at a 1% rate, insurance $150, utilities $180, upkeep $100. That's about $1,800 a month. If you move out and it sits empty, a vacant-home policy can run $150 to $250 a month, and a standard policy may stop covering it.
  • Listing: at $250,000 after light repairs of about $6,000, less 6% commission and roughly 2% other closing costs, you'd net around $104,000 after the mortgage. Add 60 to 90 days of holding, about $4,500, and you're near $99,500.
  • Cash sale: cash buyers usually pay about 60-80% of repaired value. At 72%, that's $187,200, and with the buyer covering closing costs you'd net about $67,000 after the mortgage, in two to four weeks.

That's a $32,000 gap. If your spouse will sign and you can carry the house for three months, list it. A cash offer earns its discount only when something forces the issue: a foreclosure date, a house that won't pass inspection, or a spouse whose signature might not be available in 90 days because they're about to be moved. 📋 What To Do This Week

  1. Pull the deed and note exactly how title is held, including words like "husband and wife," "tenants by the entirety" or "joint tenants."
  2. Find out if any no-contact order exists. If it does, stop and call a lawyer before contacting your spouse.
  3. Call a local title company, give them the deed and your state, and ask who must sign.
  4. Find your spouse's current location and inmate number, and call the facility about notary visits.
  5. Have a POA drafted for the state where the house sits, and ask that it also cover vehicles and accounts.
  6. Call the mortgage servicer, tell them about the hardship, and write down the name of whoever you spoke with.
  7. Confirm the homeowner's policy is still in force, and tell the insurer if the house will be empty.

✅ Bottom Line

You can't sign for your spouse just because you're married, and you may not be free to sell even a house in your own name. Read the deed, find out how your state treats married owners, and check for a no-contact order. If your spouse is willing, one notarized POA settles most of it. If they aren't, you're looking at a court, and the question becomes whether the marriage or the house gives out first. Either way, a five-minute call with a title company will tell you more than a month of worrying.

❓ Frequently Asked Questions

Can I sell my husband's house if he's in jail?

Not on your own signature. If the house is in his name, he has to sign the deed in front of a notary or give you a power of attorney. Marriage alone gives you no authority to sign his name, and a signature he approved over the phone is still a forgery.

Can I sell a house that's only in my name while my spouse is in jail?

Sometimes. In a common-law state without a joinder rule you may be able to sell alone. In community-property states, and in states with homestead joinder or dower rules, your spouse will probably have to sign even though the deed shows only your name. Ask a title company before you sign a contract.

Does jail count as incapacity so I can sell without my spouse?

Generally no. Incapacity means a court has found a person unable to manage their affairs. Someone who is competent and locked up still has full legal capacity, so statutes like Texas Family Code section 5.003, which require a judicial declaration of incapacity, don't apply.

What if my spouse in jail refuses to sign?

Nobody can force a signature without a court. Depending on your state, you may ask a court to authorize the sale, or file for divorce or legal separation and have the court deal with the house. Those routes take months and usually cost several thousand dollars.

Can I contact my spouse about the house if there's a no-contact order?

Not directly, and not through a relative. Asking for a signature can violate the order. Have your attorney contact your spouse's defense attorney, or ask the court to allow limited contact about the property.

If my husband goes to jail, can I sell his truck or other things?

Anything with a title in his name needs his signature or a power of attorney, the same as a house. Household goods are less clear and depend on who owns them. A POA that covers vehicles and personal property solves this along with the house.

Does the mortgage company have to talk to me if I'm not on the loan?

No, not without authorization. A power of attorney or a signed third-party authorization from your spouse lets the servicer discuss the account with you. Foreclosure deadlines don't pause while you arrange that.

Can a restitution order take our house?

It can attach to your spouse's interest, depending on the state and the type of order. Whether your share is protected depends on how title is held. Tenancy by the entirety protects against one spouse's separate debts in some states, so see a lawyer before changing title.

Should I just have my spouse quitclaim the house to me?

It can be simpler than a POA when you're keeping the house, and it may trigger questions from a lender or creditors. It's also permanent and takes the same notarized signature, so talk to a lawyer about the debts in the case before choosing it.

Will a cash buyer work with a spouse who's in jail?

Yes, that's a large part of what we do. The signature rules are the same as in any sale, so the buyer still needs a valid POA or a notarized deed. A good buyer's advantage is that they'll wait for the paperwork and won't walk because a closing date moved.
