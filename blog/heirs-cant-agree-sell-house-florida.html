Heirs Can't Agree on a Florida House? Find Out First Whether It Was Homestead

In most states, when heirs fight over a house, somebody files for partition and a judge orders it sold. Florida is different when the house was the parent's homestead and a spouse survived: the law can split ownership into a life estate and a remainder, and in that shape nobody can force a sale. Whether your family is stuck in that trap, or in an ordinary co-ownership fight a court can end, decides everything else. Here's how to tell, and what to do about it.

By Dustin Ray, Founder of Pages of Purpose LLC. Not legal advice. Florida homestead and probate law is technical, it changes, and laws differ by state. Before anyone signs, files, or makes an election, talk to an attorney licensed in Florida, where the property is.

The call usually goes like this. Mom or Dad died owning a house in Florida. A stepparent, or the surviving parent, still lives there. The children want to sell, or one child wants to move in, or one wants to keep it as a rental and the others want their money. Someone looked up "partition" and assumed a judge could simply order the house sold and the money divided. Then a Florida lawyer told them it may not be that simple.

It isn't, because of the Florida homestead rules. Before you argue about price, repairs, or who gets what, you need to answer one question: was this house the deceased owner's Florida homestead, and did a spouse or a minor child survive? The answer determines who owns what, who can veto a sale, and whether any court in Florida can break the tie.

🏠 The Homestead Question Comes Before the Family Fight

Florida's Constitution (Article X, Section 4(c)) restricts how a homestead can be passed on. If the owner was survived by a spouse or a minor child, the owner generally could not leave the homestead to whoever they wanted in a will. The only exception: with no minor child, it can be left to the spouse. A will that tries to leave it elsewhere doesn't control the house. Instead, the house passes under the descent statute, Section 732.401 of the Florida Statutes.

Under Section 732.401, when a spouse and descendants survive, the spouse takes a life estate in the homestead and the descendants take a vested remainder. In plain terms, the spouse has the right to live in or use the house for the rest of their life, and the children own what's left after the spouse dies. A stepparent and a set of stepchildren can end up owning the same house in two different layers, often without anyone having planned it.

That layering is the source of most Florida inheritance deadlocks we see. It's also why advice from friends in other states is so often wrong. In Georgia or Ohio, two siblings who inherit a house usually own it as co-owners, and either one can ask a court to divide or sell it. In Florida, a spouse with a life estate and children with a remainder aren't co-owners in that sense. They own different interests in time.

🧭 Three questions that sort out your situation

  1. Was the house the owner's permanent residence (usually it carried the homestead exemption on the property appraiser's records)?
  2. Did a spouse survive? A spouse can include a second or third spouse the children barely know.
  3. Did a minor child survive? Then the will cannot leave the house away from the family, and any sale will likely involve a court-supervised guardian for the child's share.

If the answer to the first question is no (the house was a rental, a vacation place, or the owner lived elsewhere), it's an ordinary estate asset. The personal representative can usually sell it, and the family fight looks like one in any other state. If the answers are yes and yes, keep reading. The next section is the one that matters most.

⏳ The Six-Month Election Decides Whether the Deadlock Can Be Broken

Section 732.401(2) gives the surviving spouse a choice. Instead of the life estate, the spouse may elect to take an undivided one-half interest in the homestead as a tenant in common, with the other half going to the descendants. The election must be made within 6 months after the death, and while the spouse is alive. There is a narrow petition process for extending it, but families should not plan around it.

That choice matters far beyond the spouse's own share. It changes which legal tools exist later:

  • 🔒 Life estate plus remainder (no election). Florida's partition statute, Section 64.031, lets tenants in common, joint tenants and coparceners bring partition. A life tenant and remaindermen aren't any of those. Florida courts have held that a life tenant can't partition against the remaindermen, and the remaindermen don't have a partition claim against the life tenant either. In practice, no one can force a sale. It takes agreement.
  • 🔓 Spouse elects one-half as tenant in common. Now the spouse and children are co-owners. Any of them can file for partition, and a deadlock has an ending.
⚠️ The six-month clock is the most important date in a Florida homestead inheritance. If a spouse survived and the family already expects to disagree about the house, the spouse should talk to a Florida probate attorney about the election well before the 6-month mark. Once it passes, the family may be locked into a life estate that only unanimous agreement, or the spouse's death, can undo.

The election isn't automatically better for the spouse. A life estate gives the right to the whole house for life. A one-half interest gives half the value now, but it exposes the spouse to a partition suit by the children. A spouse who wants to stay in the house usually keeps the life estate. A spouse who wants out, or can't afford the house alone, often benefits from electing. The Florida Bar Journal has published commentary describing surviving spouses who are "trapped" in life estates they can no longer afford. That's the scenario the election is meant to avoid.

💸 Who Pays While the Family Argues

Carrying costs are where Florida inheritance disputes turn bitter, and the law splits them in a way most families don't expect.

While the life estate is in place, Section 732.401(3) points to Chapter 738 of the Florida Statutes. Under Sections 738.701 to 738.705, as applied by Section 738.801, the life tenant generally pays ordinary expenses: recurring property taxes, insurance premiums, routine maintenance, and the interest portion of any mortgage. The remaindermen generally bear improvements likely to outlast the life estate, such as a new roof.

That split creates the classic standoff. A surviving spouse on a fixed income pays the taxes and insurance on a house they don't fully own. Children who live elsewhere pay almost nothing and see the value grow. The spouse wants to sell; the children have little reason to agree. Or the reverse: the children want cash now, and the spouse is comfortable staying put for twenty years.

Once a spouse elects tenancy in common, expenses are shared in proportion to ownership, starting when the election is recorded. If one heir is paying everything, a partition case lets the court account for those payments when it divides the proceeds.

🧾 What carrying a vacant Florida house actually costs

Many families don't realize the property tax bill can jump after a death. While the parent lived there, the house usually had the homestead exemption and the Save Our Homes cap, which limits how fast the assessed value rises. According to the Florida Department of Revenue, when homestead status is lost or ownership changes, the house loses the Save Our Homes benefit and is reassessed at just value the following January 1, with some exceptions for transfers at death. If no heir moves in and claims homestead, the next tax bill can be far larger than anything the parent paid.

🏛️ When Partition Is Available: Florida's Chapter 64 and the Heirs Property Act

If the heirs are co-owners (because the house wasn't protected homestead, because there was no surviving spouse, or because the spouse elected one-half), Chapter 64 of the Florida Statutes governs partition. The case is filed in circuit court in the county where the property sits.

Florida also adopted the Uniform Partition of Heirs Property Act in 2020, codified at Sections 64.201 to 64.214. It applies to "heirs property," which, under Section 64.202, is generally a house held by tenants in common, with no binding agreement on partition, where at least one owner got title from a relative, and where relatives hold at least 20% of the interests or make up at least 20% of the owners. Most inherited family houses qualify. The Act changes the fight in three ways:

  • 💲 The court determines value first (Section 64.206), usually by appraisal.
  • 🤝 Cotenant buyout. Under Section 64.207, once value is set, the heirs who don't want a sale get 45 days to elect to buy out the ones who asked for one, at the court-determined value times each seller's fractional share. The court sets a payment deadline at least 60 days after notice.
  • 🏷️ Open-market sale, not a courthouse auction. If the house is sold, Section 64.210 requires an open-market sale through a Florida-licensed broker at no less than the determined value, unless sealed bids or an auction would be better for the owners. Partition in kind (physically dividing the land) is preferred when it's practical, but it rarely is for a single-family house.

Costs are shared. Under Section 64.081, the attorneys' fees for the partition are generally apportioned among the parties according to their interests and can be paid out of the sale proceeds, along with taxes due. That keeps one heir from bankrupting the others with litigation, but it also means everyone's share shrinks as the case drags on. A contested partition in Florida often runs 6 to 18 months and can cost $10,000 to $40,000 or more in combined fees.

⚖️ What the Personal Representative Can and Can't Do

Families often assume the executor (in Florida, the personal representative) can settle the matter by selling the house. Sometimes that's true. Often, with homestead, it isn't.

For an ordinary estate asset, a personal representative can sell under a power of sale in the will, or, without one, under Section 733.613, which says no title passes until the court authorizes or confirms the sale. Heirs who object can be heard, but they can't simply block it.

Protected homestead is different. Section 733.608 makes the decedent's property an estate asset "except the homestead," and Florida probate courts routinely issue an order determining homestead status, which confirms the house passed directly to the heirs at death, outside the estate and generally free of the decedent's unsecured creditors. The upside is that creditors can't force a sale. The downside for a family that disagrees is that the personal representative usually can't sell it either. A buyer's title insurer will typically want deeds from every person who received an interest, and from the spouse as life tenant.

Filing a petition to determine homestead usually costs a court filing fee plus attorney time, often a few thousand dollars. For context, Florida clerks charge around $400 to open a formal administration and $235 to $345 for summary administration, depending on estate size.

🧩 Ways Florida Families Actually Get Unstuck

🤝 Everyone signs and lists the house

Best when the house is sound and the family can agree on a price

If the life tenant and all remaindermen, or all co-owners, will sign, list it with a good local agent. On a house in decent shape, the open market almost always pays more than any cash buyer. Agree in writing up front how proceeds are split. For a life estate, that usually means an actuarial value for the spouse's share based on age, with the rest to the remaindermen.

🏡 The spouse rents, or the family waits

Fine when nobody needs money and the spouse can afford it

A life tenant can live there or rent the house and keep the rent. Waiting costs nothing in legal fees, but it can mean years of taxes and insurance, and rental can cost the house its homestead tax treatment.

💵 Buy out the person who wants out

Best when one side wants the house and can finance it

A child who wants to keep the house can buy the spouse's life estate, or a spouse can buy the remainder. Get an appraisal and an actuarial calculation so the price is defensible. With co-owners, the Heirs Property Act buyout gives the keepers a court-backed version of this.

🧾 Sell one heir's share

Best when one person needs out and the others won't move

An heir can sell their own undivided interest, or their remainder interest, without the others. The price is well below a proportional share of the whole, because the buyer inherits the dispute. It turns an illiquid share into money without a lawsuit.

⚖️ File for partition

Only when the heirs are co-owners and talks have failed

Available to tenants in common, not against a life estate. Expect months, shared fees, and a court-set value. Often the filing itself is what finally brings everyone to the table.

💵 A worked example

Say a house in Lakeland would be worth $300,000 fully updated. It needs a roof, a new A/C system and cosmetic work, about $40,000 in total. A cash buyer will typically offer 60% to 80% of repaired value depending on condition and location; for this house, something like $200,000 to $215,000 is realistic. Listing it as-is might bring $250,000, less about $15,000 in commissions and closing costs, and it might take three to five months at roughly $1,200 a month in carrying costs. If everyone agrees and can wait, listing usually nets more, roughly $25,000 to $30,000 here. If the family will spend another year fighting, those carrying costs and legal fees close most of that gap.

For a life estate, the split is the other half of the math. A surviving spouse in their late 70s might hold a life estate worth a modest share of the proceeds under the IRS actuarial tables commonly used for this; a spouse in their 50s would hold a much larger one. Get the number calculated before anyone negotiates. It often ends the argument by itself.

🧒 When a Minor Child Is One of the Heirs

A minor can't sign a deed. If a minor child holds a remainder or a share, someone, usually a court-appointed guardian of the property, has to act for the child, and a Florida court will typically need to approve the sale and supervise the child's share of the money. Add a few months and the cost of a guardianship. It's one more reason to start with a Florida probate attorney rather than a listing agent.

📋 What To Do This Week

  1. Write down the date of death and count six months forward. If a spouse survived, that's the election deadline.
  2. Pull the property appraiser's record to see whether the homestead exemption was on the house and what the current just value is.
  3. List every heir, including a surviving spouse, minor children, and the children of any child who died earlier.
  4. Get the will, if there is one, and have a Florida probate attorney say whether it controls the house or whether Section 732.401 does.
  5. Insure the house as vacant if no one lives there, and keep the power and A/C on.
  6. Get one appraisal everyone accepts before anyone makes or rejects an offer.
  7. Put the options and their dollar outcomes in writing for every heir. Disagreements often shrink once everyone sees the same numbers.

✅ Bottom Line

A Florida family that can't agree about a parent's house needs to answer the homestead question before anything else. If a spouse survived and kept the life estate, no one can force a sale: the way out is agreement, a buyout, or waiting. If the heirs are co-owners, Chapter 64 and the Heirs Property Act give everyone a path, with a buyout right for the people who want to keep the house and an open-market sale if they can't.

❓ Frequently Asked Questions

Can heirs force the sale of an inherited house in Florida?

If the heirs own the house as tenants in common, any one of them can usually file a partition action under Chapter 64 of the Florida Statutes, and the court can order a sale. If the house was the decedent's homestead and a surviving spouse holds a life estate, the children own a remainder, and partition generally isn't available between a life tenant and remaindermen, so a sale requires agreement.

What is the six-month homestead election in Florida?

Under Section 732.401(2), a surviving spouse may elect, instead of a life estate, to take an undivided one-half interest in the homestead as a tenant in common, with the other half going to the descendants. The election must be made within 6 months after the death and during the spouse's lifetime.

Can a surviving spouse with a life estate sell the house alone?

No. A life tenant can sell only the life estate itself, which few buyers want. Selling the whole house requires the remaindermen to sign as well. A life tenant can live in the house or rent it out and keep the rent.

Can my stepmother stay in Dad's Florida house for the rest of her life?

If the house was Dad's homestead, she was his spouse, and she did not elect the one-half interest, she generally holds a life estate and can stay for life. The children own the remainder and take full ownership when she dies.

Who pays property taxes on a Florida house held as a life estate?

Under Chapter 738 of the Florida Statutes, the life tenant generally pays ordinary expenses such as recurring property taxes, insurance and mortgage interest. The remaindermen generally bear the cost of improvements that will outlast the life estate.

Can the personal representative sell a Florida homestead over the heirs' objections?

Usually not when it is protected homestead. Protected homestead passes directly to the heirs at death and is excluded from the estate assets the personal representative controls, so a buyer will normally need deeds from the people who inherited. A non-homestead house can usually be sold by the personal representative under a power of sale or with court authorization.

Does Florida have the Uniform Partition of Heirs Property Act?

Yes. Florida adopted it in 2020 at Sections 64.201 to 64.214 of the Florida Statutes. For qualifying inherited property, it requires a court-determined value, gives cotenants who oppose a sale a right to buy out those seeking one, and favors an open-market sale through a licensed broker over an auction.

How long does a partition lawsuit take in Florida?

An uncontested or quickly settled case can wrap up in a few months. A contested case with appraisals, a buyout period and a broker sale commonly takes 6 to 18 months.

Will the property taxes go up after my parent dies in Florida?

Often, yes. If no heir moves in and claims the homestead exemption, the house can lose its homestead exemption and Save Our Homes cap and be reassessed at just value the following January 1, which can raise the bill substantially.

Is it better to list an inherited Florida house or sell it for cash?

If every heir will sign, the house is in reasonable shape, and nobody needs money immediately, listing usually nets more. A cash sale makes more sense when the house needs major repairs, carrying costs are piling up, or the family needs certainty and a fast close.
