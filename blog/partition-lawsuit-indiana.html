Partition Lawsuits in Indiana: The Statute Sends You to Mediation First, and Sells the House if That Fails

Indiana is the rare state that wrote mediation into its partition statute as a command. Under IC 32-17-4-2.5 the court appraises the property within 30 days, refers the case to a mediator within 45, and must warn the owners in the same order that the property will be sold if they cannot agree within 60. There are no commissioners and no in-kind preference for a house. Here is how the clock runs and where the money goes. Or ask us a question, free.

Almost every state treats mediation in a property lawsuit as optional — something a judge can suggest, or that a local rule encourages, or that the lawyers arrange when trial gets close. Indiana wrote it into the partition statute as a command.

IC 32-17-4-2.5(a) says that not later than 45 days after the court has acquired jurisdiction over all the parties with an interest in the property, the court shall refer the matter to mediation in accordance with the Indiana rules of alternative dispute resolution. Not "may." Not "on motion of a party." Every partition case in Indiana goes to a mediator, and the mediator gets the file before any judge decides anything about who owns what or what the property is worth to each of them.

That one sentence shapes everything else. An Indiana partition case is not primarily a trial about dividing land. It is a court-enforced settlement conference with a sale hanging over it, and the parties who do well are the ones who treat the mediation as the main event rather than a formality on the way to a hearing.

⚖️ Mediation Is Not Optional Here

The statute's referral is to Indiana's Rules for Alternative Dispute Resolution, which govern civil mediation statewide. In practice that means a neutral mediator — typically drawn from Indiana's registry of qualified civil mediators — a confidential session the parties attend with their lawyers, and a report back to the court saying only whether the case resolved.

Three things about that session matter more than the law students' version of it:

  • Nothing said in mediation comes back at a hearing. Indiana's ADR rules make the process confidential, which is exactly why a co-owner can float a number they would never put in a pleading.
  • The mediator has no power to decide anything. They cannot order a sale, set a price or rule on who owns what. Their leverage is entirely informational: everyone in the room already knows what the court does if nobody agrees.
  • Somebody pays for it. Indiana mediators commonly charge in the range of $200 to $400 an hour, usually split among the parties, so a half-day session on a single-family house often costs the group $800 to $1,500 in total. That is cheap compared with what the statute's default costs.

And the court has to tell everyone what the default is. Under subsection (d), the referral order must advise the parties that the property will be sold if they are unable to reach an agreement within 60 days of the order, and that they may agree on the method of sale — and that if they do not, the property may be sold at public auction or by the sheriff.

📅 The 30–45–60 Clock

Indiana's deadlines run off the date the court gets jurisdiction over every interested party, so service drives the whole schedule.

  1. Day 0 — jurisdiction over all parties. Everyone with an interest, identified in the title search, has been served or has appeared.
  2. Within 30 days — the appraiser. Unless every party waives the appraisal, the court appoints a licensed real estate appraiser, who files the appraisal with the court. The court then notifies the parties of the appraised value (subsections (e) and (f)).
  3. Within 45 days — referral to mediation (subsection (a)). Mediation may not begin until the appraisal report is on file, unless the appraisal was waived (subsections (b) and (c)).
  4. 60 days from the referral order — the deadline to agree (subsection (d)).
  5. Within 30 days of the mediator's failure report — or of the parties filing their agreed method — the court orders the sale: by the method all the parties agreed on, or by directing the parties to select an auctioneer (subsection (g)).
  6. 30 days to pick an auctioneer. Miss that and the court orders the sheriff to sell the property the way property is sold at execution under IC 34-55-6.

Add service time, a contested appearance or two, and the mediation calendar, and a clean Indiana partition case still takes most of a year. But the statutory spine is short, and the practical consequence is that there is no stage where delay helps a party who wants to keep the property. The clock runs toward a sale by itself.

🧾 The Appraisal, and the Mistake of Waiving It

Subsection (e) requires the court to appoint a licensed Indiana appraiser within 30 days unless the parties agree to waive the appraisal, and subsection (c) lets a case move to mediation with no appraisal when everyone has waived it. Waiving sounds efficient. It is usually a mistake.

The appraised value is the only neutral number in the case. It is what a buying co-owner's offer gets measured against, what a reluctant seller compares to an auction result, and what makes a mediator's "you are $20,000 apart" statement meaningful. Waive it and the mediation becomes an argument between one side's Zillow printout and the other side's hopes, which is how cases end up at a sheriff's sale that nobody wanted.

Appraisals on an ordinary Indiana single-family house generally run $450 to $800; a multi-unit or rural property with outbuildings costs more. It comes out of the case, and it is the best money spent in it.

⚠️ The appraisal is not a price floor. Nothing in IC 32-17-4 requires a partition sale to bring the appraised value, or any fraction of it. Unlike states that forbid confirming a sale below a set percentage of a court-fixed value, Indiana lets the auction or the sheriff's sale produce whatever it produces. The appraisal informs the negotiation; it does not protect the price.

🔍 The Title Search You File With the Petition

IC 32-17-4-2 requires the petition to describe the premises and to set out the rights and titles of the interested parties — and then requires the person filing to cause a title search to be made and to file a copy of the results with the court. Indiana front-loads the work that other states discover halfway through a case.

It is also the most useful diagnostic you will get. The search tells you who actually holds record title, whether a probate was ever opened, whether a deceased co-owner's interest passed by will, by intestacy or by a recorded transfer-on-death deed, and what is recorded against the property — mortgages, judgment liens, tax liens, sewer or ditch assessments, old mechanic's liens nobody released.

Two money points follow from it. Subsection (h) requires the court, when it orders the sale, to notify all lienholders and others with an interest identified in that search or in the title or lien search required in an estate under IC 29-1-17-11. And subsection (i) gives whoever paid for the search a right to reimbursement out of the sale proceeds. The person who files does the work, but the group pays for it.

🏛️ When an Executor, Administrator or Trustee Files

IC 32-17-4-1 is broader than most partition statutes about who may bring the case. It allows a person holding an interest as a joint tenant or tenant in common to compel partition either in their own right or as executor or trustee — and, where the sale of a deceased co-owner's estate is necessary, it allows the decedent's administrator or executor to file. Subsection (b) lets a trustee, administrator or executor be made a defendant instead, to answer as to whatever interest they hold.

That matters when a house is half owned by a living person and half by an open estate. The personal representative does not have to wait for the estate to close and distribute before dealing with the land; the fiduciary can be the plaintiff. And IC 32-17-4-23 covers the odd ownership that comes out of old wills: a person who owns an undivided fee interest in land plus a life estate in the rest of it — or the person who owns the fee subject to that life estate — can compel partition and have the fee simple interest set off and determined.

If the estate side is where you are stuck rather than the co-ownership side, our guide on what happens to a house when there is no will and the one on heirs who can't agree on selling cover that half of the problem.

🔨 Four Ways the Property Can Actually Be Sold

Indiana's statute contemplates four endings, and they do not pay the same.

1. The method everyone agrees on. Under subsections (d)(2) and (g)(1), the parties may agree on the method of sale, and the court orders that method. This is where a buyout by one co-owner, a negotiated sale to a third party, or a listing with a specific broker gets blessed. It is the only ending the parties control.

2. An auction run by an auctioneer the parties select. If mediation fails, the court orders the parties to select an auctioneer within 30 days (subsection (g)(2)). Any person may advertise the sale at their own expense, but subsection (k) gives them no reimbursement for doing it.

3. A sheriff's sale. If nobody selects an auctioneer in time, the court orders the sheriff to sell the property in the same manner as property sold at execution under IC 34-55-6 — published notice, a courthouse auction, a sheriff's deed. It is the fastest way to turn a house into the smallest number of dollars available.

4. A listed sale that replaces a scheduled auction. Subsection (n) lets all the parties, at any time before the auction, tell the court in writing that they want to sell through a real estate professional they have jointly selected at a listing price they have agreed on; the court must then rescind the auction order. If the listing expires unsold, any party can have the auction reinstated.

In our experience of these cases nationally, the spread between ending 3 and ending 4 on the same house is routinely 15% to 30% of the price. The statute hands that money to the parties if they can sign one letter together.

🧹 Liens, Easements, and the Order the Money Comes Out

Subsection (h) is the clean-title provision: the property must be sold free and clear of all liens and special assessments, except prescriptive easements, easements of record and irrevocable licenses, with any sum secured by a lien or assessment satisfied out of the proceeds. A buyer at an Indiana partition sale is not inheriting the mortgage or the judgment; the money is.

Then the waterfall, from subsections (h) through (l):

  1. Liens and special assessments, paid from the proceeds.
  2. Reimbursement to whoever paid for the title or lien search.
  3. Pro rata reimbursement to any person who paid a tax or special assessment on the property.
  4. The reasonable expenses of the sale.
  5. Whatever remains, divided among the owners in proportion to their ownership interests.

And subsection (m) closes the loop for an owner who buys: a party with an ownership interest who becomes the successful purchaser, by settlement or at auction, is given full credit for the percentage of their interest before the purchase. The practical arithmetic of that credit, and what it does to a buyout negotiation, is laid out in our guide to selling a co-owned house in Indiana.

🧭 What Indiana Does Not Have

Three absences are worth knowing, because they are the ones out-of-state advice gets wrong.

No commissioners. The 2012 rewrite repealed most of the old chapter — sections 3 through 22 of IC 32-17-4 are all marked repealed. There is no three-commissioner body walking the property and reporting on how to divide it, as Illinois, Idaho and many other states still use.

No real in-kind preference. Apart from the fee-and-life-estate situation in IC 32-17-4-23, the statute does not instruct the court to prefer physical division, and it does not require a finding that the property cannot be divided before ordering a sale. Indiana simply assumes the answer for a house.

No heirs-property buyout procedure. Nothing in Chapter 4 provides the appraisal-plus-right-of-first-refusal framework the Uniform Partition of Heirs Property Act gives family co-owners in states that adopted it, and nothing gives long-held family land extra protection from a sale. Check the Uniform Law Commission's enactment tracker for the current list of adopting states before assuming a neighbor state's rules apply in Indiana. What Indiana offers instead is the mediation requirement and the co-owner's purchase credit — useful, but you have to use them.

⌛ Time, Fees and County Practice

Partition is filed under IC 32-17-4-2 in the circuit court, superior court, or probate court with probate jurisdiction in the county where the land or any part of it is located. Marion County has a dedicated probate court; most other counties route these to the circuit or superior court docket.

Realistic timelines: a cooperative case that settles at mediation closes in roughly 5 to 9 months from filing. A case that goes to an auctioneer sale runs 9 to 14 months. Add a contested appearance, an unlocatable owner requiring service by publication, or a dispute about whether an interest passed by a transfer-on-death deed, and 14 to 20 months is normal.

Costs in the Indiana market: filing fees in the low hundreds; a title search $150 to $400; the court-appointed appraisal $450 to $800; mediation $800 to $1,500 for the group for a half-day; attorney fees $5,000 to $15,000 for an uncontested case and $20,000 to $45,000 once it is genuinely fought. An auctioneer's commission typically runs 5% to 10%; a sheriff's sale carries statutory costs instead. Indiana charges no state real estate transfer tax, but a sales disclosure form goes to the county auditor under IC 6-1.1-5.5 with a small filing fee.

🙅 When Filing Is the Wrong Move

Do not file if the only real obstacle is that nobody has made a written offer yet. Everything the statute forces — an appraisal, a facilitated conversation, a deadline, a method of sale — the owners can do voluntarily for a fraction of the cost, and the voluntary version has no filing fee, no title search requirement and no auction sitting behind it.

Do not file if the house is sound, every owner will sign, and nobody needs money in the next six months. A conventional listing beats every ending in the table above, and on the Anderson numbers it beats a cash offer from us by $15,000 to $25,000 on the whole house. We will say that plainly on the phone.

Do not file to punish a co-owner. Indiana's statute has no mechanism for that: there is no fee shifting against the uncooperative party, no finding of fault, and the money for the appraisal, the mediator and the sale comes out of the proceeds everyone shares.

Where a cash sale genuinely helps: repairs nobody can fund, a vacant house taxed at the non-homestead cap, an owner who cannot be located so no ordinary closing can happen, a judgment or tax lien that has to be paid from proceeds, or one owner who would rather sell their own undivided interest today than spend a year in a case. 🗃️ Four Exits

🤝 Settle at mediation as the agreed method of sale

Best controlled outcome

Use the court's appraisal, the purchase credit in subsection (m), and your tax receipts. An agreement on the method of sale is binding and the court orders it, so a buyout or a specific listing can end the case.

🏷️ Replace the auction with a listing under subsection (n)

Most money for everyone

All parties sign one letter naming a jointly selected real estate professional and an agreed price, before the auction happens. The court must rescind the auction order. Routinely worth 15–30% over a sheriff's sale.

📄 Do it privately before anyone files

Cheapest

One appraiser, one written agreement, one deadline, and a clause covering who gets reimbursed for taxes and repairs. Saves the filing fee, the title search, the mediator and the sale costs.

📋 What To Do Before the Mediation

  1. Read the title search that was filed with the petition, line by line. It tells you who owns what, what is recorded against the property, and whether an estate has to be involved.
  2. Do not waive the appraisal. It is the only neutral number you will get, and mediation cannot even begin until it is filed.
  3. Assemble your receipts for taxes and special assessments. They come off the top of a court sale under subsection (j), and they are a negotiating chip in a settlement.
  4. Price your own exit before you walk in: what a lender would give you to buy the others out, what a broker thinks the house brings as-is and repaired, and what a cash buyer would pay this month.
  5. Decide in advance what you will sign. If you want the house, go in with financing and a number. If you want out, go in willing to sign the subsection (n) letter, because that is the highest-price ending available to you.
  6. Calendar the 60 days. After that, the method of sale stops being your decision and becomes the court's.

✅ Bottom Line

Indiana's partition statute is a mediation statute with a sale attached. The court appraises the property within 30 days, sends the dispute to a mediator within 45, tells the owners in writing that the house will be sold if they cannot agree within 60, and then sells it — by the method they chose, by an auctioneer, or by the sheriff. There are no commissioners, no in-kind preference for a house, and no heirs-property buyout rights to fall back on.

The leverage the statute does give you is all front-loaded: keep the appraisal, bring your tax receipts, use the purchase credit if you want to own the house, and get every party to sign the letter that turns an auction into a listing. Do those four things and the worst outcomes in the table above never happen to you.

❓ Indiana Partition Questions, Answered

Is mediation mandatory in an Indiana partition case?

Yes. IC 32-17-4-2.5(a) requires the court, not later than 45 days after it has acquired jurisdiction over all the parties with an interest in the property, to refer the matter to mediation under the Indiana rules of alternative dispute resolution. It is not a motion a party has to make, and it is not discretionary with the judge.

When does an Indiana court appoint an appraiser, and should we waive it?

Unless every party waives the appraisal, the court must appoint a licensed real estate appraiser within 30 days of acquiring jurisdiction, and the appraiser files the appraisal with the court before mediation can begin. Waiving it is usually a mistake: the appraised value is the only neutral figure in the case, and without it a mediation becomes an argument between two guesses.

What happens at the mediation, and who pays the mediator?

A neutral mediator, typically from Indiana's registry of qualified civil mediators, holds a confidential session with the parties and their lawyers and reports to the court only whether the case resolved. The mediator cannot order a sale or set a price. Indiana mediators commonly charge $200 to $400 an hour, usually split among the parties, so a half-day session often costs the group $800 to $1,500.

What happens if we do not reach an agreement within 60 days?

The property is sold. Under IC 32-17-4-2.5(d) the referral order must tell the parties the property will be sold if they cannot agree within 60 days. After the mediator reports failure, the court has 30 days to order the sale by whatever method the parties agreed on, or to order the parties to select an auctioneer within 30 days, failing which the sheriff sells it.

Why does Indiana make you file a title search with the petition?

IC 32-17-4-2 requires whoever files to cause a title search to be made and to file the results with the court, along with a description of the premises and the parties' rights and titles. The search identifies the lienholders the court must notify under subsection (h), and whoever paid for it is entitled to reimbursement from the sale proceeds under subsection (i).

Can an executor, administrator or trustee file for partition in Indiana?

Yes. IC 32-17-4-1 allows a person holding an interest as a joint tenant or tenant in common to compel partition in their own right or as executor or trustee, and allows a decedent's administrator or executor to file where the sale of the decedent's estate is necessary. A trustee, administrator or executor may also be made a defendant to answer as to the interest they hold.

What does a sheriff's sale look like in an Indiana partition case?

If the parties fail to select an auctioneer within 30 days of the court's order, the court directs the sheriff to sell the property in the same manner property is sold at execution under IC 34-55-6 — published notice, a public auction at the courthouse, and a sheriff's deed to the buyer. It is the lowest-price ending available in these cases and it is the statutory default.

Does a buyer at an Indiana partition sale get clear title?

Largely, yes. IC 32-17-4-2.5(h) requires the property to be sold free and clear of all liens and special assessments except prescriptive easements, easements of record and irrevocable licenses, with any secured sum satisfied out of the proceeds. Mortgages, judgment liens and tax liens are paid from the money rather than following the property.

Can an Indiana court divide the land instead of ordering a sale?

Rarely for a house. The 2012 rewrite repealed the old commissioner sections of IC 32-17-4, and the statute does not direct the court to prefer physical division or require a finding that the property cannot be divided. The one clear in-kind route left is IC 32-17-4-23, which lets an owner holding an undivided fee interest plus a life estate, or the fee owner subject to it, have the fee set off.

How long does an Indiana partition case take and what does it cost?

A case that settles at mediation usually closes in 5 to 9 months; one that goes to an auctioneer sale runs 9 to 14 months; a contested case with service by publication or a disputed interest can take 14 to 20 months. Budget filing fees in the low hundreds, $150 to $400 for the title search, $450 to $800 for the appraisal, $800 to $1,500 for mediation, and $5,000 to $15,000 in attorney fees if nobody fights.
