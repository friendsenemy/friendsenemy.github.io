Keep It or Sell It? In a Texas Divorce, the Owelty Lien Usually Decides

Texas protects homesteads so firmly that its constitution limits what can be borrowed against one. That's why a Texas divorce buyout has its own instrument, the owelty of partition, and why a refinance that would be routine elsewhere can fail here if the paperwork is wrong. Whether an owelty will work for your numbers is the real keep-or-sell test. Here's how to run it, what happens to the house under community property, and when each path pays.

Couples in most states settle the house like this: one spouse refinances, takes out enough cash to pay the other, and that's that. In Texas that plan runs into the state constitution. Section 50 of Article XVI lists the only kinds of debt that can be secured by a homestead, and an ordinary home-equity cash-out is tightly limited, including a cap of 80% of value.

So Texas family lawyers use a tool most Americans have never heard of. If you're trying to decide whether one of you can keep the house, understanding it comes before the price, before the listing agent, and often before the settlement talks.

🪙 Owelty of Partition: The Texas Way to Pay a Spouse for the House

An owelty is a debt owed by the spouse who keeps a home to the spouse who gives it up, created in the divorce decree or a written agreement and secured by a lien on the whole property. Texas voters added it to the constitution in the 1990s, and Article XVI, § 50(a)(3) now specifically covers a debt from dividing or awarding a family homestead in a divorce. Title underwriters treat the lien as coming ahead of the staying spouse's homestead rights, which is why lenders will accept it.

Here's how it normally goes:

  1. The decree or agreement awards the house to one spouse and sets the owelty amount.
  2. The departing spouse signs an owelty deed, and an owelty deed of trust secures the amount owed.
  3. The staying spouse refinances. The new loan pays off the old mortgage and the owelty, so the departing spouse gets a check.

Because the money pays a court-recognized division instead of a voluntary cash-out, lenders generally treat this as a rate-and-term refinance. One Texas lender puts the difference plainly: 80% loan-to-value for a Texas cash-out compared with up to 95% on an owelty refinance. Limits vary by lender and loan program, so treat 95% as a ceiling, not a promise.

Get this in the paperwork, not after it. The decree must spell out the property, the amount and the terms, and the owelty deed should be recorded before the refinance closes. If those pieces are missing or vague, the lender may treat the loan as a cash-out, and you're back at the 80% cap and worse pricing. Ask a lender to review the draft language before the decree is signed.

🧪 The two-minute test

Take the mortgage balance, add what you'd owe your spouse, and divide by the house's value. Then ask whether you can qualify alone for that loan at today's rates. If the answer is under about 80%, you have choices. If it's between 80% and 95%, only a properly papered owelty is likely to do it. If you're above that, or your income can't carry the payment and the Texas tax escrow, the house is probably going to be sold, and it's better to find that out now than after a year of fighting over it.

🌾 Community Property, and Why a Texas Judge Doesn't Have to Split It Evenly

Texas is a community property state. Property acquired during the marriage is presumed to be community, and a spouse who claims otherwise generally has to prove it. Family Code § 3.003 sets that presumption, and it takes clear and convincing evidence to beat it. A house bought before the marriage, or received by gift or inheritance, is generally separate property, even if the mortgage was paid with community earnings.

Two points surprise people from common-law states:

  • "Community" doesn't mean 50/50. Under Family Code § 7.001 the court divides the estate in a way that's "just and right." Judges have broad discretion and can consider earning capacity, health, the size of separate estates and fault.
  • A separate-property house can still produce a claim. A judge generally can't take separate real estate away from its owner. But if community money paid down the mortgage or improved the house, the community may have a reimbursement claim under Family Code § 3.402. That claim is about money, not title, and it can materially affect a settlement.

So before you talk about the owelty amount, establish what the house is. Dig out the deed, the date of purchase, and any gift or inheritance records. The number you think you're splitting may not be the number a judge would use.

✍️ Homestead Signatures, Standing Orders and the 60-Day Wait

Under Family Code § 5.001, whether a homestead is community or separate, neither spouse can sell or put a lien on it unless the other joins in, with narrow exceptions. That applies even if only one spouse is on the deed. A title company will ask for both signatures while you're still married.

Timing rules also shape what you can do:

  • Waiting period. Family Code § 6.702 requires 60 days from filing before a divorce can be granted, with exceptions tied to family violence.
  • Standing orders. Dallas, Collin, Denton, Rockwall and Kaufman counties have used standing or general orders that take effect at filing and generally stop spouses from selling or hiding property. Practice varies by county and these orders can change, so check your county's current order. Harris and other counties may rely on temporary restraining orders or temporary orders instead.
  • Selling during the case. If both spouses agree, you can usually sell with a signed agreement, and the proceeds can sit in a lawyer's trust account until the decree divides them. If one refuses, the judge can deal with it in temporary orders or the final decree.

🚨 Don't Let the Mortgage Lapse: Texas Moves Fast

This is where Texas differs from a judicial foreclosure state. Most Texas home loans are foreclosed without a lawsuit. Once a loan is past the federal 120-day point, Property Code § 51.002 requires at least 20 days to cure on a residence and at least 21 days' notice of sale, and the auction happens on the first Tuesday of the month. A couple fighting over who pays can lose the house and most of the equity in a few months. 🔀 Picking a Path

Owelty refinance

Best way to keep the house

If a lender approves you on your income alone, this is usually the cleanest route. It takes the other spouse off the old debt and pays them. Don't sign a decree that depends on a refinance until a lender has reviewed it, and include a deadline and a fallback sale if the loan doesn't happen.

Sell with a listing agent

Most money if you can cooperate

Texas homes in decent condition still sell best to owner-occupants, and an agent will usually beat any cash price. You need to agree on price, repairs and showings. If you can do that, stop reading and call an agent.

Keep the house jointly for a while

Riskier than it sounds

Some decrees let a parent stay until a child finishes school, then sell. It works with trust and clear terms on taxes and repairs. Without them, you've stayed business partners with someone you just divorced.

Sell as-is for cash

Worth the discount only in some cases

When a foundation has moved, the roof is gone, payments are behind, or one spouse will sabotage showings, the speed of a cash close can protect equity that would otherwise vanish. Otherwise, it's expensive certainty.

💵 Running It on a Texas House

Consider a house in the Houston suburbs worth $340,000 with $200,000 owed, and an agreement to split the $140,000 equity evenly. Taxes in many Texas metro areas run around 2% to 2.5% of value after exemptions. Call it $7,500 a year, or about $625 a month, and homeowner's insurance is often high in this part of the state, say $300 a month. Add $150 for upkeep. That's $1,075 a month before principal and interest.

  • Owelty: the new loan needs to cover $200,000 plus $70,000, or $270,000, about 79% of value. That's under 80%, but closing costs push it over, so a properly papered owelty is much safer. The spouse keeping the house must qualify alone, at today's rate, with that tax escrow included.
  • List at $340,000: with roughly 6% in commissions and seller costs, about $20,400, the couple nets around $119,600, or $59,800 each.
  • Cash sale at 70%: $238,000 less $200,000 leaves $38,000, or $19,000 each.

For a house in good shape, that cash number isn't close, and you shouldn't take it. Now change the facts. Say the foundation needs $35,000, the roof needs $15,000 and the mortgage is three months behind with a sale date coming. The listing price drops, buyers' lenders balk, and the house could go to auction on the first Tuesday. That's when a quick cash close may protect more than it costs.

🗓️ If You're Already Divorced and the House Wasn't Dealt With

Texas has a specific fix. If a decree failed to divide the house, the former spouses become joint owners, and either can bring a post-divorce suit under Family Code § 9.201 in the court that signed the decree. Texas Law Help describes § 9.202's deadline as two years from when the other former spouse clearly denies your rights. That court can still divide the property under its just-and-right approach. A partition under Property Code chapter 23 may also be possible and generally divides the property equally. If your ex has told you that you have no claim, the clock may have started, so see a lawyer now. Our co-owner guide and Texas partition guide explain what a forced sale looks like.

📋 What To Do This Week

  1. Find the deed and the purchase date, and work out whether the house looks community or separate.
  2. Get a current payoff and value, then run the owelty math above.
  3. Ask a lender experienced with Texas owelty loans for a preapproval on one income.
  4. Read your county's standing order, if any, before listing or borrowing.
  5. Agree in writing on who pays the mortgage, taxes and insurance until the house is resolved.
  6. Give your attorney proposed owelty language to review with the lender.
  7. Check the mortgage status online. If it's behind, deal with that first.

✅ Bottom Line

In Texas, the keep-or-sell decision comes down to whether a properly documented owelty refinance works on one income. Test that first. Figure out what's community and what's separate, respect the homestead signature rule and any standing order, and don't let the mortgage slip in a state that can foreclose in weeks. If you can't refinance and the house is sound, list it. A cash sale makes sense when condition, arrears or a sale date take listing off the table.

❓ Frequently Asked Questions

What is an owelty lien in a Texas divorce?

It's a debt from the spouse keeping the house to the spouse giving it up, created by a decree or written agreement and secured by a lien on the entire property. The Texas Constitution allows this against a homestead. The staying spouse usually pays it off through a refinance.

Is an owelty refinance different from a cash-out refinance in Texas?

Yes. A Texas home-equity cash-out is capped at 80% of value and carries extra rules. A properly documented owelty refinance is generally treated as rate-and-term, and some lenders go up to 95%. Limits vary, and poor paperwork can push the loan back into cash-out treatment.

Can my spouse sell our Texas homestead without me?

Generally no. Family Code section 5.001 requires both spouses to join in a sale or encumbrance of the homestead, even if it's separate property or only one name is on the deed. Narrow exceptions exist, such as a spouse declared incapacitated by a court.

Is the house split 50/50 in a Texas divorce?

Not automatically. Texas divides the community estate in a way that's just and right, which can be unequal. Judges may consider earning power, health, fault and each spouse's separate property. A house that is separate property generally stays with its owner, though a reimbursement claim may apply.

How long does a Texas divorce take if we sell the house?

A divorce can't be granted until at least 60 days after filing, with limited exceptions. If you agree, the sale can close during or after that period with proceeds held in trust. A contested case with a dispute over the house may take many months or more.

Can I list our house after filing for divorce in Texas?

Check first. Several Texas counties use standing orders that generally prevent selling property once a case is filed, and other courts issue temporary orders. A signed agreement or court permission usually solves this. Don't list on your own without asking your lawyer.

What happens if the divorce decree didn't mention the house?

The former spouses generally hold it as co-owners. Either can sue for a post-divorce division in the court that granted the divorce. Texas Law Help reports a two-year limit that starts when your ex clearly denies your rights. Partition may also be an option.

What if I bought the house before we got married?

It's generally your separate property, and a judge usually can't award it to your spouse. But your spouse must still join in a sale if it's your homestead, and the community may have a claim if marital funds paid the mortgage or improved it.

How quickly can a Texas house be foreclosed during a divorce?

Faster than many expect. After the federal 120-day point, Texas requires at least 20 days to cure on a residence and at least 21 days' notice of sale, with auctions on the first Tuesday. Most foreclosures don't need a lawsuit. Stay current or act early.

Should we sell to a cash buyer during a Texas divorce?

Usually not if the house is in good condition and you can work together, since a listing will likely net far more. Cash can make sense when repairs are heavy, payments are behind or a foreclosure date is near. Compare net proceeds to each spouse in writing.
