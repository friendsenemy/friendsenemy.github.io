Heirs Can't Agree on a Pennsylvania House? The Inheritance Tax Clock and the Executor's Power Usually Settle It

Pennsylvania is one of the few states that taxes what children inherit, and the bill on a family house comes due nine months after death whether or not the siblings agree. It is also a state where the executor or administrator can usually sell the house without the heirs' signatures. Those two facts decide most Pennsylvania family disputes. Here is how they work, what Philadelphia adds, and when listing beats a cash offer. Or ask us a question, free.

Picture three siblings in Pennsylvania a few months after their mother's death. The oldest has letters from the Register of Wills and wants to sell the house in Scranton. The middle one lives in it and says Mom wanted him to have it. The youngest lives in Ohio and keeps getting letters from the Department of Revenue. Each of them thinks the others have to agree before anything happens.

In Pennsylvania, that assumption is mostly wrong in both directions. The heir in the house usually can't stop a sale by the personal representative, and the personal representative can't stop the inheritance tax from coming due. Understanding those two rules ends more Pennsylvania standoffs than any family meeting.

💸 Pennsylvania Taxes the Inheritance Itself, and the Clock Starts at Death

Most states have no inheritance tax at all. Pennsylvania does, and it applies to a house left to children. The Department of Revenue's rates:

  • 👫 0% to a surviving spouse, and to a parent inheriting from a child aged 21 or younger.
  • 👨‍👩‍👧 4.5% to lineal heirs: children, grandchildren and other descendants (and parents of an older child).
  • 👬 12% to siblings.
  • 🧑‍🤝‍🧑 15% to nieces, nephews, friends and everyone else.

The tax is due at death and becomes delinquent nine months after the date of death, with interest after that. If it is paid within three months of death, the estate gets a 5% discount on the tax. Property owned jointly by spouses is exempt.

Here's why that matters for a family that disagrees. On a $200,000 house left to three children, the tax on the house alone is $9,000. On the same house left to a brother and sister of the deceased, it's $24,000. That money has to come from somewhere, and in many estates the house is the only significant asset. A sibling who wants to "wait and see" is, in effect, voting to let interest accrue on a state tax bill. A personal representative who needs to pay it has a legitimate, documented reason to sell.

The warning that matters most: the nine-month inheritance tax deadline doesn't pause for a family dispute, and the personal representative is responsible for filing the return and paying the tax from estate assets. If the heirs are arguing about the house in month seven, have a Pennsylvania attorney or accountant calculate the tax and the interest now, and decide how it will be paid, before anyone's position hardens.

🗝️ The Personal Representative Can Usually Sell Without the Heirs' Signatures

Pennsylvania splits title and control in a way that surprises people. Under 20 Pa.C.S. § 301, legal title to real estate passes at death to the heirs or the beneficiaries named in the will, but it passes "subject to" all the powers of the personal representative. And those powers are broad:

  • 🏷️ Power to sell without a court order. Under § 3351, unless the will says otherwise, the executor or administrator may sell any real property that wasn't specifically devised, at a public or private sale, without asking the Orphans' Court. The heirs' consent isn't required by the statute.
  • 🏠 Specific gifts are different. If the will says "my house at 12 Elm Street to my son," that house is specifically devised, and § 3351 requires the son to join in the sale. Without his signature, the representative needs an Orphans' Court order under § 3353.
  • ⚖️ When the will takes the power away. If the will prohibits a sale or doesn't give the authority, § 3353 lets the representative ask the Orphans' Court for an order of sale.

So in the common case, a will leaving everything "equally to my children" or no will at all, the sibling with letters can sign an agreement of sale and a deed alone. Title insurers will still review the estate file, and a careful representative tells the heirs what they're doing, but the statute doesn't give each heir a veto.

🛋️ The Heir Living in the House: What § 3311 Does and Doesn't Protect

The most common Pennsylvania standoff we see is a family member who lived with the parent and stays on after the death. Section 3311 gives them a real but limited protection. In general, the personal representative must take possession of, maintain and administer the estate's real estate. But real estate occupied at death by an heir or devisee with the decedent's consent is an exception: the representative doesn't automatically take possession of it.

The same section then adds two limits. The Orphans' Court may direct the representative to take possession anyway, and "nothing in this section shall affect the personal representative's power to sell real estate occupied by an heir or devisee." In plain terms: the sibling in the house may get to stay while the estate is administered, but living there doesn't stop the sale. If the occupant refuses to cooperate with showings, an appraisal or a closing, the representative's attorney can ask the Orphans' Court for an order.

The fair way to handle it is usually to give the occupying heir a choice early: buy the others out at a value set by an independent appraisal, sign a short agreement to move by a date in exchange for a credit at closing, or plan to leave when the house is sold. Courts tend to look favorably on a representative who offered that choice and unfavorably on an occupant who ignored it.

🔄 When the Real Problem Is the Executor

Because the personal representative has so much control in Pennsylvania, the dispute often shifts from "should we sell?" to "should this person be in charge?" Heirs who think the representative is stalling, underselling to a friend, or living in the house rent-free while the tax runs have a remedy. Under 20 Pa.C.S. § 3182, the Orphans' Court can remove a representative who is wasting or mismanaging the estate, has failed to perform a duty imposed by law, has become incapacitated, has moved out of Pennsylvania without posting the security the court requires, or whose continued service is "likely to jeopardize" the estate's interests.

Removal petitions are not quick or cheap, and a court won't remove a representative just because siblings dislike the sale price. They are effective when there's evidence: missed inheritance tax deadlines, unpaid property taxes, an uninsured vacant house, or a sale to an insider well below an appraisal. Keep records of all of it.

🏙️ What Philadelphia Adds to the Picture

Philadelphia families deal with the same statutes and a few local facts:

  • 🧾 Probate fees scale with the estate. The Register of Wills, which also serves as Clerk of the Orphans' Court, charges a probate fee based on estate value; for example, $491.25 for estates between about $50,000 and $200,000, and $596.25 between $200,000 and $300,000. A caveat costs $50 and a petition $75.
  • 🏛️ Transfer tax is heavy on a sale. Since July 1, 2025, Philadelphia's realty transfer tax is 3.578% plus the state's 1%, for 4.578% of the price. Transfers from an estate to heirs are generally excluded from the tax; a sale to a buyer is not. On a $200,000 sale, that's $9,156 in transfer tax, divided between buyer and seller as the agreement of sale provides.
  • 🧩 Tangled titles have a fund. Philadelphia VIP's Tangled Title Fund, paid for by the City, can cover up to $6,500 in costs to clear title for a qualifying heir (plus up to $3,500 more in some cases for transfer tax), if household income is at or below 80% of area median income and the home is or will be their primary residence. If one heir wants to live in a Philadelphia rowhouse that's still in a grandparent's name, look there before looking at buyers.
  • 🧾 Real estate tax. Philadelphia's rate has been 1.3998% of assessed value since 2016.

🧱 No Small-Estate Shortcut for the House

Families sometimes hope to avoid opening an estate by using Pennsylvania's small-estate procedure. Section 3102 does allow the Orphans' Court to distribute an estate of $50,000 or less without full administration, but the $50,000 is measured exclusive of real estate, and the procedure covers personal property. The house itself can't pass that way. In practice, an inherited Pennsylvania house almost always needs either letters from the Register of Wills or a deed signed by every heir, plus proof of who the heirs are.

⚖️ Partition: Orphans' Court Inside the Estate, Common Pleas After It

Pennsylvania has two partition routes, and which one applies depends on timing:

  1. 📂 While the estate is open. Under 20 Pa.C.S. § 3534, the personal representative or any distributee can ask the Orphans' Court to divide and allot the estate's property in kind. When a house can't be divided, the court can order it sold, either in a sale limited to the distributees (a way for one sibling to outbid the others) or in a sale open to the public.
  2. 🏛️ After the house is distributed. Once the heirs hold title as co-owners, a partition action goes to the Court of Common Pleas under the Pennsylvania Rules of Civil Procedure, Rules 1551 through 1574. These cases commonly take a year or more and involve a master or a court-ordered sale if the co-owners can't agree.

Unlike Georgia, Texas, California and a growing list of states, Pennsylvania has not adopted the Uniform Partition of Heirs Property Act. House Bill 1498 passed the House 181-22 in June 2025 and went to the Senate Judiciary Committee; a Senate version, SB 890, was amended in committee and then tabled in October 2026. Until a bill becomes law, Pennsylvania heirs don't have the statutory appraisal-and-buyout window those states provide. Our partition lawsuit guide explains how partition works generally.

🧭 Choosing a Path in a Pennsylvania Estate

Sell through the representative and settle the shares from the proceeds

Most common, usually best

The representative lists or sells the house, pays the inheritance tax, debts and expenses, and distributes cash. Disagreements about who deserves what become disagreements about money, which are easier to resolve than disagreements about a house.

Let the heir who wants it buy it from the estate

Good if they have financing

The representative can sell to an heir like any other buyer, ideally at an independently appraised price so the other heirs can't later claim self-dealing. The buyer's share of the estate can be credited against the price.

Ask the Orphans' Court to step in

When the representative is the problem

Petitions to compel action, to remove the representative under § 3182, or for distribution in kind under § 3534 put a judge in charge of the decision. They cost time and fees, but they end standoffs that negotiation can't.

An heir sells their interest after distribution

Only after the estate is done

Once heirs hold title, each can usually sell their undivided share. Buyers pay a steep discount because they inherit the family conflict and a possible partition case. Before distribution, an heir generally has an interest in the estate rather than a recordable share of the house, which is harder to sell.

💵 Running the Numbers on a Philadelphia Rowhouse

Take a Philadelphia rowhouse with an after-repair value of $200,000, assessed at $170,000, that needs about $40,000 of work: new roof membrane, a bathroom and a kitchen. Three children inherit it equally.

The inheritance tax. Based on a $170,000 date-of-death value for this example (the return uses fair market value, often supported by an appraisal), 4.5% is $7,650. Paid within three months, the 5% discount saves about $380. Paid after nine months, interest starts.

Monthly carrying cost (illustrative; use the actual bills):

  • 🧾 Real estate tax: 1.3998% × $170,000 = about $2,380 a year, or $198 a month.
  • 🛡️ Vacant-property insurance: commonly $100 to $200 a month.
  • 💧 Water, gas and electric kept on through the winter: $100 to $200 a month.
  • 🧹 Snow, trash, minor repairs and code compliance: $50 to $150 a month.

That's roughly $450 to $750 a month, or $5,400 to $9,000 a year, before any mortgage, plus attorney fees that climb quickly once removal or partition petitions are filed.

A cash offer. Cash buyers commonly pay 60% to 80% of after-repair value. With $40,000 of work, a fair offer here might be about $130,000 to $145,000, say $138,000 (69%). A retail listing, if the house can be shown and the heirs don't interfere, might bring $160,000 to $175,000 as-is, less a commission and the seller's share of the 4.578% transfer tax. That still nets more than the cash offer in most cases, which is why we tell cooperating families to list.

The cash offer wins when the house can't be shown because an heir won't leave, when it needs too much work for financed buyers, when the inheritance tax deadline is close and the estate has no other cash, or when a year of removal and partition litigation would eat the difference.

🙋 When to Ignore Cash Buyers (Including Us)

If the representative is acting in good faith, the heirs are cooperating, and the house is in decent shape, list it with a local agent. If a family member wants the house and qualifies for a mortgage at an appraised price, let them buy it from the estate. If a Philadelphia heir qualifies for the Tangled Title Fund and wants to live in the home, start there. A cash buyer is the right tool for the harder cases, not the default.

📋 What To Do This Week

  1. Confirm who has letters: call or search the Register of Wills in the county where the deceased lived.
  2. Read the will for a specific gift of the house or any limit on the executor's power to sell.
  3. Write down the three-month discount date and the nine-month inheritance tax deadline, counted from the date of death.
  4. List every estate bill being missed: property tax, insurance, water, utilities. Those records matter if a removal petition becomes necessary.
  5. If a relative lives in the house, put an offer in writing: buyout at appraisal, a move-out date with a credit, or a timeline tied to the sale.
  6. Meet with a Pennsylvania estate attorney before anyone files in Orphans' Court or signs an agreement of sale.

✅ Bottom Line

Pennsylvania gives the personal representative the power to sell most inherited houses without the heirs' signatures, and it gives the Department of Revenue a claim on the inheritance that comes due nine months after death. Together those rules mean the question for most families isn't whether the house will be sold but who will run the sale, how fast, and at what price. When the representative won't act or acts badly, the Orphans' Court is where heirs push back.

If the heirs cooperate and the house is sound, list it. If someone won't leave, the house needs heavy work, or the tax deadline is close and cash is short, ask us. We'll tell you plainly which situation you're in.

❓ Frequently Asked Questions

Do all heirs have to sign to sell an inherited house in Pennsylvania?

Usually not, if an estate is open. Under 20 Pa.C.S. § 3351, the executor or administrator can sell real estate that wasn't specifically devised without a court order and without the heirs joining, unless the will says otherwise. If the will gave the house to a specific person, that person must join or the representative needs an Orphans' Court order. If no estate is open, every heir must sign the deed.

How much is Pennsylvania inheritance tax on a house left to children?

4.5% of the value passing to children, grandchildren and other lineal heirs. Siblings pay 12%, most other heirs pay 15%, and a surviving spouse pays 0%. The tax is delinquent nine months after death, and paying within three months earns a 5% discount on the tax.

Can a sibling living in the inherited house stop the sale in Pennsylvania?

Generally no. Under 20 Pa.C.S. § 3311, the personal representative doesn't automatically take possession of real estate an heir occupied at death with the decedent's consent, but the section states that this doesn't affect the representative's power to sell it. The Orphans' Court can also direct the representative to take possession.

How do you remove an executor in Pennsylvania who won't sell?

An heir can petition the Orphans' Court under 20 Pa.C.S. § 3182. Grounds include wasting or mismanaging the estate, failing to perform a duty imposed by law, incapacity, leaving the state without required security, and circumstances where keeping the representative in office would likely jeopardize the estate. Evidence such as missed tax deadlines or an unpaid, uninsured property matters.

Can a Pennsylvania small estate petition transfer a house?

No. The small-estate procedure in 20 Pa.C.S. § 3102 applies to estates of $50,000 or less measured exclusive of real estate, and it distributes personal property. An inherited house generally needs letters from the Register of Wills or a deed from every heir.

Has Pennsylvania adopted the Uniform Partition of Heirs Property Act?

Not as of November 2026. House Bill 1498 passed the House in June 2025 and was referred to the Senate Judiciary Committee, and Senate Bill 890 was tabled in October 2026. Partition still follows the Rules of Civil Procedure (Rules 1551 to 1574) in Common Pleas, or 20 Pa.C.S. § 3534 in Orphans' Court while an estate is open.

What is the transfer tax when an estate sells a house in Philadelphia?

Since July 1, 2025, the combined Philadelphia and Pennsylvania realty transfer tax is 4.578% of the price: 3.578% for the City and 1% for the state. Transfers from an estate to the heirs are generally excluded, but a sale to an outside buyer is taxed. How the tax is split is set in the agreement of sale.

How long do creditors have to make claims against a Pennsylvania estate?

The representative advertises the grant of letters once a week for three weeks under 20 Pa.C.S. § 3162. Under § 3532, a representative who distributes is generally protected against claims not known within one year after the first complete advertisement, and a creditor wanting to reach real estate conveyed in distribution must file notice of the claim within one year after death.

What is the Philadelphia Tangled Title Fund?

It is a City-funded program run by Philadelphia VIP that can pay up to $6,500 in costs to clear title to a home, with up to $3,500 more in some cases for transfer tax, for heirs whose household income is at or below 80% of area median income and who live in or will live in the home.

When does a cash offer make sense for a Pennsylvania estate house?

When the house needs repairs financed buyers won't accept, when an heir is living there and showings aren't possible, when the inheritance tax deadline is close and the estate has no other cash, or when removal or partition litigation would cost more than the difference. If the heirs cooperate and the house is sound, a listing usually nets more.
