Selling a Co-Owned House in Texas: Count the Signatures Before You Count the Money

Texas is a community property state with one of the strongest homestead laws in the country. Together they mean a co-owner's husband or wife can have a say in the sale even when their name is nowhere on the deed. Here is who actually has to sign, what happens when one owner refuses, and which exit nets the most. Or ask us a question, free.

By Dustin Ray, Founder of Pages of Purpose LLC. Not legal advice — laws differ by state, Texas courts and title companies apply these rules case by case, and your facts matter. Before you sign, file, or send a demand letter, talk to an attorney licensed in Texas, where the property is.

You own a house in Texas with someone else. Maybe it was a brother and sister buying a starter home together in 2012, an unmarried couple who split up, two friends who bought a rental in Arlington, or three cousins who ended up with Grandma's place in Pasadena. Now you want to sell and they don't, or they want to sell and can't agree on a price, or one of them simply stopped answering.

Most of the advice online says the same two things: any co-owner can force a sale with a partition suit, and you can always sell your own share. Both are true in Texas. But before either one matters, there is a question that trips up more Texas co-owners than anything else: whose signature does a title company actually need to close? In Texas, the answer often includes people who are not on the deed at all.

💍 The Signatures Texas Requires That Aren't on Your Deed

Two Texas rules reach past the names on the deed. Neither exists in most common-law states, and together they decide how hard your sale will be.

Community property can give a spouse a hidden half

Texas presumes that property a married person acquires during the marriage is community property. That presumption is in Section 3.003 of the Texas Family Code, and a spouse who claims otherwise has to prove it by clear and convincing evidence. So if your co-owner bought their share while married, their spouse may own half of that share even if the deed shows only your co-owner's name. Inheritances and gifts are the main exception: a share your co-owner inherited from a parent is normally their separate property.

Title companies know this. When a married co-owner sells, the title commitment will usually ask for the spouse to sign the deed, or for proof that the share is separate property. Expect that request; it's routine. It does mean the person you need to persuade may be a sister-in-law you've met twice.

Homestead joinder applies even to separate property

The second rule is stronger. Section 5.001 of the Texas Family Code says that whether the homestead is the separate property of either spouse or community property, neither spouse may sell, convey, or encumber the homestead without the joinder of the other spouse. If your co-owner lives in the house with a husband or wife, that spouse has to join any deed of your co-owner's interest, even when the share came to your co-owner by inheritance and the spouse has no ownership in it at all.

Here's how that plays out. Two siblings inherit their father's house in Mesquite. The brother moves in with his wife. Five years later the sister wants to sell. The brother is open to it, but his wife isn't, because she doesn't want to move. Under Section 5.001, the brother cannot sign a voluntary deed to his share without her. The sister is not stuck, because her share isn't anyone's homestead but her own, and partition remains available. But the friendly, signed-by-everyone sale she pictured now needs a fourth person's agreement.

Before you make any offer or demand: pull the deed from the county clerk's records, then write down every owner's marital status and whether they live in the house. If any married owner lives there, their spouse is effectively a signer. Plan the conversation around that person, not just the names on title.

🧾 Texas Doesn't Give Survivorship Unless Someone Wrote It Down

Many co-owners assume that if the other owner dies, the survivor gets the whole house. In Texas that is usually wrong. Section 101.002 of the Texas Estates Code says that when two or more people hold property jointly and one dies before severance, the deceased owner's interest passes as if it were held in common, with no automatic survivorship. Survivorship exists only if the owners signed a written agreement for it, which Section 111.001 allows. Texas has no tenancy by the entirety for married couples either; spouses who want survivorship need a written community property survivorship agreement.

So a deed that says only "John Smith and Mary Jones" makes them tenants in common. If Mary dies, her share goes to her will beneficiaries or, without a will, to her heirs under Texas intestacy rules. That's how a two-owner house turns into a five-owner house: Mary's three children now each hold a sixth. Every new owner is another signature, and any of them can file for partition.

Two practical points follow. First, if you co-own with someone in poor health and you both want the other to end up with the house, talk to a lawyer about a written survivorship agreement or a transfer on death deed under Chapter 114 of the Estates Code. Second, if a co-owner has already died, the heirs may need to be identified before anyone can sell. 🏠 When the Co-Owner Lives There: Possession, Rent and Tax Credits

The hardest co-ownership standoffs usually involve one owner living in the house and the other paying for part of it, or paying nothing and getting nothing. Texas law handles this in a way that surprises out-of-possession owners.

Each co-owner has the right to possess the whole property. A co-owner who lives there alone generally doesn't owe the others rent unless there is an agreement or an ouster, meaning they actively excluded the other owners: changed the locks and refused entry, for example. If you want to preserve a rent claim, ask for access or a key in writing and keep the response. A clear refusal is the evidence an attorney will want.

Necessary expenses are shared by ownership percentage. Texas courts let a co-owner who paid property taxes, insurance, mortgage principal and needed repairs seek reimbursement from the others in proportion to their shares, usually as a credit when the house is sold or partitioned. Optional upgrades a co-owner chose on their own, such as a new kitchen nobody agreed to, generally aren't reimbursable beyond any value they added. Keep every receipt and bank record; reimbursement disputes are often settled by whoever has the paperwork.

The occupant's homestead claim doesn't stop a partition. Texas courts have long treated one co-owner's homestead rights as subordinate to the other co-owners' right to partition. The major exception is a surviving spouse. Under the Texas Constitution and Section 102.005 of the Estates Code, a surviving spouse's homestead can't be partitioned among the heirs while the spouse uses it as a home. If your co-owner is a stepparent living in the house your parent left, that rule may control everything; read our Texas heirs guide before you plan anything.

The property tax exemption may only cover part of the house. When co-owners don't all live in the home, the occupant's residence homestead exemption is usually limited to their percentage of ownership. Since 2019, an heir who inherited the house and lives in it can claim the full exemption by filing the appraisal district's heir property affidavit. If your co-owner hasn't filed that, the tax bill you're splitting may be higher than it needs to be.

⚖️ Partition: The Exit Texas Law Always Leaves Open

When co-owners can't agree, Section 23.001 of the Texas Property Code lets any joint owner with a present right of possession compel a partition. The case is filed in district court in the county where the property sits, and it follows Rules 756 through 771 of the Texas Rules of Civil Procedure. It works in two stages, and each stage ends in a judgment that can be appealed:

  1. Shares and partitionability. The court decides who owns what percentage, resolves reimbursement claims, and decides whether the property can be fairly divided in kind.
  2. Division or sale. If it can be divided, the court appoints commissioners to split it. If it can't, the court orders a sale. Under Rule 770 a sheriff or constable can conduct that sale, or the court can appoint a receiver who may sell privately or at auction.

Texas prefers physical division, and the owner asking for a sale has to justify it. For a single-family house on a city lot that's rarely a problem, because you can't split a three-bedroom house in half without destroying its value. For acreage outside Tyler or San Antonio, a judge may well divide the land instead, which surprises co-owners who expected a check.

If the house was inherited and relatives hold a large share, Chapter 23A of the Property Code, Texas's version of the Uniform Partition of Heirs' Property Act, may apply. It gives the co-owners who didn't ask for a sale the right to buy out the one who did, at a court-determined value, and it replaces the courthouse-steps sale with a listing by a real estate broker. Our partition lawsuit explainer walks through how partition works in general.

Time and cost. An uncontested Texas partition with cooperative parties can wrap up in four to eight months. A contested one, with reimbursement fights and an appeal, can run eighteen months or longer. Attorney fees for a contested case commonly reach $10,000 to $30,000 per side, and receiver, appraiser and survey costs come out of the sale proceeds, shared by ownership percentage. That cost is the main reason most partition suits settle before a sale is ever ordered: the filing itself is often what brings the other owner to the table.

🔀 Each Exit, Rated Honestly

List it with an agent

Best price when everyone signs

If the house is in decent condition and every owner and required spouse will sign, list it. In most Texas metros a move-in-ready house still draws financed buyers, and you'll net more than any cash offer, including ours. The risk is time: an owner can refuse to sign the closing documents at the last minute, and a listing agreement doesn't force them to.

Buy out the other owner

Cleanest when someone wants to stay

When one owner wants the house, a buyout at an agreed appraisal avoids commissions entirely. Credit the buyer for the taxes and mortgage they've paid, credit the seller for any ouster period, and put it in a written settlement. The buyer usually has to refinance to take the other owner off any existing loan.

Sell the whole house for cash

Best when the house or the timeline is the problem

A cash sale makes sense when the house needs foundation, roof or plumbing work that would scare off lenders, when someone needs their money within weeks, or when carrying costs are eating the equity. You still need the same signatures as a listing, but there are no showings, repairs, appraisals or loan approvals to fall through.

Sell just your share

A last resort that still beats waiting forever

You can deed your undivided interest without the other owners' consent. A buyer of a share is buying a dispute, so expect an offer well below your fraction of the house's value. It's mainly worth it when you can't afford a partition suit yourself. Our guide to selling a share of an inherited Texas house covers how those shares are priced.

File for partition

The lever, not usually the finish line

Filing often ends the stalemate within a few months because the other owner sees the costs coming. If it goes all the way, the court's sale may bring less than a cooperative listing, and both sides pay for the privilege. File when talking has failed, not as a first move.

💵 The Math on a Real House in Dallas ISD

Take a 1950s ranch house in East Dallas co-owned 50/50 by two siblings. Fixed up, it would sell for about $300,000. As it stands, it needs roughly $35,000 of foundation and roof work.

Carrying costs while you argue. For 2025, the combined property tax rate for a home in the City of Dallas and Dallas ISD was about $2.23 per $100 of value, adding together the city, the school district, Dallas County, Parkland Hospital and Dallas College. On a $300,000 appraisal with no homestead exemption, that's about $6,690 a year, or $557 a month. Add vacant-home insurance at $150 to $250 a month, utilities at about $150, and lawn care and minor upkeep at about $150. A standoff costs these two owners roughly $1,000 to $1,100 a month, before any mortgage.

Option A, list as-is. A financed buyer might pay $255,000 for it in its current condition. Subtract about 8% for commissions, title and closing costs ($20,400), and four months of carrying costs ($4,300). Net: about $230,300, or $115,150 each, if both siblings and any required spouses cooperate and the buyer's inspection doesn't kill the deal.

Option B, cash sale. Cash buyers typically pay 60–80% of after-repair value, depending on condition and location. For this house a realistic cash offer might be $205,000, closing in three weeks with the buyer paying most closing costs. Net: roughly $203,000, or $101,500 each.

Option C, partition. Suppose the sister files and the brother fights. After a year, a receiver sells for $250,000. Subtract a 6% sale cost, a receiver's fee, and two sets of attorney fees at $15,000 each, plus twelve months of carrying costs. Each sibling may walk away with $85,000 to $95,000, and a damaged relationship.

On these numbers listing wins by about $27,000, which is $13,500 per owner, if it actually happens. The cash sale wins when the signatures are uncertain, the repairs are bigger than they look, or one owner simply can't wait. Partition is what both sides should be trying to avoid.

🙅 When You Shouldn't Sell to a Cash Buyer

If every owner and every required spouse will sign, the house is in reasonable shape, and nobody needs money this month, list it. You'll almost certainly net more. If one owner wants to keep the house and can qualify for a loan, a buyout is better for everyone than selling to us. And if a surviving spouse has a homestead right to live there, no buyer can make the property sellable until that right ends, so you'd be wasting your time with any investor who claims otherwise.

📋 What To Do This Week

  1. Pull the current deed from the county clerk and the appraisal district record. Note exactly how each owner holds title and whether any survivorship language exists.
  2. List every owner's marital status and whether they live in the house. Flag anyone whose spouse would have to join a deed.
  3. Gather proof of what you've paid: tax receipts, insurance, mortgage statements, repair invoices.
  4. If you're locked out, ask for a key or access in writing and save the reply.
  5. Get a realistic as-is value and an after-repair value so the conversation starts from real numbers.
  6. Send the other owners a short written proposal with two or three options: list, buyout, or cash sale. Give a deadline.
  7. If there's no answer in 30 days, book a consultation with a Texas real estate attorney about partition.

✅ Bottom Line

Texas gives every co-owner a way out, but it puts more people at the table than most co-owners expect. Count the signatures first: the names on the deed, the spouses who own community shares, and the spouses whose homestead joinder is required. Then pick the exit that matches who will actually sign. When everyone cooperates, listing usually pays best. When they won't, a cash sale or a well-timed partition filing gets you unstuck. If you want a second opinion on which situation you're in, ask us. We'll tell you plainly, even when the answer is to list it with someone else.

❓ Frequently Asked Questions

Can one co-owner sell a jointly owned house in Texas without the other?

Not the whole house. A Texas co-owner can sell only their own undivided interest without the others' consent. To sell the entire property, every owner must sign the deed, or a court must order a sale in a partition suit under Chapter 23 of the Texas Property Code.

Does my co-owner's spouse have to sign if they aren't on the deed?

Often, yes. If the house is the married co-owner's homestead, Texas Family Code Section 5.001 requires the other spouse to join any sale, even if the share is the co-owner's separate property. If the share was bought during the marriage, it is presumed community property and the title company will usually want the spouse's signature anyway.

If my co-owner dies, do I automatically get their share of a Texas house?

Usually not. Under Texas Estates Code Section 101.002, co-owners have no right of survivorship unless they signed a written survivorship agreement. Without one, the deceased owner's share passes to their will beneficiaries or legal heirs, who become your new co-owners.

Can my co-owner's homestead claim stop a partition sale in Texas?

Generally no. Texas courts treat one co-owner's homestead rights as subordinate to the other co-owners' right to partition. The big exception is a surviving spouse, whose homestead cannot be partitioned among the heirs while the spouse uses it as a home.

My co-owner lives in the house. Do they owe me rent in Texas?

Usually not, unless you had an agreement or they ousted you by actively excluding you from the property. Every co-owner has the right to occupy the whole house. If you want to preserve a claim, ask for access or a key in writing and keep their response.

Who gets credit for property taxes and repairs when a co-owned Texas house sells?

A co-owner who paid necessary expenses such as property taxes, insurance, mortgage principal and essential repairs can generally seek reimbursement from the others in proportion to their shares, usually as a credit at sale or in a partition case. Voluntary upgrades are treated differently and generally are not reimbursed beyond the value they added.

How long does a partition lawsuit take in Texas, and who pays for it?

An uncontested Texas partition can finish in four to eight months; a contested one with an appeal can take eighteen months or more. Court costs, receiver and appraiser fees are shared by ownership percentage, and each side usually pays its own attorney, often $10,000 to $30,000 in a contested case.

Can I sell just my share of a co-owned house in Texas?

Yes. You can deed your undivided interest to a buyer without the other owners' consent, though your spouse may need to join if it is community property or your homestead. Expect a price well below your fraction of the house's value, because the buyer takes on the dispute and may have to file for partition.

What if my co-owner is my ex-spouse and the divorce decree never divided the house?

Texas ex-spouses who still share title generally hold the house as tenants in common. Chapter 9 of the Texas Family Code lets either former spouse ask the divorce court to divide property the decree missed, and a partition suit is also possible. A Texas family law attorney can tell you which route fits.

Is it better to list a co-owned Texas house or sell it for cash?

If every owner and required spouse will sign and the house is in decent shape, listing usually nets more, even after commissions. A cash sale makes more sense when the house needs major repairs, someone needs money quickly, the owners cannot coordinate a listing, or carrying costs are eating the equity.
