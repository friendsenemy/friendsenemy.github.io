Selling a House From a Pennsylvania Jail or Prison: The Power of Attorney Is Where Deals Fail

Pennsylvania doesn't stop an incarcerated owner from selling a house, but it has some of the most demanding power-of-attorney rules in the country: a notary, two separate adult witnesses, a signed notice page, and an acknowledgment the agent must sign before doing anything. Miss one piece and the title company sends it back, and every retry means another signing inside an SCI or county jail. Here's how to get it right the first time, what tenancy by the entireties means for married owners, and what Pennsylvania's transfer tax and court-cost judgments take out of a sale.

By Dustin Ray, Founder of Pages of Purpose LLC. Not legal advice — Pennsylvania property, fiduciary and criminal law change, laws differ by state, and every case is different. A Pennsylvania-licensed attorney, licensed where the property is located, should review your situation before anyone signs or files.

When a family member in Pennsylvania is sentenced to a state correctional institution, or held in a county jail awaiting trial, the house they own doesn't wait. The mortgage, the school taxes and the water bill keep coming, and sooner or later someone asks whether the house can be sold. It can. Pennsylvania doesn't take away an incarcerated person's right to own or sell real estate, and a deed signed from inside, or by an agent under a valid power of attorney, is as good as any other.

Where Pennsylvania sales go wrong is almost always the paperwork, and specifically the power of attorney. Pennsylvania rewrote its POA statute in 2014 and made it one of the strictest in the country. A form downloaded from the internet, or a POA signed the way it would be in Ohio or New Jersey, often gets rejected at closing. Inside a prison, a rejected POA isn't a quick fix. It's another month. So that's where this guide starts.

🖊️ Four People, One Signature: Pennsylvania's Power of Attorney Formalities

Under 20 Pa.C.S. section 5601, a Pennsylvania power of attorney has to be:

  • 📅 Dated and signed by the principal, meaning the owner who is inside.
  • 🧾 Acknowledged before a notary (or another officer authorized to take acknowledgments), and that notary can't be the agent.
  • 👥 Witnessed by two adults, each at least 18. Neither witness can be the agent, and neither can be the notary.

Count the people. The owner signs, a notary acknowledges, and two more adults watch and sign as witnesses. That's four people at one signing, and in a correctional facility, that's the hard part. A staff notary who arrives alone can't make the document valid. A family member who'll be the agent can't serve as a witness.

Pennsylvania adds two more pieces most states don't have:

  • ⚠️ The notice page. Section 5601(c) requires a notice in capital letters at the beginning of the POA, signed by the principal, explaining the powers being granted. Without a signed notice, an agent whose actions are challenged has the burden of proving they acted properly, and title companies treat a missing notice as a red flag.
  • ✍️ The agent's acknowledgment. Under section 5601(d), the agent has no authority to act until they sign an acknowledgment, attached to the POA, promising to act in good faith, in the principal's best interest and within the authority granted. The agent signs this outside, but it has to be done before they sign anything for the sale.

📋 The Checklist a Pennsylvania Title Company Will Hold You To

Before the POA goes into the facility, have the closing title company or a Pennsylvania attorney review the draft. Expect them to check:

  1. Notice page at the front, in capital letters, with a signature line for the owner.
  2. Specific real estate authority: the power to sell, convey and sign deeds and settlement documents, ideally naming the property address and parcel number.
  3. Signature block with room for the owner, two witnesses who aren't the agent or notary, and a notarial acknowledgment.
  4. Agent's acknowledgment attached, signed by the agent before settlement.
  5. Recording. An acknowledged POA can be recorded with the recorder of deeds in the county where the real estate is, and title companies usually record it along with the deed.

Pennsylvania also protects people who accept a POA in good faith, and section 5608.1 generally requires a person presented with a POA to accept it or request specific documentation within seven business days. That helps once the POA is right. It doesn't help if it's missing a witness.

🏢 Making the Signing Happen in an SCI or County Jail

People serving state sentences are held by the Pennsylvania Department of Corrections in state correctional institutions such as SCI Phoenix, SCI Chester and SCI Muncy. People awaiting trial or serving shorter sentences are usually in county facilities, such as the Philadelphia Department of Prisons or the Allegheny County Jail, and each county runs its own rules.

The Department of Corrections directs families to the superintendent's office at the specific prison for questions like this, and that's the right first call. Ask:

  • 🖋️ How does an incarcerated person request a notary for personal legal documents?
  • 👥 Can two adult witnesses, other than the notary and the agent, be present? Can staff serve as witnesses, or must they be visitors on the approved list?
  • 🪪 What identification will the notary accept from the incarcerated person?
  • 📬 How should documents be sent in, and how are they returned?

If the facility can't supply two witnesses, the usual workaround is a scheduled legal or professional visit where an outside mobile notary and two witnesses come together. Get approval before anyone drives out; showing up with the wrong number of people wastes a visit.

Send the document complete except for signatures and dates, with every signature line flagged and a prepaid return envelope. Confirm the person's current location the week you mail it. Transfers after sentencing are one of the most common reasons papers disappear.

💑 Tenancy by the Entireties: Both Spouses Sign, and Selling Can Cost Protection

Pennsylvania is one of the states with strong tenancy by the entireties. When a deed names a married couple, Pennsylvania generally treats them as owning the house as one unit. Two consequences matter here.

First, both spouses must sign. Neither spouse can sell, mortgage or transfer entireties property without the other. If the house is in both names and one spouse is in prison, the at-home spouse can't sell alone. The incarcerated spouse signs the deed, or signs a POA naming someone, often the at-home spouse, to sign for them.

Second, entireties property is shielded from one spouse's individual creditors. A creditor of only one spouse generally can't put a lien on the house or force a sale. That can include court costs, fines or a civil judgment against the incarcerated spouse alone. Debts both spouses owe together aren't protected.

That second point is an honest reason to slow down. Once the house is sold and the proceeds are split, the incarcerated spouse's share is money in his or her hands, and that money may be reachable by creditors the house was protected from. If there are large restitution orders or judgments against the spouse inside, talk to a Pennsylvania lawyer before selling. Sometimes keeping the house is the protective move.

If the marriage ends, Pennsylvania converts entireties property to a tenancy in common on divorce, and each former spouse owns a separate share. Our guide to selling a house during a divorce covers how that usually plays out. If the person inside co-owns the house with siblings rather than a spouse, see can one co-owner sell without the others.

🧾 Court Costs and Restitution: Judgments That Don't Expire

Pennsylvania treats sentencing money differently from ordinary debts. Under 42 Pa.C.S. section 9728, a sentence to pay restitution, fees, costs, fines or penalties is a judgment against the person and their property. Clerks send certified judgments over $1,000 to the prothonotary to be docketed, and smaller ones may be sent too. Most importantly for a house sale, section 9728(d) says a lien obtained this way keeps its priority indefinitely, with no writ of revival needed. Ordinary Pennsylvania judgment liens have to be revived periodically; these don't.

So a costs-and-fines judgment from years ago can still be sitting on the title. If the house is in the incarcerated person's name alone, expect the title search to find it and the settlement to pay it. The same statute also requires the Department of Corrections to deduct a percentage of money deposited to an inmate's account toward restitution and costs, often called Act 84 deductions, which is why some families prefer sale proceeds to go somewhere other than the inmate account.

⚠️ The most important warning on this page: don't let anyone talk the person inside into signing a deed to a relative or an investor to "get around" a judgment or speed things up. A transfer made to avoid known creditors can be undone, it hands over all control of the price and the money, and with an entireties property it can strip away the very protection that was keeping the house safe. Use a Pennsylvania POA, and get a lawyer's advice if judgments are involved.

💸 Pennsylvania's Transfer Tax Takes a Real Bite

Pennsylvania charges a 1% state realty transfer tax on most sales, and local governments add their own. In much of the state the total is 2%, customarily split equally between buyer and seller, though the contract decides. In Philadelphia, the combined rate has been 4.578% since July 1, 2025: 3.578% for the city plus 1% for the state. Pittsburgh is also well above the statewide norm.

That matters when comparing offers. On a $200,000 Philadelphia sale, the total transfer tax is about $9,160, and a seller paying the customary half pays about $4,580. Ask any cash buyer, including us, who pays the transfer tax and get it in the agreement of sale.

🏚️ Holding the House: What It Costs to Wait

Pennsylvania property taxes vary widely by school district, commonly around 1% to 2.5% of market value a year outside Philadelphia, where the rate is about 1.4% of assessed value. A rough monthly budget for an empty house:

  • 🏛️ Property and school taxes: $150 to $450 a month on a $150,000 to $250,000 house, depending on the district.
  • 🛡️ Vacant-dwelling insurance: about $100 to $250 a month once the regular policy's vacancy limits apply.
  • 🔥 Heat and utilities: $150 to $300 through a Pennsylvania winter to keep pipes from freezing, or the cost of professional winterizing.
  • 🧹 Upkeep: $100 to $250 for snow, grass and securing doors and windows. Cities including Philadelphia have their own rules for vacant buildings, and code violations add up.

That's roughly $500 to $1,200 a month before any mortgage. A Pennsylvania mortgage foreclosure is a court case, and Pennsylvania requires lenders to send a special pre-foreclosure notice about homeowner assistance before filing, so foreclosure moves more slowly here than in many states. That gives families time to get the POA right, but it doesn't make an unpaid mortgage free. Our guide on selling to stop a foreclosure explains the options.

🔀 The Paths Forward, Weighed Plainly

🏡 Keep the house until release

Best for entireties property and a short sentence

If the couple owns it as tenants by the entireties, there are judgments against the spouse inside, and the at-home spouse can carry the costs, holding the house may protect the family better than any sale.

🏷️ List it with a properly executed POA

Best for a sound house

Once the POA is right, an agent can list the house, handle showings, and sign every amendment and settlement document outside. A financed buyer will usually pay more than any cash buyer, including us, on a house in good condition.

🏚️ Sell as-is for cash

Best when condition or time is the problem

A cash sale fits when the house needs major work, when it's been empty through a winter, when there are judgments and liens that make a retail sale messy, or when the family can't keep paying to hold it.

🤝 Sell only the inmate's share

For co-owned and inherited houses

If the person inside owns a share with siblings, they can sell that share or be bought out. Entireties property can't be split this way while the marriage lasts.

💵 A Worked Example: A Philadelphia Rowhouse

A rowhouse in the Kensington section of Philadelphia is owned by a married couple as tenants by the entireties. The husband is serving a six-year sentence at an SCI. The wife has moved in with family and can't keep paying for the empty house. There's a $40,000 mortgage, and no judgments against both of them.

  • ✍️ Who signs: both spouses. He signs a POA inside, with a notary and two witnesses, naming her as agent; she signs the agent's acknowledgment, then signs the deed for herself and for him.
  • 📈 Values: about $220,000 fully renovated; needs roughly $50,000 of work (roof, electrical, kitchen, bath, water damage from a winter without heat).
  • 💵 Cash range: cash buyers generally pay about 60% to 80% of after-repair value, so $132,000 to $176,000. With $50,000 of work, an honest offer lands around $135,000 to $150,000.
  • 🧾 Seller's costs: the seller's customary half of the 4.578% Philadelphia transfer tax is about 2.3%, roughly $3,100 to $3,400 on that price, plus the $40,000 payoff and any unpaid water or tax bills. Net to the couple: roughly $88,000 to $103,000.
  • 🏷️ Listing as-is: perhaps $160,000 to $175,000 to an investor-minded buyer after 60 to 120 days, minus commission, the same transfer tax share and several more months of holding costs, for a net in the low $100,000s if it sells.

Listing may net $5,000 to $15,000 more here if the family can carry the house for a few more months and handle showings. If they can't, or if the house keeps deteriorating, a cash sale is a fair option. Either way, the POA has to be done right first.

📋 What To Do This Week

  1. Pull the deed from the county recorder of deeds and confirm whose names are on it and whether a married couple owns it together.
  2. Search the docket for judgments, including costs and fines, against the person inside. Your lawyer or title company can do this.
  3. Call the facility's superintendent's office and ask exactly how a notary and two witnesses can be arranged.
  4. Have a Pennsylvania attorney or the title company prepare the POA with the capitalized notice, specific real estate powers and the agent's acknowledgment, then send it in with clear instructions.
  5. Insure and winterize the house if it's empty.
  6. Get two numbers: an agent's as-is pricing estimate and a written cash offer that states who pays the transfer tax.

✅ Bottom Line

A Pennsylvania house can be sold while its owner is in jail or prison. The sale lives or dies on the power of attorney: a notary, two separate adult witnesses, a signed notice page and the agent's signed acknowledgment, all in place before the agent acts. Get that right and the rest of the sale happens outside the facility. For married owners, tenancy by the entireties means both spouses sign, and it may also mean the house is better protected from one spouse's judgments than the sale proceeds would be.

If the house is sound and the family can wait, list it, or keep it if the entireties protection matters. If it needs work, it's sitting empty, or the costs are piling up, ask us, starting with a look at your POA draft. You'll hear our honest read of the options before we ever talk numbers.

❓ Frequently Asked Questions

What does a Pennsylvania power of attorney need to be valid?

Under 20 Pa.C.S. 5601, it must be dated and signed by the principal, acknowledged before a notary who is not the agent, and witnessed by two adults who are neither the agent nor the notary. It must begin with a capitalized notice signed by the principal, and the agent must sign an acknowledgment before acting.

Can the notary also be one of the witnesses on a Pennsylvania POA?

No. Pennsylvania's statute excludes the notary, or other officer taking the acknowledgment, from serving as a witness, and the agent cannot be a witness either. A signing inside a facility therefore needs the person signing, a notary and two other adults.

Can a Pennsylvania POA agent sign for the sale right away?

Only after signing the agent's acknowledgment required by 20 Pa.C.S. 5601(d). Until the agent signs it and attaches it to the power of attorney, the agent has no authority to act, so a deed signed before then is a problem at settlement.

Can I sell our Pennsylvania house without my incarcerated husband?

Not if you own it together as a married couple. Pennsylvania treats a house deeded to spouses as tenancy by the entireties, and neither spouse can sell or mortgage it without the other. Your husband can sign the deed inside or give you a power of attorney to sign for him.

Are court costs and fines a lien on a Pennsylvania house?

They can be. Under 42 Pa.C.S. 9728, restitution, costs, fines and penalties are judgments against the person and their property, and certified judgments over $1,000 are sent to the prothonotary to be docketed. A lien obtained this way keeps its priority indefinitely without needing to be revived.

Does tenancy by the entireties protect our house from my spouse's restitution?

Generally, a creditor of only one spouse cannot place a lien on or force a sale of entireties property. Debts both spouses owe are not protected. Selling the house can change that, because once proceeds are split, the incarcerated spouse's share may be reachable, so talk to a Pennsylvania lawyer first.

How much is the realty transfer tax when selling a house in Pennsylvania?

The state charges 1% and most localities add 1%, for 2% in much of the state, customarily split between buyer and seller. Philadelphia's combined rate is 4.578% as of July 1, 2025. The agreement of sale decides who actually pays it.

How do we arrange a notary in a Pennsylvania state prison?

Start with the superintendent's office at the specific state correctional institution. Ask how notary requests work, whether two adult witnesses other than the notary and the agent can be present, what ID is accepted, and how documents should be sent in and returned.

Should the house sale money go into the inmate's account?

Usually not. Pennsylvania law requires deductions from money deposited to an inmate's account toward restitution and court costs. Proceeds are typically paid to a bank account in the owner's name or held as the owner directs, after recorded liens are paid at settlement.

Is it better to list or sell for cash when the Pennsylvania owner is incarcerated?

With a properly executed POA and a house in good shape, listing usually nets more. Holding may be best for entireties property with judgments against the spouse inside. A cash sale makes more sense when the house needs major work, has sat empty, or carries liens that complicate a retail sale.
