Need to Sell a House While Someone's in a Texas Jail or Prison? Here's How It Actually Works

Texas has 100+ state prison units and 240+ county jails, and every one of them has a different process for getting a deed notarized. That logistics problem — not the law — is what stops most sales. The law is clear: an incarcerated Texan keeps every property right they had the day before arrest. Here's the Texas-specific playbook: POA, TDCJ notaries, homestead rules, and what a cash buyer can do that a realtor won't. Or just ask us a question, free.

By Dustin Ray, Founder of Pages of Purpose LLC. Not legal advice — Texas property, power of attorney, and notary rules change, TDCJ unit policies vary, and county jails each run their own show. Before signing anything, talk to an attorney licensed in Texas.

Your husband went to Huntsville. Your son is in the Harris County Jail waiting on trial. Your brother is doing eight years at Coffield and the house he owns in Mesquite is sitting empty with the taxes going unpaid. In every one of those cases the question is the same: can the house be sold, and how?

Texas law answers the first part cleanly — yes. Incarceration does not strip property rights. A person in TDCJ custody, in a county jail, or in federal custody at FCI Seagoville or FMC Fort Worth can sell, deed, mortgage, or lease real estate they own. The Texas Constitution and Property Code don't carve out an exception for inmates, and no title company in Texas will refuse a properly executed deed because the grantor was incarcerated when they signed it.

The second part — how — is where it gets hard. This page is the Texas-specific version of our national guide to selling while incarcerated. Everything here is about Texas facilities, Texas POA law, Texas homestead rules, and Texas title practice.

The Short Answer: Three Ways to Sell From a Texas Facility

  1. Durable power of attorney. The inmate signs a Texas statutory durable POA (Estates Code Chapter 752) naming a spouse, parent, sibling, or attorney as agent. The agent signs everything else. Requires one notarized signature inside the facility.
  2. Direct signing with a facility notary. The inmate signs the deed and closing documents themselves, notarized by a TDCJ-approved notary or a mobile notary admitted to the facility. Requires coordinating a visit for every signing.
  3. Cash buyer who handles the coordination. The buyer drafts the POA, arranges the notary, runs title, and closes. One signature from the inmate. 3-6 weeks.

Texas Durable Power of Attorney: What Works From Inside

Texas has a statutory form — the Statutory Durable Power of Attorney under Estates Code §752.051. Any Texas attorney can draft one, and the statutory form is accepted by every title company in the state. Key points for an incarcerated principal:

  • It must be notarized. Texas POAs for real estate must be acknowledged before a notary. Witnesses aren't required for a POA (they are for a will), but the notary is mandatory.
  • Make it durable. The statutory form is durable by default — it survives the principal's later incapacity. That matters because transfers between units, medical holds, and administrative segregation can all make an inmate temporarily unreachable.
  • Make it limited. You can restrict the POA to "the sale and conveyance of the real property located at [address]" and nothing else. Do this. A general POA given from inside to the wrong person is how people lose everything.
  • Record it. The POA must be recorded in the county clerk's real property records in the county where the house sits before the agent signs a deed. Title companies check.
  • Agent's certification. Texas title companies will typically require the agent to sign an Agent's Certification (Estates Code §751.203) at closing affirming the POA is still in effect and the principal is alive.

Getting a Signature Notarized Inside a Texas Facility

This is the actual bottleneck, and it works differently depending on who's holding the person.

TDCJ state prisons

Every TDCJ unit has notary services available through the unit's law library or the Access to Courts program. Inmates submit an I-60 request form to the unit's Access to Courts supervisor asking for notary services. Turnaround is typically 3-10 business days depending on the unit. The inmate must have the document in hand — mail it to them with a cover letter explaining what it is. TDCJ notaries will notarize a POA or deed; they will not provide legal advice about it.

Alternatively, a mobile notary from outside can request a legal visit. TDCJ allows attorneys and their staff (including notaries working for the attorney) to visit through the attorney visitation process. A notary who is not working with an attorney has a harder time getting in. Practically: if you're using a cash buyer or an attorney, they'll arrange it. If you're doing it yourself, use the I-60 route.

County jails

Harder. Large jails — Harris County, Dallas County, Bexar County, Tarrant County, Travis County — have procedures for attorney visits and most will admit a notary accompanying an attorney. Smaller county jails vary; some have a jail employee who is a notary, some don't. Pre-trial detention adds a complication: if the person might bond out next week, waiting may be faster than coordinating a jail notary. Call the jail's administrative office directly and ask "how does an inmate get a document notarized here?" — the answer is different at every facility.

Federal facilities in Texas

FCI Bastrop, FCI Beaumont, FCI Seagoville, FMC Carswell, FMC Fort Worth, FCI Three Rivers, and the others follow Bureau of Prisons policy. Unit team staff can often notarize. Attorney visits are the alternative. Lead time is 1-3 weeks.

The thing people get wrong: Texas does not allow remote online notarization (RON) for a signer who is physically in a correctional facility in most cases, because the RON platforms require identity verification the inmate can't complete. Plan on a physical notary.

Texas Homestead: The Rule That Trips Up Married Sellers

If the house is the family homestead and the inmate is married, Texas Family Code §5.001 requires both spouses to join in the conveyance — even if only one spouse is on the deed. The spouse on the outside cannot sell the homestead alone, and the incarcerated spouse cannot sell it alone either. Both must sign, which means two signatures to coordinate: one inside, one outside.

A POA solves this neatly. The incarcerated spouse gives the outside spouse a limited POA for the sale. The outside spouse then signs once as themselves and once as agent. One notary visit inside, done.

If the spouses are divorcing or hostile, it gets harder. The homestead rule means neither can force the sale alone short of a divorce decree or a court order. A Texas family law attorney is the right call there.

What If the Inmate Only Owns a Share?

Very common: the inmate inherited a third of their mother's house along with two siblings, or owns half with an ex. An undivided interest held as tenants in common can be sold by that owner alone, without the co-owners' consent. The buyer becomes a co-owner. Texas doesn't require the co-owners to be notified or given first refusal unless the deed says so.

For incarcerated heirs this is often the best path: sell the share, get paid, and exit the family dispute from inside without needing anyone's cooperation. The remaining owners can be dealt with by the buyer through negotiation or, if needed, a Texas partition suit under Property Code Chapter 23. We cover the mechanics in the national co-owner selling guide and what to do when heirs can't agree.

One Texas wrinkle: if the house is still in the deceased parent's name and probate hasn't been done, the inmate doesn't own anything yet — they have an expectancy. Probate has to happen first (see our Dallas probate guide for the Texas process). An incarcerated heir can still participate in probate by affidavit and POA.

Where the Money Goes

Texas title companies will wire sale proceeds wherever the seller directs in writing. Options for an incarcerated seller:

  • TDCJ Inmate Trust Fund. Deposits via JPay or by money order to the TDCJ Inmate Trust Fund in Huntsville. There's no cap on the balance, but large deposits can trigger review. Not ideal for six figures.
  • Outside bank account in the inmate's name. Most banks won't open one for an incarcerated person, but an existing account stays open. Proceeds can be wired there.
  • Attorney trust (IOLTA) account. Cleanest for larger amounts. The attorney holds the funds and disburses per the inmate's written instructions, including to family for commissary, legal fees, or restitution.
  • Family member's account. Fast but risky. It's a gift to that person legally unless there's a written agreement. We've seen it go wrong.

Note: if the inmate owes court-ordered restitution or child support in Texas, the Attorney General's Child Support Division can lien real property, and restitution can attach to proceeds. Title will catch these. Deal with them before closing, not after.

The Texas Carrying-Cost Problem

Texas has no state income tax, which means property taxes are high. A $250,000 house in Harris County carries $5,000-$7,000 a year. If the owner is incarcerated and nobody's paying, the county can begin tax foreclosure after the taxes are delinquent on July 1 following the year they were due — and Texas tax foreclosure moves fast once it starts. The homestead exemption also drops off if the owner isn't living there, bumping the bill 15-25%.

Add vacant-dwelling insurance ($150-$300/month or nothing if nobody buys it), summer heat cooking an empty house with the AC off, and the Texas squatter problem that's gotten bad enough the Legislature passed a removal statute in 2025. A house that sits for a 3-year sentence in Texas loses $20,000-$50,000 in taxes, insurance, and condition before the owner ever gets out.

Step-by-Step: What to Do This Week

  1. Confirm the facility and TDCJ number (or jail booking number). You'll need it on every piece of correspondence.
  2. Pull the deed from the county clerk's real property records — most Texas counties are online. Confirm whose name is on it and how title is held.
  3. Check for homestead. If married and it's the homestead, both spouses must sign. Plan for it.
  4. Check taxes. County tax assessor-collector website. If delinquent, find out how far along the tax suit is.
  5. Decide the path: POA to family, direct signing, or cash buyer.
  6. Get the POA drafted by a Texas attorney (or by the buyer's attorney). Limited to this one property. Mail it to the inmate with instructions to submit an I-60 for notary.
  7. Record the POA in the county where the house sits once it's notarized and mailed back.
  8. List or sign with the buyer. Title company handles the rest.

Bottom Line

Texas lets an incarcerated owner sell. The law isn't the obstacle — the notary logistics are, and they're solvable at every TDCJ unit, most county jails, and all the federal facilities. If there's a trustworthy person outside, give them a limited POA and let them list it. If there isn't, or the house is deteriorating, or the taxes are already delinquent, a cash buyer who handles the coordination gets it done in weeks. Either way, don't let it sit. Texas property taxes don't wait for a release date.

Frequently Asked Questions

Can an inmate in a Texas prison sell their house?

Yes. Incarceration does not affect property rights in Texas. An inmate at any TDCJ unit, county jail, or federal facility can sell real estate they own, either by signing a deed before a notary at the facility or by granting a durable power of attorney to someone outside who signs for them.

How does an inmate get a document notarized in a TDCJ prison?

The inmate submits an I-60 request form to the unit's Access to Courts supervisor requesting notary services. The document must be in the inmate's possession. Turnaround is typically 3-10 business days. Alternatively, an attorney (with a notary on staff) can arrange a legal visit to notarize documents.

Does Texas require witnesses on a power of attorney?

No. A Texas durable power of attorney must be acknowledged before a notary but does not require witnesses. (Wills require two witnesses; POAs do not.) The statutory form under Estates Code §752.051 is accepted by all Texas title companies.

Can my spouse sell our house while I'm in jail in Texas?

If it's the homestead and you're married, no — Texas Family Code §5.001 requires both spouses to sign a conveyance of the homestead regardless of whose name is on the deed. You'd give your spouse a limited POA so they can sign for you. If it's not the homestead and only your spouse is on the deed, they can sell without you.

Can I sell just my share of an inherited house from prison in Texas?

Yes, if you hold title as a tenant in common. You can sell your undivided interest to a buyer without the other owners' consent. The buyer becomes a co-owner. If the house is still in the deceased's name and probate hasn't been done, you'll need to complete probate first.

Where does the money go if I sell a house from prison?

Wherever you direct the title company in writing: the TDCJ Inmate Trust Fund, an existing outside bank account in your name, an attorney's IOLTA trust account, or a family member's account. For larger sums, an attorney trust account is the safest — the attorney disburses per your written instructions.

Will back taxes or restitution come out of the sale?

Yes. Delinquent property taxes are a lien and get paid at closing. Court-ordered restitution and Texas child support arrears can also attach to real property or proceeds. The title company will find them. Address them before closing so they don't derail it.

How long does it take to sell a house from a Texas prison?

With a cash buyer coordinating the POA and facility notary: 3-6 weeks. With a family member using a POA and listing with a Realtor: 3-5 months. Direct signing with multiple notary visits and a retail listing: 3-6 months. The I-60 notary request is usually the slowest single step at 3-10 business days.

Can a Texas county jail inmate sell a house before trial?

Yes. Pre-trial detainees have the same property rights. The practical question is whether they'll bond out soon — if release is likely within weeks, waiting may be simpler than coordinating a jail notary. If detention will be long, proceed with a POA. Large county jails (Harris, Dallas, Bexar, Tarrant, Travis) have attorney-visit procedures that accommodate a notary.

Does a cash buyer pay less because the seller is in prison?

A reputable one doesn't. The offer is based on the house — as-is value, repairs, and the buyer's margin. The buyer absorbs the coordination cost (POA drafting, notary visit, title) as part of the service. Incarcerated-seller deals often close at 65-80% of as-is value because the houses are usually vacant and deteriorating, not because the seller is inside.
