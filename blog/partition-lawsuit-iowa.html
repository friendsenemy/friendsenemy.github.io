Partition Lawsuits in Iowa: One Referee Writes the Sale Plan, and the Court Can Approve a Price Below the Appraisal

Iowa rewrote its partition law in 2018 and moved it out of the court rules into Iowa Code chapter 651. The result puts one person at the center of every case: a court-appointed referee who orders the appraisal, recommends whether the house goes to auction or to a private listing, and reports every proposed sale back to the judge — who may approve a private sale for less than the appraised value. Here is how to work with that system instead of being run over by it. Or ask us a question, free.

Most people who search for "partition lawsuit Iowa" picture a courtroom fight between siblings. The reality in an Iowa district court is quieter and more administrative. After the judge decides who owns what share, the case is handed to a referee — usually a local lawyer — and the referee's recommendations shape almost everything that follows: how the property is valued, whether it is offered at a public auction or through a real estate agent, what price the court is asked to accept, and how long the house sits before anyone gets paid.

That is the single most useful fact about an Iowa partition. The owners who come out best are rarely the ones with the angriest pleadings. They are the ones who treat the referee as the decision-maker in practice, get their evidence in front of that person early, and argue about the sale method before the report is filed rather than after.

🧑‍⚖️ The Referee Is the Person Who Actually Runs Your Case

Iowa Code § 651.12 tells the judge to enter an initial decree that does four things: fixes the shares and interests of every owner, appoints one referee (more only if every owner agrees), orders an appraisal or other valuation, and directs the referee to file a report recommending how to finish the partition. The decree may already say whether the sale will be public or private.

From then on the referee does the legwork. Under § 651.18 a referee handling a sale takes an oath and files a report that recommends "the appropriate public or private sale process," expressly including "a public auction or private listing," with a copy of any appraisal the court required. The judge sets a hearing on that report and may approve it, modify it, or reject it. Only then does the property go on the market.

Three practical consequences follow:

  • 📞 The referee will listen to you before the report is filed. Send the referee recent comparable sales, repair bids, photos and the name of an agent who knows the neighborhood. Doing that after the report is filed means persuading a judge to overrule the person the judge appointed.
  • 🏷️ An auction is not automatic. Iowa's statute treats a private listing as an ordinary option. If you think a listed sale would bring more, say so to the referee and say why.
  • 🧾 The referee's time is a cost you share. Section 651.24 makes referees, appraisers and any attorney the referee hires with court approval part of the taxed costs. A referee who has to chase uncooperative owners bills more hours, and those hours come out of everyone's proceeds.
⚠️ The warning most Iowa co-owners need: the court "may expressly order a private sale of the property for less than the appraised value" (§ 651.18(8)). The appraisal is not a price floor. If the house sits on the market and the only offer is low, the referee can bring that offer to the judge — and every owner is then arguing against a sale that has already been negotiated.

🔎 Three Appraisers Unless You All Agree on Something Else

Iowa's default valuation method is unusual. Unless every owner agrees to a different method, § 651.12 requires the decree to appoint three disinterested persons with knowledge of property valuation to appraise the property. Three appraisers cost more than one, and for a modest house the fees can feel out of proportion — but the owners can agree to a single licensed appraiser or even to a broker's opinion of value, and in a case where nobody disputes the number that agreement is usually worth making.

Where the value really is in dispute — a farmhouse on acreage, a rental with deferred maintenance, a house a co-owner has improved at their own expense — the three-person panel is a protection, not a waste. Bring the panel what it needs: the cost of the roof one owner paid for, the inspection report from last spring, the rent the property could bring. The appraisal will go into the referee's report and, later, into the judge's thinking when a below-appraisal offer is on the table.

🏠 When One Co-Owner Lives There and the Rest Pay

The most common Iowa partition we see is not two strangers. It is an inherited house in Waterloo or Sioux City where one sibling moved in, stopped paying rent to anyone, and stopped answering the others. Chapter 651 gives the court real tools for that:

  • 🔑 Under § 651.15 the court may order the referee to lease the property or take possession of it, may issue an injunction to preserve it, and may make orders for its care and custody. Those expenses are taxed as costs.
  • ⚖️ Under § 651.7 a party may ask the court to adjudicate "any matter originating from or connected to the property, including a lien between any parties." Claims for unpaid property taxes, insurance, or the mortgage one owner carried alone belong in the same case.
  • 🚫 What the same section forbids is turning the case into a general family lawsuit: "A counterclaim to a partition petition shall not be permitted," and other claims cannot be joined except as the section allows.

If the occupying sibling has paid the taxes and kept the roof on, expect that to be credited. If they have done neither, the referee's power to take possession is often what finally moves the case toward a sale.

💰 Iowa Makes Everyone Pay the Plaintiff's Lawyer

This is the rule that changes behavior. Iowa Code § 651.23 says that on partition of real property "the court shall order a reasonable fee in favor of the plaintiff's attorney," taxed as costs. Under § 651.22 the plaintiff advances the costs, and the costs are then paid by all parties in proportion to their interests — out of the sale proceeds when there is a sale.

So if you own a one-third share and your sister files, you will generally pay roughly a third of her lawyer's reasonable fee, plus a third of the referee, the appraisers and the publication costs. Two exceptions matter:

  • ⚔️ Contests. A cost created by a contest is taxed against the losing contestant unless the court orders otherwise, and if the plaintiff loses a contest, her attorney fees for that contest are not taxed as costs at all.
  • 🧾 Your own lawyer. The statute shifts the plaintiff's attorney fee, not yours. A defendant who hires counsel pays that counsel.

The lesson is not "never contest." It is "contest only what is worth contesting." Fighting over whether a sale should happen at all, when the house plainly cannot be divided, tends to cost the person who fights. Fighting over a lowball private offer, with evidence, is often worth it.

⛔ Timing Rules That Stop Iowa Partition Cases Before They Start

Section 651.3 blocks two kinds of premature filings when the house belonged entirely to someone whose estate is open in probate:

  1. No partition action may be brought until four months after the second publication of the notice of the personal representative's appointment.
  2. No partition may be brought at any time while an application for authority to sell the real estate is pending in the probate case.

If your parent died recently and an estate is open, the faster route to a sale is often inside the probate file, through the executor, not through a separate partition case. Our guide to what to do when heirs can't agree walks through that choice.

🌾 The Heirs Property Track Starts Only on Request

Iowa adopted special rules for "heirs property" — tenancy-in-common property where there is no recorded agreement governing partition, at least one owner got title from a relative, and relatives hold enough of the ownership. Those rules (§§ 651.27–651.32) add a court-determined fair market value, a notice and 30-day objection window, a 45-day election for the non-selling owners to buy out the owner who asked for a sale, and a list of factors such as family ownership history and sentimental attachment.

The catch is the trigger. Under § 651.27 the special track applies when a cotenant requests a partition in kind. For a single-family house on a city lot, a physical division is rarely realistic, but the request is what opens the door to the appraisal and buyout procedure. If keeping the family farmhouse matters to you, raise it with your Iowa attorney in your first answer, not later.

📜 Abstracts, Liens and the Referee's Deed

Iowa still runs on abstracts of title more than most states, and chapter 651 reflects it. Under § 651.13 the court may order a complete abstract filed, may order any party holding the abstract to produce it, and may order the plaintiff to obtain one (or a plat or survey), with the cost taxed as costs. If you have the family abstract in a drawer, expect to be asked for it.

Liens are handled inside the case. Every lienholder is an indispensable party under § 651.5, § 651.14 has the court decide the nature, priority and validity of liens, and § 651.20 says real property sells free of all liens except liens held against the entire property. In plain terms: a judgment against one co-owner's share is paid out of that owner's proceeds, while a mortgage on the whole house stays attached unless it is paid off at closing. After the sale is approved, the referee records a referee's deed under § 651.19, and the buyer's title is good against every party to the case. The referee must post a bond of 125% of the sale price before conveying.

⌛ What an Iowa Partition Usually Costs and How Long It Runs

Realistic ranges, not promises:

  • 🗓️ Uncontested case ending in a listing: roughly 8–14 months from petition to distribution. Auctions can be faster because the notice period is short — two newspaper publications at least six days apart, the last at least seven days before the sale.
  • ⚔️ Contested case: 12–24 months when ownership shares, credits or the sale price are fought over.
  • 💵 Shared costs: filing fee, service and publication run a few hundred dollars; appraisers often $400–$1,500 combined; referee fees and the plaintiff's attorney fee together commonly reach several thousand dollars on a simple house and much more if contested.
  • 🏚️ Carrying costs while it runs: Iowa's effective property tax averages around 1.5% of value a year, higher in Des Moines and Davenport. On a $200,000 house that is about $250–$300 a month, plus $100–$200 for a vacant-dwelling policy, $120–$250 for utilities kept on through winter, and lawn and snow care.

💵 A Davenport Duplex, Worked Through

Three cousins inherit a Davenport duplex. Fixed up it would sell for about $240,000. It needs a roof, two furnaces and kitchen work — call it $40,000. One cousin lives in the upper unit and pays nothing.

  • 🏛️ Partition with a referee's listing: perhaps $195,000 as-is after a year, minus a 6% commission ($11,700), minus taxed costs and the plaintiff's fee (say $9,000), minus a year of taxes, insurance and utilities (about $8,000). Roughly $166,000 to split, a year from now, with a court approving every step.
  • 🔨 Partition at auction: perhaps $160,000–$175,000 at the gavel, minus similar costs, in 6–9 months.
  • 💵 Cash sale: an offer at 60–80% of the $240,000 repaired value is $144,000–$192,000; on this condition we would likely land near $165,000–$175,000, close in a few weeks, and pay closing costs. If only one cousin wants out, we can buy just that one-third interest instead.

On these numbers the listing and the cash sale end in a similar place, and the difference is time, control and family peace. On a duplex that only needed paint, the listing would win clearly and we would tell you so.

🗂️ Four Exits for Iowa Co-Owners

🤝 Agree before anyone files

Cheapest by far

One appraiser, a written buyout or listing agreement, a deadline, and a clause for who gets reimbursed for taxes and repairs. No referee, no shared plaintiff's fee.

🏷️ Ask the referee for a private listing

Best court price

Give the referee an agent, comps and a realistic list price before the report is filed. Cooperate with showings; it shortens the case.

🧮 Use the heirs property buyout

If you want to keep it

Available only on heirs property and only once a cotenant requests partition in kind. You pay the court-determined value times the seller's share, on the court's deadline.

Fastest certainty

📋 What To Do This Week

  1. Pull the deed and the abstract. Confirm how title is held and whether any owner's share has a judgment or lien against it.
  2. Check probate status. If the last owner's estate is open, note the date of the second publication of notice; § 651.3 counts from it.
  3. Write down what each owner has paid for taxes, insurance, mortgage and repairs, with receipts. Those credits are argued inside the case.
  4. Get two numbers: an agent's as-is price opinion and a written cash offer, so you know what a referee's listing is competing against.
  5. Send one calm written proposal to the other owners before anyone files. Copy it to your attorney.
  6. If you have been served, calendar the answer deadline and talk to an Iowa attorney about whether to request partition in kind.

✅ Bottom Line

An Iowa partition is less a trial than a managed sale. The judge fixes the shares, a single referee writes the plan, three appraisers (or one you agree on) set a reference value, and the court can approve a private sale below that value. Everyone shares the plaintiff's attorney fee. If you are going to spend effort anywhere, spend it on the referee's report and on the sale method — that is where the money is won or lost.

If the owners can still talk, settle outside court. If the house is sound, push for a listing. If it needs work or the case is stuck, ask us for a written number and compare it with what the referee's sale would leave you.

❓ Iowa Partition Questions, Answered Plainly

What does a referee do in an Iowa partition case?

The referee is appointed in the court's initial decree under Iowa Code § 651.12. The referee obtains the appraisal, recommends whether the property should be sold at public auction or by private listing, reports every proposed sale to the court, gives notice of hearings, and, once a sale is approved, records the referee's deed. The referee's fees are taxed as costs and shared by the owners.

Is the appraisal a minimum price in an Iowa partition sale?

No. Iowa Code § 651.18 lets the court expressly order a private sale for less than the appraised value. The appraisal is evidence the judge will weigh at the sale hearing, so owners who think an offer is too low need to come to that hearing with comparable sales, repair estimates or a better offer.

How many appraisers does an Iowa court appoint in a partition?

Unless all owners agree to another method, the initial decree appoints three disinterested persons with knowledge of property valuation. Owners can agree to one appraiser or another way of estimating value, which usually saves money when the value is not seriously disputed.

Do I have to pay the attorney of the co-owner who sued me in Iowa?

Generally, in part. Iowa Code § 651.23 requires the court to award a reasonable fee to the plaintiff's attorney on partition of real property, taxed as costs, and costs are shared in proportion to each owner's interest. Costs created by a contest are taxed against the losing side, and a plaintiff who loses a contest does not recover attorney fees for that contest.

Can an Iowa partition be sold through a real estate agent instead of an auction?

Yes. The referee's report must recommend a public or private sale process, and the statute names private listing as an option. The judge decides after a hearing. Owners who want a listing should give the referee an agent, comparable sales and a list price before the report is filed.

Can I file a partition while my parent's estate is still in probate in Iowa?

Not right away if the parent owned the whole property. Iowa Code § 651.3 bars a partition action until four months after the second publication of notice of the personal representative's appointment, and at any time while an application to sell the real estate is pending in probate.

What happens to a lien against one co-owner's share in an Iowa partition sale?

Iowa Code § 651.20 provides that real property is sold free of all liens except liens held against the entire property. A judgment or lien against one owner's share is paid from that owner's portion of the proceeds; a mortgage on the whole house stays attached unless it is paid at closing.

Can the court remove a co-owner who is living in the house during an Iowa partition?

The court has tools to do so. Iowa Code § 651.15 allows the court to order the referee to lease the property or take possession of it, to issue an injunction to preserve it, and to make orders for its care and custody, with the expenses taxed as costs.

When do Iowa's heirs property protections apply in a partition?

They apply to tenancy-in-common property that qualifies as heirs property under Iowa Code § 651.1 and only when a cotenant requests partition in kind. The court then determines fair market value, allows objections, and gives the non-selling owners 45 days to elect to buy out the owner who asked for a sale.

How long does a partition lawsuit take in Iowa?

A cooperative case that ends in a referee's listing commonly takes 8 to 14 months from petition to distribution. Auctions can be faster because the published notice period is short. Contested cases over shares, credits or price often run 12 to 24 months. A negotiated buyout or a cash sale can close in weeks.
