Selling Your Share of a Co-Owned Connecticut House: Why the Town Land Records, Not the Paperwork in Your Drawer, Decide What You Can Sell

Connecticut's survivorship statute turns on recording. A severance deed, and even a divorce decree, doesn't change ownership as far as the rest of the world is concerned until it's on the land records in the town where the house sits. Add a seller-paid conveyance tax and a Probate Court that can order inherited property partitioned or sold, and a co-owner in Connecticut has a different checklist than a co-owner next door in New York or Massachusetts. You're on the deed to a Connecticut house with someone else, and you want out. Maybe it's an ex-spouse who stayed in the house, a sibling you inherited it with, or a partner who stopped paying their half. Before talking about lawsuits or buyouts, a Connecticut co-owner needs to answer a more basic question: what does the public record say you own today? In this state, that answer can be different from what you think your divorce decree or your own deed accomplished.

🗂️ The Town Clerk's Land Records Decide What a Connecticut Co-Owner Can Sell

Connecticut keeps its land records town by town, at the town clerk's office, not at a county recorder. Every deed, mortgage, lien, certificate of devise and recorded court decree affecting your property is indexed there. Connecticut's joint tenancy statutes, General Statutes §§ 47-14a to 47-14k, lean on those records more heavily than most states' laws do. Two rules in particular make recording the event that counts:

  • ✂️ Changing your own survivorship interest. Under § 47-14j, a joint tenant can make any change to their interest that a conveyance to a stranger could make, by signing an instrument with deed formalities. That instrument isn't effective until it's recorded on the land records of the town where the house is.
  • 📜 Divorce. Under § 47-14g, a divorce severs a married couple's joint tenancy and makes them tenants in common as to each other, unless the decree says otherwise. But the severance isn't effective as to anyone else until a certified copy of the decree, or an abstract showing its effective date, is recorded in that town's land records.

The practical upshot is simple. If you've signed something or been divorced and nothing was recorded, a title examiner, a lender or a buyer may still see a survivorship joint tenancy, and your ex or co-owner may still be positioned to take the whole house if you die first.

⚠️ The warning that matters most: if you were divorced in Connecticut while on a deed with your former spouse, call the town clerk or search the land records online and confirm that the decree or an abstract of it was recorded. Until it is, the severance doesn't bind third parties, and a sale or mortgage of the house can be built on the wrong assumption about who owns what.

💍 A "Tenants by the Entirety" Deed Is a Joint Tenancy Here

Connecticut does not give married couples the special entireties form of ownership that New Jersey, Pennsylvania and many other states do. Section 47-14a lists the deed language that creates a joint tenancy with survivorship, and one of the listed forms is a conveyance to two grantees "as tenants by the entirety." In other words, if your deed uses that phrase, Connecticut reads it as an ordinary survivorship joint tenancy. The same section lists other phrasings that do the same thing, including "as joint tenants with right of survivorship," a grant to the owners "and unto the survivor of them," and simply adding "as joint tenants" after the names.

Section 47-14a also says the owners' interests can be equal or unequal. If one of you put in two-thirds of the purchase price and the deed recites unequal shares, Connecticut honors that. If the deed is silent, expect a buyer and a title company to start from equal shares.

📜 Divorce Severs Survivorship, but Only After the Decree Is Recorded

For divorced co-owners, § 47-14g does most of the work automatically. Once the marriage is dissolved, unless the decree provides differently, the former spouses become tenants in common with each other. If they owned with a third person, say a parent, the parent's survivorship relationship with the remaining owners doesn't change.

Three things to check on your decree before you sell or buy out:

  1. 🔍 Does it say something different? Many Connecticut decrees order one spouse to quitclaim the house to the other by a date, or order a sale. If so, the decree controls, and selling your interest to a third party could violate it.
  2. 🗓️ Has the deadline passed? If your ex was ordered to refinance or buy you out by a date and didn't, your remedy may be a motion in the family case rather than a new lawsuit.
  3. 📌 Is it recorded? If not, record a certified copy or abstract in the town land records before doing anything else.

✂️ Selling or Severing Your Own Interest

A tenant in common in Connecticut can sell their undivided interest without the other owner's signature. A joint tenant can, too. Under § 47-14c, a conveyance by fewer than all the joint tenants to someone outside the group severs the joint tenancy as to the interest conveyed, and the buyer holds it as a tenant in common with the remaining owners. If there were three or more joint tenants, the ones who didn't sell keep their survivorship relationship with each other.

Some owners don't want to sell yet; they just don't want their share to pass to the other owner if they die first. Section 47-14j lets them sign and record an instrument that converts their own interest without involving anyone else. Because that's a recorded change to title, it's worth having a Connecticut lawyer draft it and confirm how it interacts with any mortgage.

What none of these steps does is give you money from the other owner or the right to move in and exclude them. To get the house sold over their objection, you need agreement or a court. 🏛️ Inherited Shares: the Probate Court Can Order a Partition or Sale

Connecticut has a route most states don't: while an estate is still being settled, the Probate Court can partition property the person who died owned with others. Under General Statutes § 45a-326, when someone dies owning an undivided interest that wasn't specifically left to anyone in the will, the executor or administrator, together with the owners of the major portion of the other interests, can petition the Probate Court handling the estate to partition the property. If the court finds a sale will better promote the owners' interests, or that the property can't be beneficially divided, it can order a sale in the manner it finds best.

Some limits make this a narrow tool rather than a general solution:

  • 🗳️ Who can ask. The petition comes from the estate's fiduciary plus the owners of most of the remaining interest, not from any single heir acting alone.
  • 📣 Notice and a full hearing. Unless everyone in interest signs the petition, the court must give public notice and hold a full hearing, and it can order partition or sale only if that's in the best interests of the estate and the parties.
  • 💼 A probate bond. Before a sale order takes effect, the person selling generally must post a probate bond, unless the court waives it.
  • 👥 A committee. For a physical division, the court can appoint a committee of three disinterested people to divide the property and report back.

If you and your siblings inherited a share of a house that your parent owned with someone else, ask the estate's attorney whether a Probate Court petition is faster than a Superior Court case. For family disagreements more generally, see our guide on what to do when heirs can't agree.

🧾 The Seller's Conveyance Tax, and the Deeds That Skip It

Connecticut charges a real estate conveyance tax when property changes hands, and unlike in many states, the seller pays it. Under General Statutes § 12-494, the state portion on a residential sale is 0.75 percent of the price up to $800,000, 1.25 percent on the portion from $800,000 to $2.5 million, and 2.25 percent above that. Towns add 0.25 percent, and certain designated towns can add up to another 0.25 percent.

When you sell only your interest, the tax is figured on what you're paid for that interest. On a $150,000 sale of a half share in a town with the standard municipal rate, that's about $1,500 in combined state and town tax.

Section 12-498 lists exempt transfers. Two matter to co-owners: "deeds of partition," and deeds made under a Superior Court decree under § 46b-81 (the divorce property statute) or § 52-495 (partition). That means a deed one ex-spouse gives the other under a divorce decree is generally exempt, while a sale of the whole house to an outside buyer is not. Ask the closing attorney to confirm the exemption before you rely on it.

📊 Choosing a Path

🏷️ List it together

Highest net

If both owners will sign, list with a local agent. Connecticut inventory has been tight, and a house that's merely dated often sells close to asking. You'll pay commission and the conveyance tax, and you'll still come out ahead of any discounted route.

🤝 Buyout under a recorded agreement

Cleanest for divorced owners

If your ex or sibling wants the house, a buyout using a recent appraisal and a quitclaim at closing is usually the simplest fix. If it's done under a divorce decree, the deed may be exempt from conveyance tax.

🧩 Sell your interest to a specialist

Fastest exit

You can close in a few weeks without the other owner. You'll accept a discount for handing the buyer a co-owner and a possible lawsuit, and you'll pay conveyance tax on your price.

⚖️ Court-ordered division or sale

Last resort

Superior Court partition or sale for most co-owners; the Probate Court route under § 45a-326 when an estate is mid-settlement. Both take months and lawyers.

💵 Holding and Selling a New Britain Two-Family

Connecticut's property taxes are among the heaviest in the country, and that changes the math of waiting. Take a two-family house in New Britain owned 50/50 by a brother and sister. Fixed up, it would sell for about $380,000; as it stands, about $340,000. The upstairs unit is vacant and the downstairs tenant is month-to-month.

  • 🧾 Property tax. Connecticut towns assess at 70 percent of market value. New Britain's real property mill rate for fiscal 2026 is 39.18, according to the state's published mill rates. On an appraised value of $340,000, that's $238,000 assessed and about $9,325 a year, or roughly $777 a month.
  • 🛡️ Insurance. A landlord policy with a vacant unit runs roughly $150 to $260 a month.
  • 🔥 Heat and upkeep. Heating a vacant unit through a Connecticut winter, plus snow removal and repairs, can add $150 to $350 a month.
  • 💸 Offsetting rent. The downstairs rent helps, but the siblings must account to each other for it.

Before any rent, holding costs run roughly $1,075 to $1,390 a month. A year of disagreement costs about $13,000 to $16,700.

The three exits on this house:

  • 📈 Listed together: $340,000 less about 6 percent commission and roughly 1 percent conveyance tax leaves about $316,000, or $158,000 each before closing costs.
  • 💵 Cash sale of the whole house: at 60 to 80 percent of the $380,000 repaired value, $228,000 to $304,000. Here we'd likely be around $265,000 to $285,000, closed in a few weeks with the tenant in place, or about $132,500 to $142,500 each before conveyance tax.
  • 🧩 One sibling's half, alone: offers might run $95,000 to $125,000, because the buyer is taking on the other sibling, the tenant and possibly a court case.

  1. 🗂️ Search the land records in the town where the house sits and print the vesting deed and anything recorded since.
  2. 📜 If you're divorced, confirm the decree or an abstract is recorded; if not, get a certified copy and record it.

✅ Bottom Line

In Connecticut, a co-owner can sell or sever their own interest without permission, a "tenants by the entirety" deed is just a survivorship joint tenancy, and a divorce severs survivorship between former spouses unless the decree says otherwise. But in each case, what binds the rest of the world is what's recorded in the town land records. Add a conveyance tax the seller pays, and a Probate Court that can order inherited property partitioned or sold, and the right first step is almost always a trip through the land records, not a lawsuit.

❓ Frequently Asked Questions

Does Connecticut have tenancy by the entirety?

Not as a separate form of ownership. Connecticut General Statutes 47-14a treats a deed to two people as tenants by the entirety as a survivorship joint tenancy, so either owner's interest can be conveyed or severed like any other joint tenancy.

Does divorce end a joint tenancy in Connecticut?

Yes, between the former spouses. Under 47-14g, divorce severs their joint tenancy and makes them tenants in common unless the decree says otherwise. It isn't effective against anyone else until a certified copy or abstract of the decree is recorded in the town land records.

Can one joint tenant sever survivorship in Connecticut without the other?

Yes. Section 47-14j lets a joint tenant change their own interest by signing an instrument with deed formalities, and 47-14c says a conveyance to an outsider severs that interest. Either way, the change takes effect when it's recorded on the town land records.

Where are Connecticut property records kept?

In the land records of the town where the property is located, maintained by the town clerk. Deeds, mortgages, liens and recorded court decrees affecting the house are all indexed there.

Who pays the conveyance tax when a Connecticut co-owner sells?

The seller. The state rate on a residential sale is 0.75 percent up to $800,000, 1.25 percent from $800,000 to $2.5 million and 2.25 percent above, plus a municipal tax of 0.25 percent that some designated towns can raise.

Is a deed between ex-spouses under a divorce decree subject to Connecticut conveyance tax?

Generally not. Section 12-498 exempts deeds made under a Superior Court decree under 46b-81, the divorce property statute, as well as deeds of partition. Confirm with the closing attorney before relying on it.

Can the Connecticut Probate Court order an inherited house sold?

In some cases. Under 45a-326, during settlement of an estate, the executor or administrator and the owners of the major portion of the other interests can petition the Probate Court to partition the property, and the court can order a sale if that better promotes the owners' interests.

Can I sell my half of a Connecticut house if my co-owner refuses?

Yes, as a tenant in common, or as a joint tenant whose conveyance severs survivorship as to the share sold. The buyer becomes a co-owner with the other owner. Expect a discount, and you'll owe conveyance tax on your price.

Can Connecticut joint tenants have unequal shares?

Yes. Section 47-14a says the interests of joint tenants may be held in equal or unequal shares. If the deed doesn't say, title companies and buyers generally start from equal shares.

Should Connecticut co-owners list the house or sell for cash?

Listing usually nets more when every owner will sign and the house is in good shape. A cash sale makes sense with tenants, major repairs, an uncooperative owner, or heavy property taxes that make every month of waiting expensive.
