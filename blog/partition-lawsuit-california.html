Partition Lawsuits in California: You Can't Refuse One, but You Can Choose the Exit

California's Code of Civil Procedure makes partition a right for any co-owner, not a favor the judge grants. Saying no doesn't stop it. What you can control is which of California's built-in exits you take: a contractual waiver, a partition by appraisal, the 45-day buyout, or a deal before anyone files. Here's how each one works, what each one costs, and when the smartest move is simply to list the house.

Most people who type "how to avoid a partition lawsuit" into a search bar in California are hoping to hear about a defense. A loophole, a hardship argument, some way to tell a judge that a sister who wants out shouldn't be allowed to force the issue. California mostly doesn't offer one. What it offers instead is unusual: a set of statutory exits that let co-owners end the co-ownership without the most expensive version of the lawsuit. Knowing those exits is the real answer to the question.

🔑 California Treats Partition as a Right, So "Avoiding" It Means Choosing Your Exit

The key sentence is in Code of Civil Procedure Section 872.710(b): partition as to concurrent interests in property "shall be as of right unless barred by a valid waiver." Read that slowly. The judge at a California partition trial is not deciding whether partition is fair, whether the plaintiff has a good reason, or whether the defendant would be hurt by losing the house. The judge is deciding whether the plaintiff owns an interest and whether that right was waived. If the answer is yes and no, partition happens.

That changes the strategy for both sides. If you're the co-owner who wants to stay, the energy people pour into "fighting" usually goes nowhere and gets billed by the hour. If you're the co-owner who wants out, you hold a strong hand, but you still pay for the lawsuit until the end, and a cheap negotiated exit often beats winning slowly.

So the useful questions in California are different from the ones in most states:

  • 🔍 Was the right to partition waived or limited? That's the main defense the statute recognizes.
  • 🏛️ Is this even the right court? Spouses and open probate estates have their own forums.
  • 🧾 Can we agree on value? If so, partition by appraisal or a negotiated buyout can replace most of the lawsuit.
  • ⏱️ If it's filed, who will use the 45-day buyout window? That's where the defendant's leverage lives after 2023.

🚪 The Three Arguments That Actually Stop or Redirect a California Partition

1. A valid waiver, including one hiding in a TIC agreement

Co-owners can give up or restrict the right to partition by contract, and California courts enforce those deals. The most-cited example is LEG Investments v. Boxler (2010) 182 Cal.App.4th 14, where a tenancy-in-common agreement gave the other owner a right of first refusal. The court read that clause as modifying the right to partition rather than wiping it out: the selling owner first had to offer the interest to the other owner, and only after that offer was refused or ignored could partition go forward. The lesson for you is to dig out every document you signed when you bought: a TIC agreement, an LLC operating agreement, a buy-sell clause, even a side letter. A right of first refusal or a no-partition period can buy time, set a price formula, or force an offer to you before anything else happens.

2. Spouses go to family court, not partition

Section 872.210(b) says an action between spouses or putative spouses to partition their community or quasi-community property can't be brought under the partition title at all. Married owners divide a California house through the dissolution case, where community property rules, reimbursement claims and the family court's own timing apply. If your co-owner is a spouse or ex-spouse and the divorce isn't final, the partition complaint is the wrong tool. Our California divorce guide covers that path.

3. An open probate estate has its own partition procedure

If a parent died and the estate is still being administered, siblings don't have to wait for distribution and then sue each other. Probate Code Section 11950 lets any beneficiary entitled to an undivided interest, or the personal representative at a beneficiary's request, petition the probate court for a partition, allotment or other division that avoids handing out undivided shares. That keeps the dispute in the case that already exists, in front of a judge who already knows the estate. It only reaches interests subject to administration, so it won't help when the house passed outside probate, for example through a trust or joint tenancy. For the bigger picture on stuck estates, see what to do when heirs can't agree.

One more narrow point: if the dispute is between a life tenant and the people who inherit after them (successive estates rather than co-owners), Section 872.710(c) says partition is allowed only if it's in the best interest of all parties. That's a real balancing test, unlike the co-owner rule, and it's one of the few places a California judge weighs hardship.

⚠️ The most expensive mistake: answering a California partition complaint with a general "I don't want to sell" defense. It isn't one. If you have no waiver, wrong-court or successive-estate argument, spend your money on value, credits and the buyout instead, because the costs of partition can come out of the sale proceeds before anyone is paid.

🧾 Partition by Appraisal: The California Statute Almost Nobody Uses

California has a procedure that most states lack and that most co-owners have never heard of: partition by appraisal, Sections 873.910 through 873.980. Once the parties' ownership interests are undisputed (or a court has decided them), they can agree in a written document filed with the clerk that one or more of them will buy the others out at a value set by a court-appointed referee.

The written agreement has to name the property, the parties and their interests, who is willing to acquire, the referee or referees everyone consents to, and the appraisal date. It can also add protective terms, and this is where the procedure gets useful: the agreement can say the buyer may walk away if the appraisal comes in above a stated number, require a deposit, set credit terms and allocate the expenses. A party then moves the court to approve it, and if the terms are equitable the court approves it and stays any pending sale.

The court appoints one referee, or three if the agreement says so, to appraise the property and the interests. Any party can move to confirm, modify or set aside the referee's report on 10 days' notice. If everything is regular, the court orders the interests transferred to the buyers once they pay the price and their share of the costs. The agreement binds heirs and successors, and if the deal collapses, everyone falls back to their ordinary partition rights.

Why it matters: the biggest fight in most California partition cases is about value, and partition by appraisal hands that question to a neutral referee under rules the owners write themselves. It's slower than a handshake buyout but much faster and cheaper than a contested trial, and it gives the owner who wants to stay a defined price and a defined deadline.

⏱️ After Filing: The Buyout Window That Gives Defendants Leverage

For partition cases filed on or after January 1, 2023, the Partition of Real Property Act (CCP Sections 874.311 to 874.323) governs any tenancy in common that isn't controlled by a recorded agreement binding all the cotenants. Its value hearing and buyout rules are the second big exit, and they favor the owner who wants to keep the house.

  • 💲 Value comes first. Under Section 874.316 the court sets fair market value before it considers the merits, usually by appointing a disinterested licensed appraiser. Parties get notice and 30 days to object, and if every cotenant agrees on a value or method, the court adopts it.
  • 🕒 Forty-five days to elect. Under Section 874.317, if anyone asked for a sale, every cotenant who didn't ask for one gets 45 days after notice to elect to buy out the sellers at that value times their fraction.
  • 🏦 Sixty days or more to pay. The court then sets a payment date at least 60 days later. If one buyer defaults, the others get 20 days to take the remainder.
  • 🧩 In kind before sale. If no one buys, Section 874.318 tells the court to divide the property physically unless that would cause "great prejudice" to the owners as a group, weighing factors in Section 874.319 that include how long the family has held the land, sentimental attachment, current use, and who has paid the taxes and upkeep. A single house almost always ends in a sale, but the factors still matter for a large lot, a duplex or rural acreage.

Practical takeaway: if you want to keep the house, line up financing before the value hearing, not after. Forty-five days to elect and roughly two months to pay is not much time to qualify for a cash-out refinance or an equity loan on a house with a recorded lawsuit against it.

📍 What Changes the Day a California Partition Complaint Is Filed

The filing itself has consequences people don't expect.

  • 📌 A lis pendens goes on title. Section 872.250 requires the plaintiff to record a notice of pendency in the county recorder's office immediately after filing. From that moment, anyone dealing with the property is on notice. Refinancing, selling or borrowing against your share becomes very hard until the case resolves or the notice comes off.
  • 🏛️ It's a Superior Court case in the county where the house sits. Section 872.110 puts jurisdiction in the superior court and venue where the real property is located, so a Kern County house is litigated in Bakersfield even if every owner lives in Los Angeles.
  • 💳 Each side pays to appear. Under the Judicial Council's 2026 statewide fee schedule, the first paper in an unlimited civil case is $435 for the plaintiff and $435 for each defendant who answers. In California, a defendant generally has 30 days after service to respond.
  • 🛡️ The court can protect the property meanwhile. Section 872.130 lets the judge issue restraining orders to prevent waste or interference with the property. If a co-owner in possession is stripping fixtures or letting the roof go, ask your lawyer about this early.

🧮 Credits Before Cash: The Section 872.140 Accounting

Whatever exit you take, the money is not simply split by percentages. Section 872.140 lets the court order "allowance, accounting, contribution, or other compensatory adjustment among the parties according to the principles of equity." In practice that means:

  • 🏠 The owner who paid the mortgage principal, property taxes and insurance can usually ask for credit for the other owners' shares of those payments.
  • 🔨 Improvements that raised the value can be credited, generally up to the value they added rather than what they cost.
  • 🔑 An owner living in the house can be charged for that use in some circumstances, especially where the others were shut out or the occupant is claiming credits for payments. California's general rule doesn't make an occupying cotenant pay rent just for living there, so this point is fact-heavy and worth an attorney's read.

Separately, Sections 874.010 and 874.040 define the "costs of partition" (attorney fees for work done for the common benefit, the referee's fee, title reports, surveys) and let the court spread them by ownership share or as equity requires. For cases under the newer act, Section 874.321.5 limits charging those costs to an owner who opposed partition. Bring receipts, bank statements and tax bills to your first meeting with a lawyer. In a lot of California cases, the accounting moves more money than the sale price does.

⚖️ Four California Exits, Rated

🤝 Negotiated buyout before anyone files

Cheapest if value can be agreed

One owner buys the other at a number based on a shared appraisal, with the credits worked out on paper. No lis pendens, no filing fees, no referee. It fails when the two sides are more than an appraisal apart, or when the buyer can't get financing. Get the deal in writing, with escrow, and have each side's lawyer review the deed and settlement terms.

🧾 Partition by appraisal (CCP 873.910)

Best when you agree on who buys, not on price

The owners agree who acquires, a court-appointed referee sets the price, and the buyer can be protected by a walk-away ceiling. It needs a filed case and court approval, so budget for some legal fees, but it skips most of a contested trial.

⏱️ The PRPA buyout election

Strongest tool for the owner who wants to stay

Available once a case is filed and someone asks for a sale. The non-selling owner gets first claim at court-determined value. The risk is timing: if the money isn't ready within the deadlines, the chance passes and the court moves to sale.

🏷️ Court-ordered open-market sale

Fair price, slowest route

Under Section 874.320 a broker lists the house at no less than the court's value, unless the judge finds sealed bids or an auction better. It usually lands near market value, but only after 12 to 24 months of litigation, with fees, referee charges and commission coming off the top.

💵 What Each Path Costs on a $640,000 Riverside County House

Take a three-bedroom house in Riverside County inherited by two siblings. Fully repaired it would sell for about $640,000; as it stands it needs roughly $45,000 of work (roof, HVAC, kitchen). One sibling wants to sell; the other lives out of state and wants to keep it as a rental. They pay about $640 a month in property tax (California's Prop 13 base rate is 1% of assessed value plus local voter-approved charges, so about 1.1% to 1.25% is typical once a transfer resets the assessment), around $200 a month for a vacant-dwelling insurance policy, $175 in utilities and about $250 in basic upkeep. That's roughly $1,265 a month, or about $15,200 a year, before a single legal bill.

📋 What To Do This Week

  1. 📂 Pull every ownership document: the grant deed, any TIC or LLC agreement, the trust if one exists, and any written promise between owners. Look specifically for a right of first refusal, a no-partition clause or a buy-sell formula.
  2. 🏛️ Check for the wrong-court issues: is your co-owner a spouse, or is the house still part of an open probate estate? If so, the partition title probably isn't where this belongs.
  3. 🧾 Build your credit ledger: 3–5 years of mortgage, tax, insurance and repair payments, with who paid each.
  4. 📈 Get a value opinion now: an independent appraisal or at least two broker opinions, so you know whether a buyout is affordable before the 45-day clock exists.
  5. 🤝 Make a written offer before suing or answering: propose a buyout or a partition-by-appraisal agreement with a walk-away ceiling.
  6. ⚖️ Book a consult with a California real estate litigator in the county where the property sits, and bring the ledger.

✅ Bottom Line

California won't let you veto a partition by refusing to sell, because Section 872.710 makes it a right. What California does give you is a menu of exits: a waiver or right of first refusal you may already have signed, family court or probate court when those are the proper forum, a partition by appraisal the owners design themselves, and a court-run buyout that gives the owner who wants to stay first claim at a neutral value. The owners who come out best treat the dispute as a question of value and credits, settle it early, and spend their money on an appraisal instead of a trial. If the house is sound and you can cooperate, list it. If it needs work or the lawsuit is about to swallow the equity, ask us for an honest number.

❓ Frequently Asked Questions

Can I stop my co-owner from filing a partition lawsuit in California?

Usually not. Code of Civil Procedure Section 872.710(b) makes partition between co-owners a matter of right unless it was barred by a valid waiver. The arguments that work are narrow: a written waiver or right of first refusal, a dispute between spouses that belongs in family court, an open probate estate where the probate court can divide the property, or a life estate dispute where the best-interest test applies. Otherwise, your leverage is in value, credits and the buyout.

What is partition by appraisal in California?

It is a procedure in CCP Sections 873.910 to 873.980. Once ownership shares are undisputed, the owners can sign and file a written agreement naming who will buy, which referee they accept and the appraisal date. The court appoints one or three referees to value the property, and the buyers acquire the other interests at that value after paying the price and their share of costs. The agreement can let a buyer walk away if the value exceeds a stated amount.

Does a right of first refusal in our co-ownership agreement waive partition?

Not completely, but it can change the order of events. In LEG Investments v. Boxler (2010) 182 Cal.App.4th 14, a California court held that a right of first refusal in a tenancy-in-common agreement modified the right to partition: the owner who wanted out had to offer the interest to the other owner first, and could pursue partition only after that offer was declined. Have a lawyer read your agreement for any clause like this.

Can my spouse file a partition lawsuit against me in California?

Not for community or quasi-community property. Section 872.210(b) bars partition actions between spouses or putative spouses over that property, so the house is divided in the divorce case under Family Code rules instead. Former spouses who still hold title together after a final judgment may be in a different position, so check what the judgment says about the house.

My siblings and I inherited a house, but probate is still open. Can one of us force a partition?

Usually through the probate court rather than a separate lawsuit. Probate Code Section 11950 lets a beneficiary entitled to an undivided interest, or the personal representative at a beneficiary's request, petition for a partition, allotment or other division so the estate doesn't distribute undivided shares. It covers only property subject to administration, so a house held in a trust or that passed by joint tenancy is handled differently.

What does a lis pendens do to a California house in a partition case?

Section 872.250 requires the plaintiff to record a notice of pendency with the county recorder right after filing. It puts every buyer and lender on notice of the lawsuit, which makes refinancing, borrowing against the house or selling a share very difficult until the case ends or the notice is removed. If you plan to finance a buyout, start before the case is filed if you can.

Will I get credit for the mortgage, taxes and repairs I paid on our California house?

Often, yes. Section 872.140 lets the court order allowances, accounting, contribution or other compensatory adjustments according to equity. Owners who paid more than their share of the mortgage, property taxes, insurance or value-adding improvements commonly receive credits, and an owner in possession may be charged for that use in some situations. Keep records of every payment and who made it.

How much does it cost to file or answer a partition lawsuit in California?

The court filing fee is only the start: under the 2026 statewide civil fee schedule, the first paper in an unlimited civil case is $435 for the plaintiff and $435 for each answering defendant. Attorney fees are the real cost, often $15,000 to $40,000 per side for a moderately contested case and more for a full trial, plus referee, appraiser, title and broker costs that can come out of the sale proceeds.

Can a California court divide the property instead of selling it?

The statute starts with division. Section 872.810 directs the court to divide property by interest, and Section 872.820 orders a sale when the parties agree or when a sale would be more equitable. For cases under the Partition of Real Property Act, Section 874.318 requires partition in kind unless it would cause great prejudice to the owners as a group. A single-family house can rarely be divided, so it usually ends in a sale, but larger parcels sometimes are split.

Should I sell my share to an investor instead of going to court in California?

Sometimes. Selling your fractional interest gets you out quickly and leaves the dispute to the buyer, but a share sells at a discount to its slice of the whole-house value because the buyer inherits the lawsuit, the lis pendens and the cost of partition. If your co-owner can buy you out at an appraised value, or you can cooperate on a listing, you will usually net more. A share sale makes sense when you need money now or can't afford 12 to 24 months of litigation.
