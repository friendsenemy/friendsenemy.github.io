In Ohio, the House Decides How Fast You Can Divorce

A dissolution can end an Ohio marriage in 30 to 90 days, but only if you've already agreed on the house. And thanks to dower, a spouse whose name was never on the deed still has to sign. Here's how those two Ohio rules shape every option, what each one nets on a Columbus-area house, and when selling to a cash buyer is the wrong call.

Most states have one way out of a marriage. Ohio has two, and the family home is often what sorts couples into one or the other.

The first is a divorce: one spouse sues the other, and if they can't settle, a judge decides. The second is a dissolution: both spouses file together, and there is nothing left for a judge to decide. Ohio law requires a dissolution petition to come with a separation agreement that already provides for "a division of all property." That means the house. Not "we'll figure out the house later." The house, resolved, on paper, before anyone files.

So the first question for an Ohio couple isn't what the home is worth. It's whether you two can answer one sentence together: who ends up with it, or how does it get sold? Your answer picks the track, the timeline, the legal bill and how much leverage either of you has left.

🚪 Dissolution or Divorce: Settle the House First or Fight About It Later

A dissolution under Ohio Revised Code § 3105.63 runs on agreement. The two of you sign a separation agreement covering property, spousal support and, if you have minor children, parenting and child support. You file jointly. Under § 3105.64, the court holds the final hearing 30 to 90 days after the petition is filed. Both of you attend, and the case usually finishes that day. In Franklin County, at least one spouse needs six months of Ohio residency and 90 days in the county before filing.

A divorce under § 3105.01 runs on grounds. The ones people use most are incompatibility, unless the other spouse denies it, and living separate and apart for a full year without interruption. A contested divorce gets temporary orders, financial disclosures, discovery, and months of waiting. Plenty of them settle, but the settling happens after the retainers are spent.

Here's what makes the house so important. Agreeing on a dissolution doesn't mean the house has to be gone by the hearing. It means the agreement has to say what happens. Four kinds of language commonly work:

  • 🏠 One spouse keeps it. The agreement sets a refinance deadline, a buyout price and the date of the deed.
  • 🪧 List it. The agreement names who picks the agent, the starting price, who approves price cuts and how the check gets split.
  • 💵 Sell as-is now. The agreement says a sale will close by a date, or attaches a contract you've already signed.
  • 🗓️ Sell later. One spouse stays until a child graduates or until a set date, then the house goes on the market.

Any of those satisfies the "all property" requirement. "To be determined" doesn't, and a vague clause just moves the fight to a later motion. Spell out who pays the mortgage, taxes and insurance until closing, because that's the term spouses most often stop honoring.

The escape hatch cuts both ways: a dissolution isn't final until the judge grants it. If either spouse tells the court at the hearing that they're no longer satisfied, § 3105.65 requires the court to dismiss the petition, unless someone moves to convert the case to a divorce. A signed separation agreement doesn't lock in the deal on the house until the decree does. If you've got a buyer under contract, have your lawyer plan for what happens if your spouse backs out.

🖊️ Dower: The Spouse Who Isn't on the Deed Still Has to Sign

This is the Ohio rule that catches people. Nearly all states have abolished dower. Ohio kept it. Under § 2103.02, a married person has a potential life estate in one-third of the real property their spouse owned during the marriage. It's usually invisible until a house gets sold.

You bought the house two years before you married, the deed has only your name on it, and you think you can sell it without asking. In practice, you can't, at least not cleanly. A deed signed only by you leaves your spouse's dower unreleased, and the statute says dower survives in property conveyed during the marriage unless the spouse relinquished it. A title company won't insure over that, and without title insurance a retail buyer's lender won't close. So the non-owner spouse signs the deed to release dower, even though they aren't an owner.

Three things follow during a divorce:

  • ⛔ Dower gives the off-deed spouse practical veto power. Until the marriage ends or a court steps in, a voluntary sale needs their signature.
  • ⚖️ Dower doesn't decide who gets the money. It's a release signature, not a one-third share of the proceeds. The division of equity comes from the settlement or court order.
  • 🧾 The decree ends it. Section 2103.02 says dower terminates on an absolute divorce, and § 3105.65 gives a dissolution decree the same effect on property rights, dower included. After the final decree, the title examiner will want to see it, but the former spouse's dower is gone.

That last point changes the calendar. If your spouse won't cooperate and your name alone is on the deed, finishing the case may be cleaner than fighting about a mid-case signature. A judge can also step in: § 3105.171(J) lets the court order the sale of property and direct how the money gets applied.

If your home is titled in both names, you already need both signatures, and dower adds nothing. A divorcing couple usually shouldn't use a partition suit, because the domestic relations court is already handling the property.

⏱️ Timing a Closing Around the 30-to-90-Day Window

Ohio's short dissolution window creates a planning problem that slower states don't have. A typical retail listing takes longer than a dissolution does: time to clear out, photograph and price the house, then days on market, then a 30-to-45-day financed closing. You can realistically run out of case before you run out of listing.

That's rarely a real problem, because the decree doesn't require the sale to be finished. Your options:

  1. Sell first, then file. Close before you file the petition, hold the proceeds in escrow or split them, and describe the result in the separation agreement. It's the simplest path when you get along well enough to sell a house together. Both of you still sign, so dower isn't an issue.
  2. File, and sell after the decree. The agreement sets the sale terms, and the decree makes them enforceable through the court. This is the most common path, and it leaves dower behind.
  3. Close between filing and hearing. It works, especially with a cash sale that can land inside the window. Your attorneys need to amend or update the agreement so it says what actually happened to the money.

In a divorce, check the restraining order before you touch anything. Franklin County's Local Rule 43 provides for a standard mutual temporary restraining order, signed by the filing spouse, served with the case and in effect until the court changes it or a final judgment is filed. Other Ohio counties have their own versions. Orders of this kind commonly limit selling or borrowing against property without agreement or court permission. Read your own order's text, and if you want to sell mid-case, have your lawyer get a written agreed entry first.

⚖️ When You Can't Agree, Ohio Starts at Equal

If you end up in a contested divorce, § 3105.171(C)(1) says the division of marital property "shall be equal," unless equal would be inequitable. That's a different starting line from the many equitable-distribution states, where a judge begins with a list of factors and no presumption. In Ohio, the party arguing for something other than half carries that argument uphill.

The equal rule applies only to marital property, so the real fight is usually about what counts:

  • 🏷️ Premarital equity. Property a spouse acquired before the wedding is separate property under § 3105.171(A)(6), along with passive appreciation on it. If you bought the house before marriage, the down payment and market-driven growth may stay yours.
  • 🎁 Inheritances and gifts. An inheritance is separate. A gift to one spouse is separate only if proven by clear and convincing evidence, a tough standard when parents gave money for a down payment and nobody wrote anything down.
  • 🔍 Tracing. Mixing separate money with marital money doesn't automatically convert it, but the statute protects it only if it can be traced. Old closing statements and bank records are the whole case.
  • 🔨 Marital paydown and improvements. Mortgage payments made with wages during the marriage and active appreciation from a kitchen remodel can create a marital share even in a separate house.

The court can also tilt the numbers for misconduct. Under § 3105.171(E)(4), a spouse who dissipated, hid or fraudulently disposed of assets can be hit with a distributive award or a larger share to the other side. If you're tempted to take out a loan against the house or quietly let it slide into default, don't.

If a case truly stalls, the judge can order a sale. Court-ordered sales are slow and rarely bring the best price. Neither spouse has much reason to maintain the house while the order is pending, which is why even couples who dislike each other usually do better negotiating the sale terms themselves.

🧭 Four Ways Out, Honestly Ranked

🪧 List it on the open market

Best for most sound houses

If the house shows well and you can both cooperate for a few months, list it. On a decent house in Clintonville, Westerville or Hilliard, the open market almost always beats a cash offer, even after commission. If that's you, you don't need us. Write the listing terms into your separation agreement and go.

🔑 One spouse buys the other out

Best if the payment works alone

The spouse keeping the house usually needs to refinance to remove the other from the loan. Run the payment at today's rates, on one income, before you agree. A quitclaim deed alone doesn't take anyone off a mortgage. The spouse who deeds out without being refinanced off still owes the lender.

💵 Sell as-is to a cash buyer

Best when condition or conflict blocks a listing

This fits a house with a bad roof or wet basement that won't pass a lender's inspection, a spouse who won't allow showings, payments that have stopped, or a settlement that needs a firm closing date. You give up price in exchange for certainty. We buy these, and we'll tell you if your house isn't one.

🧑‍⚖️ Let the judge order it

Last resort

It's slower and more expensive, and the terms are out of your hands. It's still the right tool if your spouse is stonewalling or there's a safety issue. Don't put off that motion just because it feels aggressive.

💵 Worked Example: A Columbus-Area House, Three Ways

Take a house near Columbus worth $320,000 fully fixed up, with $25,000 of work needed (roof and basement), and a $190,000 mortgage payoff. Assume a Franklin County closing. The county's conveyance fee is $3 per $1,000 of the price, or $1 per $1,000 if the seller has the homestead exemption, plus a flat 50-cent transfer tax.

  • 📈 Fix and list. $320,000, minus $25,000 of repairs, minus 6% in commissions and about $3,000 in closing costs. Add four months of carrying costs at about $2,350 (below). Roughly $73,000 left to split after the payoff, if someone can front the repairs and nothing goes wrong.
  • 🪧 List as-is. At $285,000, with commissions, closing costs and three months of carrying. Roughly $68,000 left to split. A smaller buyer pool, but no repair risk and nobody has to find $25,000, so it may be the smartest of the three.
  • 💵 Cash sale. Cash buyers typically pay about 60-80% of repaired value. At 70%, that's $224,000. With a $700 conveyance fee, no commission and a month of carrying costs: roughly $31,000 to split.

In that example, the cash path costs about $37,000 compared with listing as-is. That's a real price. Paying it makes sense when the listing can't actually happen: payments are behind, one spouse won't let anyone in, or the house can't get past a lender. If none of that applies, list it.

🏚️ What Waiting Costs Each Month

While you decide, the house keeps billing you. Franklin County's effective property tax rate runs about 2% of market value, so on a $285,000 house expect about $475 a month. A rough monthly budget:

  • 💳 Mortgage principal, interest and escrow beyond taxes and insurance: $1,350
  • 🏛️ Property tax: $475
  • 🛡️ Insurance: $150, or $200-$300 if the house goes vacant and you need a vacancy policy
  • 💡 Utilities and upkeep: $375

That's about $2,350 a month. Who pays it needs to be in writing. Tell your insurer if the house goes empty, because standard homeowners policies can limit coverage on vacant homes. 📋 What To Do This Week

  1. Pull the deed. Your county auditor's website shows who holds title. If only one of you is on it, plan for a dower signature.
  2. Get the payoff amount. Request a written payoff statement, plus any second mortgage or home equity line.
  3. Get an honest value. A local agent's as-is estimate works, and a pre-listing appraisal is better if you're heading toward a buyout.
  4. Try the one-sentence test. Can you both agree who ends up with the house, or how it's sold? If yes, price a dissolution. If no, talk to a lawyer about a divorce.
  5. Gather tracing records. Find the closing statement, gift letters, inheritance paperwork and remodel receipts.
  6. Keep paying. Agree on who's paying what until the sale, in writing, and keep the mortgage current if you can.
  7. Don't sign a quitclaim yet. Don't sign anything transferring your interest until a lawyer has confirmed what it does to your loan liability and your share.

✅ Bottom Line

In Ohio, settling the house isn't a side issue; it's the entrance fee for the fast track. If you can agree, a dissolution can be over in 30 to 90 days for a fraction of what a fight costs. If you can't, Ohio starts at an equal split of marital property, and the argument moves to what counts as marital. Either way, remember dower: if you're married and on the deed alone, your spouse's signature, or a final decree, stands between you and a clean sale. Sell on the open market if the house and the relationship can take it. Call on a cash buyer only when one of them can't.

❓ Frequently Asked Questions

Can we get a dissolution in Ohio if we haven't sold the house yet?

Yes. The separation agreement must provide for a division of all property, but that can mean a plan to sell, with terms for listing, price, deadlines and how the money gets split. The sale doesn't have to close before the hearing.

How long does a dissolution take in Ohio?

Ohio Revised Code § 3105.64 requires the hearing 30 to 90 days after the petition is filed. Both spouses must attend, and if the court approves the agreement, the dissolution is usually final that day.

Does my spouse have to sign if the Ohio house is only in my name?

Almost always, if you're still married. Ohio keeps dower under § 2103.02. Your spouse signs the deed to release it, even without being an owner. A final divorce or dissolution decree ends dower.

Does dower give my spouse one-third of the sale money?

No. Dower is a potential life estate in one-third of the property that normally matters only at death. In a sale during the marriage, it's a right that has to be released, not a one-third payout. The equity is divided by your agreement or court order.

What if my spouse changes their mind before the dissolution hearing?

If either spouse is unsatisfied at the hearing, the court must dismiss the petition unless someone moves to convert it to a divorce under § 3105.65. Conversion carries no extra court fee, but the case then proceeds as a divorce, and any sale terms are back on the table.

Is the equity in our Ohio house split 50/50?

Marital equity starts at equal under § 3105.171(C)(1), unless equal would be inequitable. Separate property, like traceable premarital equity or an inheritance, stays with the spouse who owns it, so the split of the whole house may not be 50/50.

Can I sell the house during an Ohio divorce without a court order?

You can if both spouses agree and any restraining order allows it. Many Ohio courts issue standard mutual restraining orders at filing, such as those under Franklin County Local Rule 43. Read the order and get an agreed entry if it limits a sale.

Can an Ohio judge force us to sell the house?

Yes. Section 3105.171(J) lets the court order the sale of real property and direct how the proceeds are used. The court can also let one spouse live in the marital home for a reasonable period.

What does it cost to file for dissolution or divorce in Franklin County?

The Franklin County Clerk's fee schedule lists a $225 deposit for a dissolution and $275 for a divorce, not counting service costs. Attorney fees are the far bigger number, especially in a contested case.

Should we sell our Ohio house to a cash buyer during divorce?

Only if a normal sale won't work, because the house can't pass inspection, one spouse blocks showings, payments have stopped or a deadline is near. On a sound house with two cooperative spouses, listing usually nets thousands more.
