Inherited a Baltimore Rowhouse? Check Two Things Before You Price It: Ground Rent and a Vacant Building Notice

Most American cities have nothing like them. A Baltimore house can sit on land someone else owns, and it can carry a city violation that lets a buyer walk away from the contract. Neither shows up on a tax bill the way you'd expect, and either one can change what your inherited house is worth and who can buy it. Here's how to find out, what each one costs to clear, and when a cash buyer is the wrong choice.

By Dustin Ray, Founder of Pages of Purpose LLC. Not legal advice — laws differ by state, and Maryland statutes and Baltimore City code enforcement both change. Talk to an attorney licensed where the property is, which here means Maryland, before you act on any of this.

Picture the usual Baltimore inheritance. An aunt or a grandfather owned a brick rowhouse in Edmondson Village, Pigtown, Waverly or Highlandtown for forty years. The deed is in a box somewhere. The heating oil ran out two winters ago, and the neighbors have your number because someone found it on a piece of mail. You've been told the house is worth "something" and that you should sell it.

Before anyone gives you a price, two Baltimore questions need answers. Does the house sit on a ground rent? And has the city issued a Vacant Building Notice? In most of the country a buyer would never ask either one. In Baltimore they decide what you actually own, how a title company writes the deed, and whether a retail buyer's lender will touch the house at all.

🧱 Ground Rent: You May Own the House but Not the Dirt

A ground rent is a very old Maryland arrangement. Someone keeps ownership of the land and leases it for 99 years, renewable forever, to whoever owns the building. The rent is small, often $60 to $150 a year, and it's paid once or twice a year. Your relative may have paid it for decades without thinking about it, or may have stopped years ago. Registration figures reported to the Maryland General Assembly in 2020 showed about 92,000 registered ground rents statewide, and more than 62,000 of them were in Baltimore City.

Three things follow if your inherited house has one:

  • What you inherited is a leasehold. Maryland treats a ground lease interest as personal property rather than real property. It still gets sold and insured, but the deed has to describe it correctly. Title companies warn that conveying the wrong interest, fee simple where there's really a leasehold, causes serious trouble later.
  • Back rent is limited but real. A holder who has registered can generally recover up to three years of past-due rent, plus late fees, interest and collection costs if the lease allows them.
  • The old threat is still alive. The legislature tried to abolish ejectment in 2007. In State v. Goldberg (2014), Maryland's top court struck that down, and 2015 legislation now lets a registered holder sue for possession in circuit court after the required notice and publication. It's rare, and it's preventable, but a house that has gone years with unpaid ground rent should not simply sit.

🔎 How to check, and how to get rid of it

Start with the Maryland State Department of Assessments and Taxation (SDAT) real property search and its ground rent registry. No new residential ground rents could be created after January 22, 2007, and a ground rent that isn't registered with SDAT can't be enforced by the holder, meaning no collecting and no lawsuit. But an unregistered ground rent doesn't simply vanish. In Muskin (2011) the court threw out the automatic wipe-out of unregistered ground rents, so a title company will still care. The old deed in the box may also say "subject to the payment of the annual rent of $96."

You can usually buy the ground rent out, which is called redeeming it. Under Real Property § 8-804, a reversion reserved for more than 15 years can generally be redeemed after 30 days' notice. The price is the annual rent divided by a rate set by the lease's date: 6% for most Baltimore leases, 12% for leases created after July 1, 1982, and 4% for a short 1880s window. So a $96 rent costs about $1,600 to redeem at 6%, and a $120 rent about $2,000. Add back rent and paperwork costs. Some older leases are treated differently, so have the title company confirm.

Why this matters for your price: a ground rent almost never kills a sale. A title agent can redeem it at closing, and when a holder can't be found, agents often hold back money in escrow, commonly $500 plus three years of rent. But a buyer who finds it late will reprice or delay. Finding it first costs you a few minutes on SDAT's site.

🏚️ The Vacant Building Notice Is a Bigger Number

A Vacant Building Notice, or VBN, is a Baltimore City code violation placed on a property that's vacant, abandoned and uninhabitable. It's not the same as a house simply being empty. An inherited house can sit unoccupied without one. But a rear roof failure, an open back door, or a long stretch of nobody answering inspectors can get a house cited, and an estate with no one in charge is exactly where those things happen.

Here's what makes a VBN different from an ordinary housing violation:

  • It has to be disclosed in the contract. Under Council Bill 21-0078, passed in 2021, a seller must disclose a VBN in the contract of sale. Leaving it out carries fines and penalties, and the buyer can walk away from a contract if a VBN remains to be abated.
  • It doesn't come off by itself. Clearing it takes proper building permits, the work, and a final use and occupancy permit. The notice stays until the violation is corrected and any fines are paid. The city's housing department describes a $1,000 fine for failing to remove one.
  • It follows the house. Baltimore's housing department warns buyers that a house can look fully rehabbed and still carry a VBN. Unresolved violations can lead to citations and, eventually, tax sale.
  • It can end in receivership. The city can pursue a vacant building receiver. Once that case's notice is filed with the Bureau of Liens, the property can't be transferred without the Building Official's approval, and a court-supervised sale can follow.

To check, search the address on CoDeMap, the city's public map, and ask the title company for the lien sheet. If an inspector's notice has been sitting at the house through the mail slot, open it. The clock in that notice doesn't care that the owner died.

The practical result is simple. A house with an open VBN is usually a contractor's or investor's purchase, because most retail buyers can't finance it and many won't risk it. That's the clearest case where a cash sale isn't a fallback. It may be the realistic market.

🧓 Whose House Is It? Getting Authority to Sign

Ground rent and a VBN tell you what's being sold. You still need someone with legal power to sell it. In Maryland, a personal representative appointed by the Register of Wills or the Orphans' Court for Baltimore City signs for the estate, and our guide to what happens when there's no will covers who inherits. If your name is on the deed with the person who died as joint tenants, or if it was a tenancy by the entirety with a spouse, the house may have passed outside probate. Pull the recorded deed from the Maryland Land Records site to see which you have.

Baltimore has another common wrinkle: houses still titled to someone who died long ago, with no estate ever opened, and now several generations of heirs. You'll need to open the old estate, sometimes more than one, before a clean deed is possible. If the relatives can't agree on any of this, start with our guide for heirs who can't agree. Maryland's own rules and timelines are in our statewide inherited property guide.

💸 The Maryland Tax That Surprises Nieces and Nephews

Maryland is one of very few states that still has an inheritance tax, and it lands hard on exactly the kind of family arrangement Baltimore produces: the house that goes to a niece, a nephew, a cousin or a longtime friend.

The rate is 10% on property passing to anyone who isn't exempt. The Register of Wills' guide lists the exempt people as spouses, children and other lineal descendants, their spouses, parents, grandparents, stepparents, stepchildren and siblings. Domestic partners are also exempt. So a son pays nothing, a brother pays nothing, and a niece may owe 10% of the clear value of what she receives. On a $150,000 house, that's in the neighborhood of $15,000 before any allowed deductions. The same guide lists property in a small estate proceeding among the exempt transfers, which can matter on a very low-value rowhouse.

One more tax point catches out-of-state heirs. When a nonresident sells Maryland real estate, the settlement agent generally has to withhold state income tax at closing. Since July 1, 2025, the Comptroller's rate is 8.75% for individuals and 8.25% for entities. Inherited property generally gets a stepped-up basis, so there may be little actual tax, but you get the money back by filing, not at the closing table. A CPA can tell you whether an exemption fits.

🔀 Your Options, Rated Plainly

List with a Baltimore agent

Best price on a livable house

If the house has heat, a sound roof and no VBN, list it. Baltimore has real owner-occupant demand for houses in decent shape, and you'll very likely net more than a cash offer. Clear up any ground rent before photos go online so it doesn't surface under contract.

Rehab it, then sell

Only with real money and permits

The city's warnings about unpermitted rehabs exist for a reason. If you fix a VBN property, pull permits and get the use and occupancy permit, or you may be selling a nice-looking problem. It can pay well, but it's a construction project with a tax meter running, and every heir has to accept the risk.

One family member keeps it

Often overlooked, can be the kindest answer

If a cousin wants to live there, a buyout at an appraised value avoids commissions. Check the inheritance tax position first, and have the deed drawn so any ground rent and estate authority are handled correctly.

Sell as-is for cash

Best when the problems are the point

When there's a VBN, fire or water damage, a tangled title, or heirs in other states who want this finished, a cash buyer can close in a way a financed buyer can't. You'll give up price for that, and on a good house you shouldn't.

💵 A Worked Example: A Two-Story Rowhouse With Both Problems

Say your uncle's two-story rowhouse would sell for $210,000 fully repaired. It needs a roof, a furnace, plumbing and kitchen work, around $55,000 by a contractor's estimate. It has a $96 ground rent nobody paid for four years, and a recent VBN. Cash buyers generally work from about 60% to 80% of repaired value, which here means roughly $126,000 to $168,000, and the VBN pushes toward the lower half. An offer of $140,000 would be a realistic example, not a promise.

Now the costs of holding it, based on the current Baltimore City rate of $2.248 per $100 of assessed value, plus the State's 11.2 cents:

  • Property tax: on a $130,000 assessment, about $3,070 a year, or $255 a month.
  • Vacant-home insurance: often $100 to $200 a month. An ordinary owner-occupied policy may lapse in coverage once nobody lives there.
  • Utilities and winter protection: $75 to $175 a month.
  • Grass, boarding, trash and upkeep: $75 to $150 a month, more if citations start.

That's roughly $500 to $775 a month. Over a year of rehab and sale, carrying costs alone come to $6,000 to $9,300, on top of the $55,000. Settlement costs matter too. Baltimore City transfer tax is 1.5%, state transfer tax is 0.5%, and recordation is $5 per $500, about 3% in total, and who pays what is negotiated in the contract. If the fixed-up house brings $210,000 and you pay a 5% commission, you might clear about $135,000 after repairs and holding costs, with all the risk and a year of your life. That's close to the cash number. If the repairs were $15,000 rather than $55,000, the answer flips and listing wins easily. Run your own version before you take anyone's offer, including ours.

🙅 When You Shouldn't Sell to a Cash Buyer

Don't take a cash offer just because the house feels overwhelming. If there's no VBN, the systems work, and the heirs agree, a little cleaning and an honest agent will likely put more in your pocket. Be wary of anyone who wants a deed signed before an estate is open, who offers to "take care of" a VBN without saying how, or who asks an heir to sign away a share for a small check. If you think the offer is low, get a second one. A real buyer won't mind.

📋 What To Do This Week

  1. Search the address on SDAT's real property site and ground rent registry, and write down any holder's name and annual rent.
  2. Look up the address on CoDeMap for a Vacant Building Notice or other open violations.
  3. Find or pull the recorded deed to see how title was held and whether it mentions an annual rent.
  4. Contact the Register of Wills for Baltimore City about opening the estate if nobody has.
  5. Call the insurer, say that the owner has died and the house is empty, and ask what coverage survives.
  6. Secure the house, shut off water if it's unheated, and collect mail, especially anything from the city.
  7. Check whether any heir is outside the lineal and sibling exemptions before dividing money.

✅ Bottom Line

An inherited Baltimore house has a question most inherited houses don't: what exactly was left to you? Check for ground rent and a Vacant Building Notice before you think about price. A ground rent is usually a few thousand dollars to clear and shouldn't stop an ordinary sale. A VBN often decides your buyer pool. If the house is sound and the family agrees, list it. If it carries a VBN, heavy damage or a title nobody has touched in decades, get a real repair estimate and a written cash offer and compare them honestly.

❓ Frequently Asked Questions

How do I find out if my inherited Baltimore house has a ground rent?

Search the address on the Maryland SDAT real property site and its ground rent registry, then read the recorded deed for words like subject to the annual rent. A title company will confirm it during a title search. Ground rents can exist even when nobody has collected in years, so a lack of bills doesn't prove there isn't one.

How much does it cost to redeem a ground rent in Baltimore?

The usual price is the annual rent divided by a rate tied to the lease date: 6% for most older Baltimore leases and 12% for leases made after July 1, 1982. A $120 rent at 6% is about $2,000. Add any back rent the holder can recover, generally up to three years, and processing costs.

Can I lose an inherited house over unpaid ground rent?

It's possible but uncommon. Maryland's highest court ruled in 2014 that the state could not abolish ejectment, and current law lets a registered holder seek possession in circuit court after notice. Back rent is generally limited to three years. If you've inherited a house with unpaid ground rent, deal with it promptly rather than waiting.

What is a Vacant Building Notice in Baltimore?

It's a city code violation placed on a property that is vacant, abandoned and uninhabitable. It lists the work needed to meet code and stays with the property until the work is permitted and finished, a use and occupancy permit is issued, and any fines are paid. A house being merely unoccupied doesn't automatically have one.

Can I sell an inherited Baltimore house that has a Vacant Building Notice?

Generally yes, but you must disclose it in the contract of sale under a 2021 city law, and a buyer can walk away while it remains unabated. Most sales of VBN houses go to cash investors or contractors. If a vacant building receivership case has been filed, transfer needs the Building Official's approval, so check first.

Does a niece or nephew pay Maryland inheritance tax on a house?

Often yes. Maryland charges 10% on property passing to anyone outside the exempt list, and nieces, nephews, cousins and friends are not on it. Spouses, children and other descendants, parents, grandparents, stepchildren and siblings are exempt. The tax is calculated on clear value, so ask the Register of Wills or an attorney how deductions apply.

Do I have to fix code violations before selling an inherited house in Baltimore?

Not usually, as long as they are disclosed and the contract says who will deal with them. Many estates sell as-is and let the buyer take the violations on. The city expects whoever owns the house to address them, so make sure the contract and settlement papers are clear.

I live in another state. What happens at closing on my inherited Maryland house?

You can usually sign remotely or by mail once authority is in place. As a nonresident seller, expect the settlement agent to withhold Maryland income tax, currently 8.75% for individuals, unless an exemption applies. You'd generally recover any excess by filing a Maryland nonresident return. Check this with a CPA before closing.

The house was in my grandmother's name and she died years ago. Can it still be sold?

Yes, but somebody must open her estate first, and if any heirs have since died, their estates may matter too. There's no time limit that simply cancels the family's rights. Baltimore has many older titles like this, and sorting them out is slow rather than impossible. A Maryland estates attorney can map the heirs.

Should I list my inherited Baltimore house or sell it for cash?

With a sound structure and no VBN, listing will usually net more. If it carries a VBN, has major damage, or needs heirs scattered across states to finish quickly, compare a contractor's estimate with a written cash offer. Cash buyers typically pay about 60% to 80% of repaired value.
