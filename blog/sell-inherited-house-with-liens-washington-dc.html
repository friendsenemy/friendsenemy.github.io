Inherited a DC House With Liens? Watch the Vacant-Property Tax Before Anything Else

The District taxes an empty house at up to twelve times the normal residential rate, and that tax becomes a lien ahead of the mortgage. Here is how DC's vacant classes, water liens, tax lien sale and Probate Division fit together, what an heir should file first, and when listing beats a cash sale. Or just ask us a question, free.

By Dustin Ray, Founder of Pages of Purpose LLC. Not legal advice — laws differ by state, and District of Columbia tax rates, vacant-building rules and probate practice change. Talk to an attorney licensed where the property is, which here means the District of Columbia, before you act on any of this.

Here is the situation we hear about from the District more than any other. A mother, an uncle or a grandmother owned a rowhouse in Brightwood, Congress Heights, Deanwood, Petworth or Trinidad for decades. She died. Nobody moved in. Months went by while relatives argued over who should handle things. Meanwhile the water bill went unpaid, the second-half real property tax bill came and went, and an old credit-card judgment that nobody knew about was sitting in the land records the whole time.

Every city has inherited houses with liens. What makes Washington different is that the District taxes an empty house far more heavily than an occupied one, and the bill can quietly turn into the largest lien on the property. A family that waits a year to "figure things out" can owe more in vacant-property tax than in all the other debts combined. The good news is that DC law also gives heirs a specific shield against that tax, and most families never use it because nobody tells them it exists.

🏚️ An Empty Inherited House in DC Can Be Taxed at Six to Twelve Times the Normal Rate

The Office of Tax and Revenue (OTR) taxes ordinary homes as Class 1 residential property at $0.85 per $100 of assessed value. But the District has two penalty classes that most of its neighbors do not. A building the Department of Buildings (DOB) classifies as vacant is taxed as Class 3 at $5.00 per $100. A building DOB classifies as blighted is taxed as Class 4 at $10.00 per $100.

Run those rates on a typical inherited rowhouse assessed at $430,000:

  • 🏠 Class 1 (lived in): about $3,655 a year, roughly $305 a month.
  • 🚪 Class 3 (vacant): about $21,500 a year, roughly $1,790 a month.
  • 🧱 Class 4 (blighted): about $43,000 a year, roughly $3,580 a month.

Those are not fines that go away when you sell. Unpaid real property tax is a lien on the house, it sits ahead of the mortgage, and it gets paid first out of your sale proceeds. If DOB inspects a house that has been sitting dark since the funeral, notices boarded windows, overgrown yard or a broken gutter, and reclassifies it, the estate can be paying a mortgage's worth of tax every month on a house nobody is using.

Classification is DOB's call, not OTR's. OTR's own rate page says questions about Class 3 or Class 4 status go to DOB, and DOB publishes a public Vacant Properties Map you can check by address. If the house you inherited is already on that map, everything else in this guide becomes more urgent.

⚠️ The most important warning on this page: do not assume a house is safe from the vacant rate because "it's just between owners." DC's vacant-building law counts the building, not the reason it's empty. The protection for heirs is real, but you have to claim it, and it only starts when a probate case is actually filed.

🛡️ The Probate Exemption Most DC Families Never Claim

The District's vacant-building statute, D.C. Code § 42-3131.06a, lists the situations in which an empty building is not charged the higher vacant rate. One of them is written almost exactly for inherited houses: a building that is "the subject of a probate proceeding or the title is the subject of litigation" is exempt from the increased rate for up to 3 tax years. The statute also says the exemption's starting year is fixed by the filing date of the probate case or the lawsuit.

Read that carefully, because it rewards the families who move first. The exemption is tied to a filed case. Heirs who spend a year deciding whether to open probate are not protected during that year. Heirs who file with the Probate Division right away and then take their time selling have up to three tax years of cover from the vacant rate, as long as DOB accepts the exemption.

Two other exemptions in the same section can matter for an inherited house:

  • 🔨 Active renovation. A building under active construction or rehabilitation with a valid building permit can be exempt for up to 3 tax years. That helps an heir who plans to fix the house and move in or rent it.
  • 📝 Pending permit. If the owner has a building permit application in process to make the house fit to live in, the exemption runs for up to half a tax year.

There is also a hardship exemption the Mayor can grant in extraordinary circumstances. Don't plan around it. The probate exemption is the one built for families, and DOB handles the paperwork for all of these, not the court and not OTR. Ask your probate attorney to file the exemption with DOB the same month the case opens.

🧮 The Lien Stack on a DC House, Ranked by Who Gets Paid First

When an inherited DC house sells, the settlement company pulls a title report and pays every recorded lien from the proceeds before the heirs see a dollar. The order matters, because it tells you which debts are certain to be paid in full and which ones a buyer's title company will insist on clearing before closing.

  1. District real property taxes. Always first. This includes any Class 3 or Class 4 vacant-rate tax.
  2. DC water and sewer charges. D.C. Code § 34-2407.02 makes a recorded certificate of delinquency for unpaid water and sewer bills a lien with priority over every other lien except District taxes. That means an unpaid DC Water bill outranks the mortgage.
  3. Mortgages and home equity lines, generally in the order they were recorded with the Recorder of Deeds.
  4. Judgment liens. Under D.C. Code § 15-102, a money judgment from DC Superior Court or the federal district court becomes a lien on the debtor's DC real estate once it is filed and recorded with the Recorder of Deeds, and it lasts as long as the judgment is in force.
  5. Federal tax liens filed by the IRS against the person who died, which a title company will require you to pay off or get discharged before closing.
  6. Unsecured debts such as credit cards and medical bills. These are not liens on the house unless a creditor already won a judgment and recorded it. They are claims against the estate, and the personal representative deals with them through probate.

The practical point: if the house is worth more than everything in that list, liens are an accounting problem, not a crisis. They come out of the sale price at closing. Where heirs get into real trouble is when the top two items, District tax and water, keep growing while nobody has legal authority to sell. If heirs also disagree about selling at all, our national guide on what to do when heirs can't agree on an inherited house covers the family side of that standoff.

💧 DC Water's Lien Loses Its Owner-Occupant Shield After a Death

The water-lien statute has a protection that sounds reassuring: the District will not sell the residence of an owner who occupies a single-family home for unpaid water and sewer charges. While your parent was alive and living there, that shield applied.

Once the house is empty, it doesn't. Nobody occupies it, so nothing in that subsection stops enforcement. The statute lets the District file a certificate of delinquency once bills are 60 days unpaid and enforce the lien once charges are more than 180 days unpaid, using the same tax-sale procedure it uses for unpaid property taxes. A house sold at tax sale for water charges that isn't redeemed within 180 days, with 2% interest per month, can end with a deed going to the purchaser.

What to do about it is simple. Get the DC Water account number off an old bill, ask for the balance, and keep the account current, or at least keep it from reaching the 180-day mark. Water is cheap compared to the risk of a tax-sale purchaser getting involved.

📅 The Annual Tax Lien Sale and the Six-Month Fuse

The District sells liens on delinquent properties once a year. OTR held the 2026 annual tax lien sale on August 19 and 20, and it runs over-the-counter and discount sales of unsold liens at other times. According to OTR's 2026 tax sale FAQ:

  • 💲 An improved property goes on the list when it owes at least $2,500 in delinquent tax (the threshold for vacant land is $200).
  • 📈 To redeem, you pay the delinquent amount plus interest of 1.5% per month or part of a month, along with capped costs.
  • ⏳ The lien purchaser can begin a foreclosure of the right of redemption six months after the last day of the sale if the taxes are still unpaid.
  • 🧾 The costs you reimburse are capped, for example up to $300 for a title search and up to $1,500 in attorney fees before the fifth status hearing in the foreclosure case. Those caps keep the cost of fixing a tax sale from spiralling, but they are still real money.

For an empty inherited house, the risky combination is a Class 3 reclassification followed by nobody paying. At $1,790 a month in vacant-rate tax, the $2,500 threshold is crossed in about six weeks. If the property has already been sold at tax sale, tell your attorney and anyone you are talking to about buying the house. Redemption must be part of the closing plan.

OTR's tax sale page also points heirs to two free resources: the District's Real Property Tax Ombudsman and the Heirs Property Assistance Program, a partnership between Legal Counsel for the Elderly and the DC Affordable Law Firm. It offers technical help to DC residents seeking clear title after a family member dies, and need-based legal help for those at or below 80% of the District's median family income. If you qualify, use it before you pay anyone, including us.

⚖️ Getting Authority to Sign: The Probate Division at 515 5th Street NW

A house titled only in your parent's name can't be sold until someone has legal authority to sign the deed. In the District that authority comes from the Probate Division of the Superior Court of the District of Columbia, at 515 5th Street NW. The court appoints a personal representative, publishes notice to creditors, and creditors have six months from the first publication to file claims.

The court's own guide to probate says the process takes a minimum of eight months and often a year or longer. That doesn't mean you have to wait eight months to sell. A personal representative's powers under D.C. Code § 20-741 include powers to sell estate property. The court's guide notes that court approval is sometimes required or prudent, such as when the will leaves the house to a specific person but it has to be sold, when an heir objects to a sale, or when the personal representative wants to buy the house. A DC probate attorney can tell you which of those applies before you sign a contract.

The District's small-estate procedure only covers estates of $80,000 or less (D.C. Code § 20-351). Almost no DC house fits under that number, so an inherited house nearly always means a full estate. If there's no will, see what happens to a house when there's no will for how heirs are identified; in DC the court still has to appoint someone before the house can be sold.

🧾 Why the court case also helps with liens

Opening the estate does three things for a lien problem at once. It starts the probate exemption from the vacant rate. It gives one person the power to talk to OTR, DC Water and the mortgage servicer, who otherwise won't discuss the account with a relative. And it starts the six-month creditor clock, which is how unsecured debts get settled or barred so the house can close cleanly.

📜 Transfer-on-Death Deeds Skip Probate, but Not the Liens

The District has adopted the Uniform Real Property Transfer on Death Act (D.C. Code § 19-604.01 and following). If your parent signed and recorded a transfer-on-death deed before dying, the house can pass to the named beneficiary without a probate case. Check the land records with the Recorder of Deeds before assuming you need probate at all.

Two cautions. First, a transfer-on-death deed passes the house with its mortgage, its tax lien, its water lien and any recorded judgment still attached; the beneficiary inherits the debts recorded against the house along with the house. Second, because no probate case is filed, the probate exemption from the vacant rate is not available. A TOD beneficiary who leaves the house empty is exposed to Class 3 from the start and should either occupy it, start a permitted renovation, or sell promptly.

If the house had a mortgage, federal law (the Garn-St Germain Act) generally prevents the lender from calling the loan due just because the house passed to a relative at death. The payments still have to be made, or the loan will go into default like any other.

🏥 Medicaid Recovery, IRS Liens and Old Judgments

Medicaid. The DC Department of Health Care Finance (DHCF) must seek repayment from the estate of a Medicaid beneficiary who received coverage at age 55 or older. DHCF will not pursue recovery while a surviving spouse lives in the home, or while a child under 21, or a blind or disabled child, lives there, and it offers undue-hardship waivers. If your parent was in a nursing home on Medicaid, assume DHCF will file a claim and plan the closing around it.

IRS liens. A federal tax lien against your parent follows the house. The IRS has a process for a certificate of discharge so a sale can close when the lien is paid from proceeds. Start that early; it can take weeks.

Judgments. The judgment lien from step 4 above is the one families most often miss, because the creditor may have sued years ago and never collected. A title search finds it. Sometimes the creditor will accept less than the full amount to release the lien at closing, especially on an old consumer debt. Your attorney or settlement company can ask.

District estate tax. DC has an estate tax with an exclusion of roughly $5 million, indexed each year. A single rowhouse estate is almost never close to it, but a larger estate with a Capitol Hill house and retirement accounts can be. DC has no inheritance tax on heirs.

🔀 Four Exits, With Honest Verdicts

🏡 Repair, then list on the open market

Most money if the house is sound

DC has deep buyer demand for rowhouses in decent condition. If the roof, systems and structure are fine and the heirs agree, list it. Keep the house out of the vacant classes by opening probate first and pulling permits for any real work. Liens are paid at closing and you keep the difference.

💵 Sell as-is for cash

Best when liens and repairs are both piling up

A cash buyer pays less than a retail buyer would, but it closes once the personal representative has authority, doesn't need lender-ready condition, and works with the title company to pay off District tax, DC Water, judgments and the mortgage from the proceeds. It's the right call when nobody can fund repairs or the vacant rate is already running.

🔑 One heir keeps it

Good if someone truly wants to live there

An heir who moves in turns an empty house into an occupied one, which takes it out of the vacant classes entirely. They will still need to buy out the other heirs and pay or refinance the liens. That works best when there is real equity and the heir can qualify for a loan.

⏸️ Wait and see

The most expensive choice in DC

In most states, waiting costs insurance and upkeep. In the District it can cost the vacant rate, water penalties, 1.5% a month on a tax-sale lien and, eventually, the house. If the family can't decide, at least open probate so the exemption clock starts and someone has authority.

💵 One Ward 7 Rowhouse, Four Liens: The Numbers

Say you inherit a three-bedroom rowhouse that would be worth about $520,000 fully renovated. It needs roughly $80,000 of work: kitchen, bathrooms, an old boiler and some roof. OTR assesses it at $430,000. The title search shows:

  • Mortgage balance: $95,000
  • Unpaid real property tax with penalties: $7,800
  • DC Water certificate of delinquency: $4,600
  • A 2019 consumer judgment recorded with the Recorder of Deeds: $11,200

Total liens: $118,600.

🏷️ Path A: sell as-is for cash

Cash buyers usually pay about 60–80% of repaired value. On a $520,000 house that is $312,000 to $416,000, depending on condition and how hard the title is. Say the offer is $360,000 (about 69%). The District's deed transfer tax on a residential sale under $400,000 is 1.1%, or $3,960, customarily paid by the seller. Subtract the liens and about $5,000 in probate attorney fees: the heirs net about $232,400, in roughly 30–45 days once the personal representative can sign.

🛠️ Path B: renovate and list

Spend $80,000 and four to six months on repairs, list at $520,000, and pay about 5% in commissions ($26,000), the 1.45% transfer tax that applies at $400,000 and above ($7,540), and maybe $5,000 in buyer concessions. Six months of carrying costs at the Class 1 rate come to about $4,500 (tax, vacant-home insurance, utilities, upkeep), and about $2,500 more in mortgage interest and lien interest. After the same $118,600 in liens and $5,000 in attorney fees, the heirs net about $270,900.

That's about $38,000 more for listing, if someone can put up $80,000 and the project stays on schedule. Now change one thing: DOB classifies the house as vacant during renovation, and nobody filed the probate or permit exemption. Six months at Class 3 instead of Class 1 adds roughly $8,900 in tax. Get reclassified as blighted and it's closer to $19,700. The listing path still wins on paper, but by much less, and it carries all the risk.

🧾 What it costs each month to keep it empty

  • Real property tax: about $305 a month at Class 1, about $1,790 at Class 3, about $3,580 at Class 4.
  • Vacant-home insurance: commonly $150–$250 a month, since a standard homeowner's policy may not cover a house left unoccupied.
  • Utilities (heat to protect pipes, power, DC Water base charges): roughly $100–$200 a month.
  • Yard, snow, gutters and inspections: $75–$150 a month.
  • Mortgage payment, if there is one.

🙅 When Listing Beats Selling to Us

We buy inherited DC houses with liens. We'll still tell you not to sell to us if:

  • The house is in good shape and the liens are small compared with the equity. A retail buyer will pay more and the liens come out at closing anyway.
  • Probate is open, the exemption is filed, and the heirs agree on an agent and a price.
  • One heir wants to live there and can refinance. Moving in solves the vacant-rate problem better than any sale.
  • You qualify for the Heirs Property Assistance Program and haven't contacted it yet.

A cash offer makes sense when the house needs a lot of work, the lien total is uncertain or rising, the heirs live far away, or a tax-sale purchaser is already involved. Our guide to why cash offers drop after a title search explains how surprise liens change an offer.

📋 What To Do This Week in DC

  1. Look up the property on DOB's Vacant Properties Map and on OTR's real property tax database to see its current class and any delinquent balance.
  2. Call DC Water with an old bill or the service address, ask for the balance, and pay enough to keep it short of 180 days if you can.
  3. Find the will and check the Recorder of Deeds for a transfer-on-death deed.
  4. Hire a DC probate attorney (or contact the Heirs Property Assistance Program) and file with the Probate Division so the probate exemption and the creditor clock both start.
  5. Ask the attorney to file the vacant-building exemption with DOB as soon as the case number exists.
  6. Order a title search so you know about judgments, IRS liens and any tax-sale certificate before you talk price with anyone.
  7. Secure the house: lock it, keep heat on in winter, cut the grass, and put a vacant-home policy in place.

✅ Bottom Line for DC Heirs

Liens on an inherited Washington house are usually paid out of the sale, so they're rarely the thing that sinks a family. What sinks families in the District is time: an empty house that DOB moves into Class 3 or Class 4, a water lien that loses its owner-occupant protection, and a tax-sale lien charging 1.5% a month. File probate early, claim the vacant-building exemption, keep DC Water current, and get a title search. Then decide, with real numbers, whether to repair and list or sell as-is. If the house is sound and the family agrees, list it. If it isn't, ask us for a written offer and compare.

❓ DC Inherited-House Lien Questions

Will DC tax an inherited empty house at the vacant-property rate?

It can. If the Department of Buildings classifies the house as vacant it is taxed as Class 3 at $5.00 per $100 of assessed value, and as Class 4 at $10.00 per $100 if classified blighted, compared with $0.85 per $100 for an ordinary Class 1 home. The higher tax becomes a lien on the house like any other real property tax.

Does opening probate protect an inherited DC house from the vacant rate?

Usually, if you claim it. D.C. Code § 42-3131.06a exempts a building that is the subject of a probate proceeding from the increased vacant rate for up to 3 tax years, starting from the tax year set by the probate filing date. The exemption is handled by the Department of Buildings, so ask your attorney to file it as soon as the case is opened.

Can the District sell an inherited house at tax sale for unpaid property taxes?

Yes. OTR's 2026 tax sale rules put an improved property on the list once it owes at least $2,500 in delinquent tax. Redeeming costs the delinquent amount plus 1.5% interest per month and capped costs, and the lien buyer can start a foreclosure of the right of redemption six months after the sale if the taxes are still unpaid.

Can DC Water put a lien on my late parent's house?

Yes. Under D.C. Code § 34-2407.02 a certificate of delinquency for water and sewer bills unpaid 60 days or more becomes a lien that outranks every lien except District taxes. The District can't sell an owner-occupied single-family home for water charges, but once the owner has died and the house is empty, that protection no longer fits the facts.

Who gets paid first when an inherited DC house with liens is sold?

District real property taxes first, then DC Water and sewer charges, then mortgages and home equity lines generally in recording order, then recorded judgment liens and federal tax liens. Unsecured debts like credit cards are claims against the estate, not liens on the house, unless a creditor already recorded a judgment.

Can I sell an inherited DC house without opening probate?

Only if the house passed outside probate, for example by a recorded transfer-on-death deed, joint ownership with survivorship, or a trust. If it was titled in your parent's name alone, the Probate Division of DC Superior Court has to appoint a personal representative to sign the deed. The small-estate procedure only covers estates of $80,000 or less, which almost no DC house fits.

Does a DC transfer-on-death deed wipe out the liens?

No. A transfer-on-death deed under the District's Uniform Real Property Transfer on Death Act passes the house without probate, but the mortgage, tax, water and judgment liens stay attached. Because no probate case is filed, the probate exemption from the vacant rate isn't available either, so the beneficiary should occupy, renovate under permit, or sell promptly.

Will DC Medicaid take money from the sale of the house?

It may. DHCF seeks repayment from the estate of a beneficiary who received Medicaid at age 55 or older. It won't pursue recovery while a surviving spouse, a child under 21, or a blind or disabled child lives in the home, and it offers undue-hardship waivers. If your parent was on Medicaid in a nursing home, plan for a claim at closing.

Who pays DC transfer and recordation taxes when we sell?

The District charges a deed transfer tax and a deed recordation tax, each 1.1% of the price on residential sales under $400,000 and 1.45% of the full price at $400,000 or more. By custom the seller usually pays the transfer tax and the buyer the recordation tax, but it can be negotiated in the contract.

Should we list the house or take a cash offer if the liens are bigger than we expected?

If the house is sound and the equity comfortably covers the liens, listing usually nets more because the liens come out of the sale either way. If it needs major work, nobody can fund repairs, or the vacant rate or a tax-sale lien is already running, compare a written cash offer, typically 60% to 80% of repaired value, against the full cost of repairing and waiting.
