Selling a House From a Virginia Jail or Prison: Act Before a Court Appoints a Committee

Virginia has an old and unusual law: when someone is convicted of a felony and sentenced to a state prison, a circuit court can appoint a "committee" to take over their property. Until that happens, the owner keeps full authority and can sign a deed or a power of attorney. That makes the first few weeks the best time to plan a sale. Here's how the committee law works, how to get a POA a Virginia clerk will record, and when listing beats a cash offer. Or ask us a question, free.

When a Virginia family calls us about a house, the person who owns it is often in one of two places: a local or regional jail waiting for trial or transfer, or one of the Virginia Department of Corrections' facilities, which held about 22,900 people in October 2026. Either way, the questions are the same. Can they still sell? Who signs? What happens to the mortgage while they're gone?

They can still sell, and in most Virginia cases the person inside still decides. But Virginia has a statute many families have never heard of: a court-appointed committee for the estate of a person convicted of a felony and sentenced to a state correctional facility. Knowing when a committee can be appointed, and what it changes, tells a family what to do first.

🏛️ Virginia's "Committee" for a Prisoner's Estate

Virginia Code §§ 53.1-221 through 53.1-228 set out the committee system:

  • 📋 Who it applies to. Under § 53.1-221, a person convicted of a felony and sentenced to a state correctional facility. It's not triggered by an arrest, a misdemeanor, or a stay in a local jail awaiting trial.
  • 🙋 Who can ask. "Any party interested" can move the circuit court where the prisoner's estate (or part of it) is located to commit the estate to a person the court selects. If there's no property in Virginia, the court where the offense occurred handles it.
  • 🧾 What the committee is. A fiduciary who posts bond and accounts like other fiduciaries under § 64.2-1200 and following, through the commissioner of accounts. If the person appointed won't serve or can't post bond, § 53.1-226 makes the sheriff the committee.
  • 👨‍👩‍👧 Family support comes first. Under § 53.1-224, the committee must provide maintenance for the prisoner's spouse and family, subject to creditors' claims.
  • 🏠 The house can be sold. Under § 53.1-227, the prisoner's real estate can be leased or sold to pay debts through a court proceeding, or sold, exchanged or encumbered to pay for improvements.
  • 🔙 Release ends it. Under § 53.1-225, within 10 days after release the committee files an accounting and asks to be discharged, and the estate goes back to the owner.

Two other provisions explain why committees still get appointed. Section 53.1-222 lets a committee sue and be sued for the prisoner. And § 53.1-223 says that, with some exceptions, a civil suit against a prisoner can proceed only through a committee, a guardian ad litem or an attorney of record. In a divorce where the court will divide marital property under § 20-107.3, the court must appoint a committee first. So the people most likely to ask for one are a spouse filing for divorce and creditors trying to collect.

⏱️ Until a Committee Exists, the Owner Still Decides

Here's the part that matters most for families. Section 53.1-221 says that until a committee is appointed, the prisoner keeps full capacity and authority over their property. They can sign a deed. They can sign a power of attorney. They can make a will.

That gives the family a clear sequence:

  1. 🖋️ Get a recordable power of attorney signed early, ideally before sentencing or right after, while the owner is in a local jail with easier visits.
  2. 🗣️ Decide together whether to keep, rent or sell, and put the decision in writing so the agent isn't guessing.
  3. ⚖️ If a divorce or a creditor's suit is coming, talk to a Virginia attorney about how a committee appointment would affect the agent's authority and the sale. A committee answers to the circuit court, and a sale the agent is halfway through may need the court's involvement once a committee is in place.

When the owner can't or won't cooperate, the committee route is also the way a family member can get authority. A spouse who needs to sell the family home to keep a roof over the children's heads can ask to be appointed committee, post bond and seek court approval for the sale. It's slower and costs more than a POA, but it works when nothing else will.

The warning that matters most: don't wait for a crisis to get the power of attorney signed. Once someone is transferred into a VADOC facility, every document takes mail time and a notary appointment, and once a committee is appointed the decision may move to a court. A properly drafted POA signed in the first weeks is the cheapest protection a Virginia family has.

🖋️ A Virginia Power of Attorney a Clerk Will Record

Virginia's Uniform Power of Attorney Act is at Va. Code § 64.2-1600 and following. For a house sale, these sections matter:

  • ✍️ § 64.2-1603 (execution). The principal signs, or someone signs the principal's name in the principal's conscious presence at their direction. A signature acknowledged before a notary is presumed genuine.
  • 📁 § 55.1-600 (recording). The circuit court clerk records a writing that has been acknowledged, or proved by two witnesses. Since the deed will be signed by the agent, the clerk will need the POA in recordable form, so notarize it.
  • 🏠 § 64.2-1625 (real property). General authority over real property lets the agent sell, exchange, convey, quitclaim, mortgage and lease.
  • 📝 § 64.2-1624. The agent can execute, acknowledge, deliver and record instruments.
  • 🚫 § 64.2-1622(A). Gifts and changes to survivorship rights need an express grant. If the plan involves deeding the house to a family member for little or nothing, ordinary real estate authority isn't enough.

Write the POA with the property's address and legal description, the agent's authority to sign a purchase contract, a deed and settlement papers, and, if relevant, authority to deal with the mortgage lender. A Virginia settlement agent or closing attorney can tell you what they need before the document goes into the facility.

🏢 Free Notary Service in VADOC Facilities

VADOC Operating Procedure 866.3, "Legal Access" (effective June 1, 2025), requires each facility to make notary services available free of charge, within one working day of the request. The inmate appears in person with an ID card, the notary verifies identity, and the inmate signs a statement that the notary is not a party to the action. The procedure is written around sworn legal documents, so confirm with the facility in advance that its notary will take an acknowledgment on a power of attorney or deed, and ask the settlement agent what notarial wording they need.

Mail is another detail Virginia does its own way. Legal correspondence from attorneys and courts goes to the VADOC Centralized Mail Distribution Center (Inmate Name and Number, 3521 Woods Way, State Farm, VA 23160), where it's scanned and screened before same-day delivery. Have the attorney or settlement agent send the POA as legal mail rather than a relative sending it as personal mail. Local and regional jails set their own notary and mail rules, so call the jail directly. Our jail notary guide covers the general options.

💍 Tenancy by the Entirety: Two Signatures on One Deed

Many Virginia married couples own their home as tenants by the entirety. Under § 55.1-136, an interest held that way can't be severed by a written instrument unless it's a deed signed by both spouses as grantors. In practice, selling an entireties home means both spouses sign, so the spouse at home needs either the incarcerated spouse's signature or a POA from them. Virginia is not a community-property state, so if the house is titled to the incarcerated spouse alone, the other spouse usually doesn't need to sign to convey it, though a title company may still ask.

Entireties ownership also matters for creditors. Under long-standing Virginia case law, property held by the entirety generally can't be reached by creditors of only one spouse. A restitution judgment against one spouse may not attach to an entireties home, but once the house is sold, the proceeds may be treated differently. Ask a Virginia attorney before closing if one spouse has significant judgments. Our guide on selling when your spouse is in jail covers the general situations.

🛡️ Virginia's Homestead Exemption Is Bigger Than People Think

Virginia's homestead exemption in § 34-4 protects $5,000 of property from most creditors ($10,000 for a householder 65 or older), plus up to $50,000 in a principal residence, plus $500 per dependent, with inflation adjustments starting April 1, 2027. That's a creditor exemption, not a property-tax break, and there are rules about how it is claimed. For an incarcerated owner with judgments, it can protect part of the home's value, but it's not automatic protection for sale proceeds. A Virginia attorney can tell you whether and how to claim it.

🧾 Restitution, Court Costs and Judgment Liens at Closing

A Virginia closing pays off recorded liens, and criminal cases often leave several:

  • 📌 Docketed judgments. Under § 8.01-458, a money judgment becomes a lien on real estate once it's docketed in the circuit court where the land sits. For judgments dated on or after July 1, 2021, § 8.01-251 sets a 10-year life with up to two 10-year extensions; older judgments had 20 years.
  • ⚖️ Restitution. Under § 19.2-305.2(B), restitution orders are docketed as judgments, and a victim's enforcement of a docketed restitution order isn't subject to any statute of limitations.
  • 🧮 Court costs and fines. Under § 19.2-341 these are judgments too. The current limits are long; beginning January 1, 2027, the law changes to 10 years from the judgment.
  • 💸 Tax refund set-off. Courts can also collect restitution and costs through Virginia's set-off program, which intercepts state tax refunds.

Run a judgment search in the circuit court for the city or county where the house is before you list. A lien you discover at settlement can wipe out the net proceeds or delay closing for weeks.

⏳ Virginia Foreclosures Move Quickly

Most Virginia home loans use a deed of trust, and the trustee can sell without a lawsuit. Under § 55.1-321, the owner of owner-occupied residential property must get notice by certified or registered mail at least 60 days before the sale (14 days for other property). Under § 55.1-322, if the deed of trust is silent, the sale is advertised once a week for four weeks, and it must take place at least eight days after the first ad and no more than 30 days after the last.

That means a family can go from first missed payment to a trustee's sale in a few months. If payments have stopped, a sale needs to close before the trustee's sale date. Our guide on selling to stop a foreclosure explains the options.

🧭 How Virginia Families Usually Proceed

Keep the house and let the agent manage it

If payments are covered

For a short sentence with a manageable mortgage, keeping the house may be best. The agent under the POA can deal with the lender, insurer and local treasurer, and the family can revisit a sale later without starting the paperwork over.

List it through an agent

Best price for a sound house

A clean, occupied house in a decent market will usually net the most on the open market. The agent under the POA signs the listing, contract and deed, and the settlement agent records the POA with the deed.

Sell to a cash buyer

When the calendar or condition decides it

When a trustee's sale is weeks away, the house is vacant and deteriorating, or liens make a retail sale complicated, a buyer who can close quickly once the POA is back may save the equity. Expect less than a retail price.

Petition for a committee

Last resort, sometimes necessary

If the owner won't sign anything, or a divorce or civil suit requires a committee, a spouse or other interested party can ask the circuit court to appoint one. The committee posts bond, accounts to the court, and can sell with court approval.

💵 A Worked Example: A Richmond House With a Trustee's Sale Coming

Say the house would be worth $240,000 after repairs. It needs about $35,000 of work (roof, HVAC and floors), the owner is serving a three-year VADOC sentence, the mortgage balance is $120,000, and the 60-day notice of a trustee's sale arrived last week.

Monthly cost of holding it (illustrative):

  • 🧾 Real estate tax: Richmond's rate is $1.20 per $100 of assessed value. On a $210,000 assessment, that's $2,520 a year, or $210 a month. (Fairfax County's FY2027 rate is $1.12 and Virginia Beach's is $0.97, so check your locality.)
  • 🛡️ Vacant-home insurance: commonly $100 to $200 a month.
  • 💡 Utilities: $100 to $200 a month.
  • 🌱 Lawn, gutters, minor repairs: $75 to $150 a month.
  • 🏦 Missed mortgage payments, late fees and foreclosure costs added to the payoff.

Roughly $485 to $760 a month before the mortgage.

A cash sale. Cash buyers commonly offer 60% to 80% of after-repair value. Here a fair offer might be about $160,000 to $175,000, say $168,000 (70%). The seller pays Virginia's grantor tax of 50 cents per $500 (about $168), plus the regional WMATA capital fee if the house is in a Northern Virginia locality (not Richmond). After paying off about $128,000 with arrears and fees, plus closing costs, the owner might net around $35,000 to $38,000, before any judgment liens.

A retail listing as-is might bring $195,000 to $210,000 if it can be listed, shown and closed before the trustee's sale, netting perhaps $55,000 to $68,000 after commission and payoff. With 60 days on the clock and a POA still to be signed inside, that's a real if: if the timeline is realistic, list; if it isn't, a quick sale protects what's left.

🙅 When You Shouldn't Sell to a Cash Buyer

If the house is in good condition, there's no trustee's sale scheduled, and someone can manage showings, list it. If the family can keep paying and the owner will be home soon, keep it. And if the sale is happening because of a divorce, let the attorneys and the court handle the committee question first. 📋 What To Do This Week

  1. Find out exactly where the owner is: a local or regional jail, or a VADOC facility (VADOC's inmate locator will show it).
  2. Pull the deed from the circuit court clerk's land records and note how title is held.
  3. Run a judgment and lien search in that circuit court.
  4. Have a Virginia attorney draft a recordable POA with real property authority and send it as legal mail.
  5. Ask the facility, in writing, how to schedule a notary for an acknowledgment.
  6. If a trustee's sale notice has arrived, write down the sale date and work backward: POA back, buyer chosen, settlement scheduled.

✅ Bottom Line

Virginia lets a circuit court hand a felony prisoner's property to a committee, but until that happens the owner keeps full authority. Use that window to sign a recordable power of attorney, check the circuit court for judgment and restitution liens, and watch the 60-day trustee's sale notice. With those in place, a Virginia house can be sold from inside a VADOC facility much like any other sale.

If the house is sound and there's time, list it. If a trustee's sale is close, the house needs work or the liens are tangled, ask us, and we'll tell you honestly which situation you're in.

❓ Frequently Asked Questions

What is a committee for a prisoner in Virginia?

Under Va. Code § 53.1-221, when someone is convicted of a felony and sentenced to a state correctional facility, the circuit court can, on motion of any interested party, commit their estate to a person it selects. That committee posts bond, manages the property under court supervision, must provide for the prisoner's spouse and family, and accounts to the court. It ends after release.

Can a Virginia prisoner still sell their own house?

Yes. Section 53.1-221 provides that until a committee is appointed, the prisoner keeps full capacity and authority over their property. They can sign a deed or a power of attorney so an agent can sell for them.

When is a committee required in Virginia?

With some exceptions, § 53.1-223 says civil suits against a prisoner proceed through a committee, a guardian ad litem or an attorney of record. In a divorce where the court will divide marital property under § 20-107.3, the court must appoint a committee first. Families also ask for one when the owner won't or can't sign a power of attorney.

What does a Virginia power of attorney need to sell real estate?

Under § 64.2-1603 the principal signs, and a signature acknowledged before a notary is presumed genuine. To be recorded with the deed under § 55.1-600, the POA should be acknowledged. General real property authority under § 64.2-1625 lets the agent sell and convey; gifts and changes to survivorship rights need an express grant under § 64.2-1622.

Do Virginia prisons provide notaries?

Yes. VADOC Operating Procedure 866.3 requires each facility to provide notary services free of charge within one working day of a request. Confirm in advance that the facility's notary will take an acknowledgment on a deed or power of attorney. Local and regional jails set their own rules.

Where should legal mail to a Virginia prisoner be sent?

Legal mail from attorneys and courts goes to the VADOC Centralized Mail Distribution Center, with the inmate's name and number, at 3521 Woods Way, State Farm, VA 23160. It is scanned, screened and delivered the same business day. Local jails have their own mail rules.

Does my spouse have to sign to sell our Virginia home if they're in prison?

If you own it as tenants by the entirety, yes. Under § 55.1-136, an entireties interest can be severed only by a deed signed by both spouses. The spouse inside can sign the deed in front of a notary or sign a power of attorney. If the house is titled only in the incarcerated spouse's name, Virginia doesn't have community property, so the other spouse usually doesn't need to sign.

How long does a restitution judgment stay a lien on Virginia property?

Restitution orders are docketed as judgments under § 19.2-305.2, and a victim's enforcement of a docketed restitution order isn't subject to any statute of limitations. Other judgments dated on or after July 1, 2021 generally last 10 years, with up to two 10-year extensions, under § 8.01-251.

How much notice does a Virginia homeowner get before a foreclosure sale?

For owner-occupied residential property, § 55.1-321 requires notice by certified or registered mail at least 60 days before the trustee's sale. For other property it's 14 days. The sale must also be advertised under § 55.1-322, so a scheduled sale can arrive within a few months of the first missed payments.

What does a seller pay in transfer taxes in Virginia?

Under § 58.1-802 the grantor tax of 50 cents per $500 of value is paid by the seller unless the parties agree otherwise. In Northern Virginia localities the seller also pays a regional WMATA capital fee of 10 cents per $100. State and local recordation taxes are separate and are allocated in the sales contract.
