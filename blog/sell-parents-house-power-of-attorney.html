Selling Your Parents' House With Power of Attorney: The Authority Ends at Death

Yes, an agent with the right power of attorney can sell a parent's house. But that authority stops when your parent dies, title companies get a say, and selling now rather than later can change the family's taxes and Medicaid picture. Here's what to check in the document, how the rules differ by state, and when you shouldn't sell at all.

By Dustin Ray, Founder of Pages of Purpose LLC. Not legal advice — laws differ by state, and powers of attorney, tax and Medicaid rules change. Before you sign anything, talk to an elder law or real estate attorney licensed in the state where the property is.

A family usually gets to this question at a hard moment. A parent has moved into memory care, or fallen, or simply can't manage the house anymore. The bills keep coming, the roof's getting older, and you've got a folder with a power of attorney someone signed years ago. You want to know whether that paper lets you sell.

Usually it does. But three things decide whether it should: whether this document actually covers the sale, whether the sale can close while your parent is alive, and whether selling now helps or hurts them. The second and third get far too little attention, and they're where families lose real money. So we'll start with the clock.

⏳ The Authority Ends at Death, So the Closing Has to Come First

A power of attorney lets your parent (the principal) authorize you (the agent, or attorney-in-fact) to act for them while they're alive. It's not a will, and it's not a transfer of ownership. Under the Uniform Power of Attorney Act, which about 30 states and D.C. have adopted in some form, a power terminates when the principal dies. Every state ends up in about the same place.

That gives you a simple rule that catches families off guard: the deed has to be signed and the sale completed while your parent is alive. A signed contract isn't enough. If your parent dies with a sale pending, your authority generally stops right there, and the house goes through their estate. The contract may still bind the estate, but the person who signs the deed will be an executor or administrator appointed by a probate court, or the heirs. That can take weeks or many months.

Never sign after a death. The Uniform Act protects a person acting in good faith without actual knowledge of the death. It doesn't protect an agent who knows. If your parent dies the night before closing, stop and call the closing agent. Signing that deed anyway can leave a defective title and put you in legal trouble. Title underwriters tell agents not to insure when they know of a principal's death.

So with a parent who's seriously ill, speed has a value that has nothing to do with money. If the house is going to be sold, plan a date, and tell the title company early. On the other hand, a parent near the end of life is exactly the situation in which you may not want to sell at all, for tax reasons we'll get to below.

The agent's power can also end sooner. Your parent can revoke it while they've got capacity. A non-durable power ends at incapacity. And a court-appointed guardian can sometimes limit or end it.

🔍 Does Your Document Really Let You Sell a House?

Don't go by what it's called. Read it, or pay a lawyer a few hundred dollars to read it. You're looking for these things:

  • 🏠 Real property authority. A general grant may work, but look for words like "sell, convey, and execute deeds" for real estate. Title underwriters are strict here. One underwriting manual puts it this way: power to sell is not a power to convey.
  • 🛡️ Durability. If your parent has lost capacity, the document must survive that. Under the Uniform Act, powers are generally durable unless they say otherwise. Older documents and some states' laws want explicit language. Michigan's version, for example, requires notarization or two witnesses for durability.
  • 🕰️ When it starts. A "springing" power takes effect only on a future event, usually a doctor's finding of incapacity. You may need letters from physicians. Florida generally doesn't allow new springing powers at all.
  • 🖋️ Proper signing. To be recorded, it generally must be notarized, and some states add witnesses. A power that can't be recorded usually can't support a deed.
  • 👥 Who the agent is. If two agents must act together, you both sign. Check for a successor if the first agent can't serve.
  • 🎁 Limits. Is a house named? Is there a limit on sales to family? Any restriction will be read closely.

If you're the agent and your parent still has capacity, the simplest option is often to skip the power entirely. Your parent signs the deed, with a notary coming to them, and nobody needs to question the document. A POA is a tool for when they can't sign, not a requirement that you take over.

🗺️ State Rules That Trip Up Out-of-State Families

The law that applies is usually the state where the house is, especially for recording. A document written in one state can be valid in another, but a closing agent may ask for more. Here are a few examples of how much the rules vary.

A married parent adds another layer. In some states a spouse has to join in selling a homestead or sign away dower, even when they aren't on the deed. Spouses own community property together in others. A POA from Dad doesn't sign for Mom, so if both are on the deed, you need authority from both. A deceased spouse's name on the title also needs clearing with a death certificate or more.

🏢 The Title Company Has the Practical Last Word

Sometimes families think the question is whether the law allows the sale. In real life, the question is whether a title company will insure it. Without title insurance, a retail buyer's lender won't close. Here's what they'll want:

  1. A copy of the power, early. Send it when the contract is signed, not the day before closing. Many insist on the original to record.
  2. Recording. The acknowledged power is usually recorded with the deed in the county where the house sits. Revocations are recorded the same way.
  3. An agent's affidavit or certification. You swear that your parent is alive and that the power hasn't been revoked. This is a statement under oath, so don't take it lightly.
  4. Contact with your parent, where possible. Underwriting guidance tells agents to confirm that the principal hasn't revoked and still wants to sell, and to check for fraud. Expect questions, especially about an older power.
  5. Your signature, in the right form. It usually looks like "Mary Smith, by Jane Smith, her attorney-in-fact." Follow the closing agent's wording exactly.

Can a title company refuse? Often, yes. Under the Uniform Act, a person presented with an acknowledged power has seven business days to ask for a certification, translation or opinion of counsel, then five days to accept it. There are listed reasons to decline, and states vary on penalties for unreasonable refusal. In practice, a title insurer isn't required to insure something it doesn't like. A wording problem found in week one can be fixed. Found on closing day, it can kill the sale.

There's no fixed age when a power "expires," unless it says so. But a 15-year-old power from a parent who now has dementia gets a closer look than one signed last spring.

💰 Sell Now or Wait? The Tax Question Few Agents Ask

If your parent's death is likely soon and the gain is big, selling first can cost your family a lot more than any repair. Two federal rules collide here.

The step-up in basis. Under Internal Revenue Code § 1014, inherited property generally takes a new basis equal to its value at death. Children who inherit a house and sell it soon after generally owe little or no capital gains tax, however cheaply the parent bought it.

The home sale exclusion. A living owner can usually exclude up to $250,000 of gain ($500,000 for a married couple filing jointly) if they owned and lived in the home for two of the last five years. IRS Publication 523 has a helpful exception: if a person becomes unable to care for themselves, and lived in the home at least 12 months of the last five years, time in a licensed care facility counts as living there.

Run both for your parent. Suppose Mom bought in 1984 for $90,000.

  • 📉 House worth $300,000. The gain is $210,000, under the exclusion if she qualifies. Tax: probably none. The step-up isn't worth waiting for.
  • 📈 House worth $600,000. The gain is $510,000. After $250,000, $260,000 is taxable, or roughly $39,000 at a 15% federal rate, plus any state tax. If she died still owning it, the family's tax could be near zero.

Her basis may be higher than the purchase price, thanks to improvements. A surviving spouse's death may have stepped up part of it already. An accountant can settle this in an hour, and that hour is worth buying before you list. If your parent has moved out years ago, make sure the five-year test is still met, because the exclusion can vanish after too long away.

🏥 Medicaid and Care Costs: Selling Changes What Your Parent Owns

The house may be treated very differently from the money it turns into. In many situations, a parent's home is an exempt asset for Medicaid, at least for a time, such as while a spouse lives there. Sell it, and the proceeds become a countable asset. Medicaid long-term care typically requires very low assets, often about $2,000. So a sale can end eligibility until the money is spent on care.

That's not always bad. The money may be just what pays for a good facility, and keeping an empty house isn't free. Many states also seek estate recovery after death, which can reach the house. But it's a decision to make on purpose, with an elder law attorney, before you sign a contract.

  • 🧾 Sell at fair market value. Medicaid looks back 60 months at transfers (42 U.S.C. § 1396p). A sale for less than the home's fair market value can be treated as a gift and trigger a penalty period. An as-is sale at a documented market price isn't the same as a family discount, but get an appraisal if you're selling below retail.
  • 🏦 Keep the money in your parent's name. Proceeds go to your parent's account, or as the elder law plan says. They don't go to you, even if you've been paying the bills.
  • 📁 Keep every record. Save the appraisal, offers, settlement statement and where the money went.

⚖️ You're a Fiduciary, Not an Heir

An agent must act in the parent's best interests, not their own and not their heirs'. Siblings often forget this until the house sells, and then they want to see the numbers.

  • 🚫 Don't buy it yourself without clear authority. Agents generally can't deed the property to themselves or a relative at a discount, or make gifts, unless the document expressly allows it. Title companies will balk, and you can be sued.
  • 🚫 Don't pay yourself from the proceeds without authority. Reimbursements need records. Compensation needs a basis in the document or state law.
  • 📣 Tell your siblings. The law may not require it, but it's the cheapest protection you'll ever have. Share the agent's opinion of value, the offers and the closing statement. A dispute over the house often turns into the same fight heirs have after a death, only earlier.
  • 📊 Get a value in writing. A neutral appraisal costs a few hundred dollars and answers most later accusations.

Penalties can be serious. Michigan's law, for example, makes an agent who wrongfully takes property liable for three times its value. And if adult protective services gets involved, a third party may refuse the power altogether.

🧭 If There's No Valid Power, or It Won't Work

When your parent can still understand and sign, have them sign a new, state-specific durable power or just sign the deed. If they've lost capacity and there's no usable power, a court generally has to appoint a guardian or conservator with authority to sell. That commonly takes a few months and can cost thousands of dollars, with court approval of the sale in many states. If your parent has already died, you're out of POA territory altogether. A co-owner complicates things too. If your parent owns with a sibling or someone else, your authority covers only your parent's share. See when a co-owner can sell.

🪧 Listing vs. Cash: What Your Parent Actually Keeps

🪧 List it

Best for most parents

Your duty is to your parent's interest, and a fair market sale is the cleanest way to show it. If the house is sound, list it. Clean it out, make simple fixes and use a good agent. You'll very likely net more, and the file looks better.

💵 Sell as-is for cash

Fits a neglected house or a tight deadline

This fits a hoarded or storm-damaged house that won't finance, a far-away family, or care bills due now. Get more than one offer, and keep them in the file. You'll need to show why the price was fair.

🕰️ Don't sell yet

Sometimes the right answer

If a big step-up is likely, or a spouse still lives there, or the Medicaid plan depends on the house, holding can win. Then take care of it: keep insurance in force and don't let taxes lapse.

Here's how the money works on a house worth $350,000 fixed up, or $310,000 as it sits. Listing as-is, a 6% commission and $3,000 of closing costs leave about $288,000 before any mortgage, and three months of carrying costs bring that down to about $285,000. A cash offer typically runs 60-80% of repaired value; at 70%, that's $245,000, or about $242,000 after closing costs and a month of carrying. In this example, your parent pays about $43,000 for speed and simplicity. If a listing is feasible, your job as a fiduciary is probably to list.

Waiting has a monthly price. Property tax ranges from under 1% to more than 2% of value a year, depending on the state and whether a senior exemption survives the move. Vacant-home insurance often runs $150 to $300 a month, since a standard policy may limit coverage after 30 or 60 days of vacancy. Utilities and lawn care add $200 to $400. Call it $700 to $1,200 a month on a paid-off $310,000 house, and tell the insurer when your parent moves out.

📋 What To Do This Week

  1. Find the original power. Check for notary, witnesses, real estate language and durability.
  2. Find the deed. See exactly who owns the house. Include a spouse or co-owner.
  3. Call a title company in the county. Ask them to review the power before you do anything else.
  4. Book an elder law consult. Ask how a sale affects care costs, Medicaid and the home.
  5. Ask an accountant the step-up question. Bring the purchase price and any major improvements.
  6. Get a written value. Use an appraisal or two agents' opinions.
  7. Protect the house. Tell the insurer it's vacant, forward the mail, winterize if needed and keep taxes current.
  8. Update the family. A short email now can save you years of argument.

✅ Bottom Line

A power of attorney can sell your parent's house if it clearly allows it, if a title company accepts it, and if the closing happens while your parent is alive. Before you use it, ask the harder questions. Would the family pay less tax if the house passed at death? Would selling end Medicaid eligibility? Is a fair market listing feasible? You're spending someone else's money, so document everything and share it. If the house is rough, the family is far away and the bills are real, a cash sale can be a fair answer. On a sound house, it usually won't be.

❓ Frequently Asked Questions

Can I sell my parents' house with power of attorney?

Usually yes, if the document grants authority over real estate, was properly signed, is accepted by a title company and your parent is still alive at closing. A durable power is needed if your parent has lost capacity.

Does a power of attorney end when a parent dies?

Yes. The authority generally ends at death. The house then passes through the estate, a trust or a survivorship deed, and you'd need authority from a court, or the heirs' signatures, to sell it.

What if my parent dies before closing?

Stop and tell the closing agent. You can't sign the deed once you know your parent has died. A signed contract may still bind the estate, but a personal representative or the heirs would need to finish the sale.

Does a power of attorney need to be recorded to sell a house?

Usually yes. The acknowledged power is typically recorded with the deed in the county where the house sits. Ask the title company early, because many want the original.

Can a title company refuse my power of attorney?

Yes. A title insurer can decline to insure if it isn't satisfied. Under the Uniform Act, a person may request a certification or opinion of counsel within seven business days, then has five days to accept. States vary.

Can I sell my parent's house to myself under a power of attorney?

Generally not without clear authority in the document. Agents owe a duty of loyalty, and a sale to yourself at a discount can be challenged as self-dealing. Get a lawyer's advice first.

Do I pay capital gains if I sell my parent's house with power of attorney?

Your parent, not you, reports the sale. They may exclude up to $250,000 of gain, or $500,000 for a qualifying couple. If the gain exceeds that, waiting until death for a step-up in basis could save a lot of tax.

Will selling a parent's house affect Medicaid?

Yes, often. A home may be exempt, but sale proceeds usually count as assets. A sale for less than fair market value can trigger a penalty under the 60-month look-back. See an elder law attorney before you sign.

Who gets the money when I sell under power of attorney?

Your parent does. Proceeds belong in your parent's account and must be used for them. An agent can't keep the money, or divide it among siblings, unless the document expressly allows it.

What if my parent has dementia and there is no power of attorney?

If your parent can no longer understand a new document, a court usually must appoint a guardian or conservator with authority to sell. Ask an elder law attorney, since this often takes months.
