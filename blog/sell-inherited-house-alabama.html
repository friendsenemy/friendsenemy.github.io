Selling an Inherited House in Alabama: Who Can Sign the Deed, and the 2026 ID Rules for Empty Homes

Since October 1, 2026, Alabama closing agents must verify who is selling an empty, paid-off house, and an executor still needs either a will that grants a power of sale or the probate judge's approval to sign. Here is how agreeable heirs line up authority, ID, Medicaid payoffs and taxes, what an empty house costs to hold, and when listing beats a cash offer. Or ask us a question, free.

By Dustin Ray, Founder of Pages of Purpose LLC. Not legal advice. Alabama probate, title and notary rules are technical, the 2026 property-fraud law is new, and laws differ by state. Before anyone signs a deed or a listing agreement for an inherited house, talk to an attorney licensed in Alabama, where the property is.

Picture the most ordinary version of this story. Your mother passed away in Dothan last spring. The house is paid off, nobody lives there now, and you and your brother both want it sold. No fight, no lawsuit, no missing cousin. In most states, that family's only real questions are how fast probate moves and whether to fix the kitchen first.

In Alabama in late 2026, there are two more. The first is a new one: a fraud law that took effect on October 1, 2026 treats an empty, paid-off house as the exact kind of property thieves target, so the people selling it now get checked more closely than they did a year ago. The second is older but just as practical: an Alabama personal representative cannot sign a deed to the house on their own say-so unless the will expressly gives them that power. Get both right and an agreeable family can sell quickly. Get either wrong and a closing that should take three weeks drifts into months.

This guide is for heirs who agree, or for the only heir. If your family is split over whether to sell at all, our guide for Alabama heirs who can't agree covers buyouts and court-ordered sales; if the deed was in two living people's names, start with selling a co-owned house in Alabama.

🔏 Since October 1, 2026, an Empty, Paid-Off Alabama House Gets an Identity Check at Closing

Alabama's Legislature passed Senate Bill 292, the Alabama Property Protection Act of 2026, and the Governor signed it on April 16, 2026 as Act 2026-536. It took effect October 1, 2026. It was written to stop seller-impersonation fraud: a stranger pretends to own a vacant lot or an empty house, signs a deed in the owner's name, and disappears with the sale money before anyone notices. Inherited houses are a favorite target, because the real owner is dead and the heirs often live in another city.

Three parts of the new law land directly on families selling an inherited house:

  • 🪪 The closing agent must validate the seller's identity. New Ala. Code § 35-21-5(b) requires a settlement agent, before closing a transfer of Class III property (the tax class that covers ordinary homes), to conduct identity document validation and identity verification of the seller when the property is vacant or not owner-occupied and has no mortgage on it. A paid-off house that has sat empty since a parent's death fits that description almost word for word. The agent has to keep the proof for five years.
  • 🤝 A real estate agent who has never met you will ask for proof. Under § 35-21-5(a), a licensed agent or broker may not sign a listing agreement with a seller who is unknown to them and isn't physically present unless the seller provides government-issued ID and ownership records for the property.
  • 💻 For-sale-by-owner websites now ask for documents too. A new chapter of the consumer code requires online real estate platforms, for listings that come straight from the owner rather than through a broker or MLS, to collect an unexpired photo ID plus an ownership record before publishing the listing. Notably, a last will and testament is one of the accepted ownership documents, along with the county's record of the owner, a deed or a title history.

The same act tightened remote notarization. An Alabama remote notary must be physically in Alabama, the signer must be located in the United States, the notary must check two government IDs (one with the signer's face and signature) plus a database check unless the notary personally knows the signer, and the video must be kept for seven years. A notary must refuse if the signer's image appears artificially generated.

What does that mean for an honest family? Mostly paperwork you can line up in advance. The heir who lives in Atlanta or Chicago can still sign remotely, but should expect a more formal ID check. An heir who lives overseas can't use an Alabama remote notarization at all under the new location rule, so the closing attorney will need another arrangement for that signature; ask about it early rather than the week of closing.

⚠️ The warning that matters most: an empty inherited house in Alabama is now officially the kind of property the Legislature considers at risk of deed theft. Until it sells, check the county probate office's land records every few weeks for any deed you didn't sign, keep the utilities and insurance in an heir's or the estate's name, and ask whether your county's judge of probate has set up the free owner-notification service the new law allows. A forged deed is far easier to unwind in the first weeks than after a second sale.

If a fake deed does show up, the act gives you faster tools than before. You can file a complaint with the Alabama Securities Commission, which can declare a fraudulent conveyance void and impose penalties of up to $100,000 per transaction. You can also bring an expedited quiet title action in the county's circuit court, where the court must set a preliminary hearing within 30 days of service, and if the court finds the deed was fraudulent you can recover your attorney fees.

✍️ Who Is Allowed to Sign the Deed: Three Answers in Alabama

The identity check only matters if the right person is signing. In Alabama, that depends on what paperwork exists, and the answer decides how fast the house can close.

📜 A personal representative whose will grants the power to sell

Ala. Code § 43-2-844 says that, unless expressly authorized by the will, a personal representative may sell, mortgage or lease estate real property only after prior approval of the court. Read that the other way around: if your parent's will expressly authorizes the executor to sell real estate, and the probate court has issued letters testamentary, the personal representative can sign a deed without going back to the judge for permission. Many Alabama wills drafted by lawyers include that language. Look for a paragraph granting the executor power to sell "any real or personal property, at public or private sale, without order of court." If it's there, this is usually the fastest clean path.

🏛️ A personal representative who needs the court's approval

If there is no will, or the will is silent about selling land, the administrator or executor has to ask the probate court to approve the sale before closing. That means a petition, notice to the heirs, and an order. When every heir consents in writing, judges often move these quickly; when someone objects, the process slows and can turn into the kind of dispute our guide to heirs who can't agree covers. Budget extra weeks, and tell any buyer up front that a court order is part of the timeline. A buyer who doesn't know that will be surprised and may walk.

👨‍👩‍👧 Every heir signing together, with no estate opened

Alabama law vests a decedent's land in the heirs or devisees at death, subject to the estate's needs. So a family where every heir is an adult, competent and willing can sometimes sell by having all of them sign one deed, with an affidavit establishing who the heirs are, without opening an estate at all. It costs less in court fees. The catch is title insurance: the buyer's title company has to be comfortable that no unpaid creditor, no later-found will and no forgotten heir can surface. Many will ask for more proof, or decline, when the death was recent. That is the subject of the next two sections.

Each route has a different weak spot. A will-granted power fails if the will was never admitted to probate. A court-approved sale fails if an heir objects. An all-heirs deed fails if one heir can't or won't sign, if an heir is a minor, or if an heir is incarcerated without a working power of attorney (our guide to an heir who inherits while in prison covers that last problem).

📭 No Transfer-on-Death Deed in Alabama, So the Paperwork Falls on the Heirs

More than half the states let an owner record a transfer-on-death deed that hands the house to a named beneficiary at death with no probate. Alabama is not one of them; it has not adopted that kind of deed. Children who grew up elsewhere are sometimes surprised by this, because they assume a parent "must have" signed something similar.

What an Alabama parent might have done instead:

  • 🔗 A deed with a right of survivorship to a child or spouse while the parent was alive. If so, the survivor may own the whole house already, and the sale needs only a death certificate and that survivor's signature. Alabama survivorship deeds come in more than one form, which our Alabama co-owner guide explains.
  • 🏡 A life estate deed that kept a lifetime right to live there for the parent and named who gets the house afterward. The remainder owners can usually sell after recording the death certificate.
  • 🗂️ A revocable living trust. The successor trustee signs under the trust's terms, with no probate court involved.
  • 📄 Nothing but a will, or nothing at all. This is the most common case, and it puts you back into the three signing routes above.

Pull the last recorded deed from the county probate office before you assume anything. It costs a few dollars and settles which of these you are dealing with.

One more trap: Alabama's summary distribution procedure for small estates covers personal property only. Families sometimes hear "small estate affidavit" and assume it will move the house. It won't; real estate goes through one of the routes above.

⏳ The Creditor Window That Sets the Earliest Clean Closing

Once letters are issued, Alabama gives creditors a defined period to file claims against the estate. Under Ala. Code § 43-2-350, claims must be presented within six months after the grant of letters, or within five months from the first publication of the notice to creditors, whichever is later. A creditor entitled to actual notice gets thirty days after that notice. Claims are filed in the office of the judge of probate.

Why that matters to a sale: a personal representative who sells the house in month two can do it, but the money generally has to stay in the estate until the claim period has run, so nobody distributes cash to heirs and then discovers an unpaid hospital bill or credit card judgment. Families who want to "sell and split it next week" should plan on holding the proceeds in an estate account for a while. That's not a reason to delay the sale itself; an empty house costs money every month, and cash sitting in an estate account doesn't.

For the all-heirs-deed route with no estate opened, there is no notice to creditors and no deadline running, which is exactly why a title insurer may hesitate soon after a death. Ask the closing attorney what that particular underwriter needs before you sign a contract with anyone.

🏥 Alabama Medicaid: Ask for the Payoff Before You Set a Closing Date

If your parent spent time in a nursing home on Medicaid, assume there may be a claim against the house until you confirm otherwise. Alabama Medicaid uses two tools.

The first is a property lien, which the Agency calls a TEFRA lien, placed on real property when an institutionalized person applies, so they can qualify for Medicaid without first selling the home. The Agency's instructions for paying one off are specific: email a copy of the sales contract and the preliminary settlement statement to its property-lien unit at least three business days before the scheduled closing. Miss that and the closing slips.

The second is estate recovery. Alabama seeks repayment for benefits received after age 55, or at any age during a stay in a nursing home or similar institution, from assets the person owned at death, which can include the house. Recovery is delayed while a surviving spouse is alive, while a child is under 21, or for the lifetime of a blind or disabled child, and there is a narrow hardship waiver. None of that is a reason not to sell. It is a reason to know the number before you accept an offer, because a Medicaid claim comes off the top of the sale proceeds.

💵 Taxes on the Sale Are Mostly Good News in Alabama

Alabama has no inheritance tax and no estate tax of its own. Federally, the house usually receives a stepped-up tax basis equal to its value on the date of death, so if you sell within a year or two for about what it was worth then, there is little or no gain to tax. Alabama taxes capital gains as ordinary income at its regular rates, which top out at 5 percent, so even a modest gain from a later sale stays small at the state level.

The state's deed tax is cheap: under Ala. Code § 40-22-1, it is 50 cents for each $500 of value conveyed, only on the value above any existing mortgage that already paid mortgage tax. On a $150,000 paid-off house that is $150. Recording fees are set by the county probate office.

Property tax is where an empty house changes the math. Alabama assesses an owner-occupied home at 10 percent of its appraised value, and senior exemptions can reduce the bill further. Those benefits were your parent's. Once nobody lives there, the house can be reclassified, and the next bill can be noticeably higher; our Alabama heirs guide walks through that reclassification. Call the county revenue commissioner and ask what the coming bill will be on the house as it now sits.

🧭 Which Route Fits Your Family

🏠 List it with a local agent

If the will gives the executor power to sell, every heir agrees, and the house shows reasonably well, listing usually puts the most money in the estate. Alabama's housing prices are moderate, so a few thousand dollars of paint, carpet and yard work can matter more here than in a pricier market. Expect the new ID rules to add a step if the agent has never met you.

Best price for a sound house

🔨 Fix it up first

Repairs pay off when the house is solid and the neighborhood supports a higher price. They don't pay off when the roof, foundation or plumbing needs real work and the heirs are managing it from another state. Every month of repairs is another month of the carrying costs above, and the estate, not any one heir, should be paying for them.

Only with firm bids in hand

💵 Sell for cash as-is

A cash sale trades price for certainty. It fits a house with deferred maintenance, an estate with a Medicaid claim or other bills to settle, or heirs who can't travel to Alabama for repairs and showings. A serious buyer will work around a court-approval timeline and will take the house with its contents.

When speed and condition decide

👪 Let one heir buy it

If a sibling wants the house, have it appraised, credit their own share against the price, and close it like any other sale with a title company. It keeps the house in the family without anyone feeling shortchanged. Get the lender's pre-approval before telling the other heirs it's settled.

🏡 A Worked Example: A Paid-Off Ranch House Outside Mobile

Here is a composite example of the kind we see. Three siblings inherit their father's brick ranch outside Mobile. The house is paid off, empty since June, and his will names the oldest daughter as executor with an express power to sell real estate without court order. Fixed up, comparable houses sell for about $210,000. This one needs a roof, an HVAC unit and a kitchen, roughly $35,000 of work, and it had Medicaid nursing-home care in his last year.

  • 💵 Cash range: 60–80% of $210,000 is $126,000 to $168,000. Given the roof and HVAC, a fair as-is offer here would land near the middle, around $140,000 to $150,000, with the buyer paying typical closing costs.
  • 🔨 Repair and list: $210,000 sale, minus $35,000 of repairs, about $12,600 in commissions at 6%, around $3,000 in seller closing costs and concessions, and four extra months of holding at about $500 a month. Net: about $157,000, if the bids hold and the house sells at full price.
  • 🏠 List as-is: many retail buyers' lenders won't finance a house with a failing roof, so the realistic buyer pool is investors anyway, at prices similar to a cash offer but with a longer timeline.

The difference between repairing and selling as-is is about $7,000 to $17,000 on paper, before any surprises. Whichever path they choose, the executor emails the Medicaid property-lien unit with the contract and settlement statement at least three business days before closing, the Medicaid claim is paid from proceeds, and the remaining money stays in the estate account until the creditor window under § 43-2-350 has closed. Because the will has a power of sale, none of this needs a court order. If the will had been silent, add the court-approval petition to the front of the timeline.

📋 What To Do This Week

  1. 🔍 Find the original will and read it for a power-of-sale clause. If there's no will, note that, too.
  2. 🏛️ Pull the last recorded deed and the tax record from the county probate office and revenue commissioner so you know exactly whose name is on the house.
  3. 📝 If an estate needs to be opened, call the probate office about filing for letters, and file promptly; the creditor window doesn't start until letters are issued.
  4. 🏥 If your parent had nursing-home Medicaid, contact Alabama Medicaid about any property lien or estate claim.
  5. 🔐 Change the locks, confirm the insurer knows the house is empty, and ask whether your county offers the free owner-notification service for new recordings.
  6. 🪪 Make sure every heir who must sign has a current government photo ID, and tell the closing attorney now about anyone who lives abroad.
  7. 💬 Get two opinions on value: a local agent's and, if the house needs work, a written cash offer. Compare the net figures, not the headline prices.

✅ Bottom Line

An agreeable Alabama family can sell an inherited house quickly, but only if two things are lined up first. The person signing the deed needs real authority, which in Alabama means a will that expressly grants a power of sale, a probate court's approval, or every heir signing together. And since October 1, 2026, that person should expect the closing agent to validate their identity, especially on an empty, paid-off house. Sort out authority and ID early, confirm any Medicaid claim, keep the house insured and watched, and then decide between listing and a cash sale on net numbers. On a sound house, listing usually wins. On a house that needs real work and heirs who live far away, a fair cash sale often leaves the family with nearly as much money and far less strain.

❓ Frequently Asked Questions

Can an executor sell an inherited house in Alabama without a court order?

Only if the will expressly authorizes it. Ala. Code § 43-2-844 says that unless the will expressly gives the power, a personal representative may sell estate real property only after prior approval of the probate court. If the will grants a power of sale and letters have been issued, the executor can sign the deed without going back to the judge.

What changed for selling an inherited Alabama house on October 1, 2026?

The Alabama Property Protection Act of 2026 (Act 2026-536) took effect. Before closing the sale of a home that is vacant or not owner-occupied and has no mortgage, the settlement agent must validate and verify the seller's identity. Real estate agents who have never met a seller in person must get government ID and ownership records, and for-sale-by-owner websites must collect photo ID and an ownership document before listing.

Does Alabama allow transfer-on-death deeds?

No. Alabama has not adopted transfer-on-death deeds for real estate. A parent who wanted to avoid probate would have needed a survivorship deed, a life estate deed or a living trust. If none of those exists, the house passes by will or by intestacy and is sold by a personal representative or by all of the heirs together.

Can the heirs sell an Alabama house without opening probate?

Sometimes. Alabama land vests in the heirs at death, so if every heir is an adult who can and will sign, they may be able to sell with one deed and an heirship affidavit. Whether that works depends on the buyer's title insurer, which may ask for an estate to be opened or for more time to pass when the death was recent.

Can I use an Alabama small estate affidavit to sell the house?

No. Alabama's summary distribution procedure for small estates covers personal property only. Real estate has to pass by a deed signed by an authorized personal representative, by all of the heirs, or under a survivorship deed, life estate or trust.

How long do creditors have to file claims against an Alabama estate?

Under Ala. Code § 43-2-350, claims must be presented within six months after letters are granted, or within five months of the first publication of notice to creditors, whichever is later. A creditor entitled to actual notice gets thirty days after that notice. Sale proceeds generally stay in the estate until that window closes.

Will Alabama Medicaid take part of the sale money?

It can. If your parent was in a nursing home on Medicaid, the Agency may hold a property lien on the house or an estate recovery claim. To get a payoff on a property lien, email the sales contract and preliminary settlement statement to Alabama Medicaid's property-lien unit at least three business days before closing. Recovery is delayed while a spouse survives, while a child is under 21, or for a blind or disabled child.

Do I owe tax when I sell a house I inherited in Alabama?

Usually very little. Alabama has no inheritance tax, the house generally gets a stepped-up federal basis at the date of death, and Alabama taxes any gain as ordinary income at rates topping out at 5 percent. The deed tax is 50 cents per $500 of value above any existing mortgage.

Can an heir who lives outside Alabama sign the deed remotely?

Within the United States, yes, through an Alabama remote notary who is physically in Alabama, records the session and verifies the signer with two government IDs and a data check. Since October 1, 2026, the remote signer must be located in the United States, so an heir living abroad needs another arrangement worked out with the closing attorney.

Is listing or a cash sale better for an inherited Alabama house?

List it if the house is in decent shape, the executor has clear power to sell and nobody needs the money right away; an agent will usually net more. A cash sale makes sense when the house needs major work, the heirs live far away, or a Medicaid claim or other debts mean the estate needs a firm closing date.
