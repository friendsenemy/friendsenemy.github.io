Sell a Probate House in Dallas, Texas — The Fast Path and the Slow One

Texas has one of the fastest probate systems in the country — if the will names an independent executor, you can be signing a sale contract within 60 days of filing. Most Dallas heirs don't know that and sit on a vacant house for a year. Here's the real process, the real Dallas County numbers, and whether listing or a cash buyer makes more sense. Your parent died and left a house in Dallas. It's in their name alone. The mail keeps coming, the property tax bill from Dallas County Appraisal District arrived, the grass is getting tall, and three different people have told you three different things about how long this takes. One said two years. One said you can't touch it until probate closes. One said just change the locks and sell it.

None of those are right for Texas. Texas is one of the few states where probate can be fast, cheap, and mostly out of court — if the paperwork was done a certain way and if you choose the right path. This guide walks through what actually happens in Dallas County probate, what it costs, how long it takes, and when selling to a cash buyer beats listing the house with an agent (and when it doesn't).

The Short Answer: Three Ways to Sell a Dallas Probate House

  1. Independent administration. If the will names an independent executor (most Texas wills do), the executor can sell the house without court approval for each step. From filing to a signed sale contract: 45-90 days. This is the fast path and it's why Texas probate has a good reputation.
  2. Muniment of title. If there are no debts other than the mortgage and the only thing to transfer is real estate, Texas lets you skip administration entirely. The court admits the will as a "muniment of title" — a one-hearing proceeding — and the deed passes directly to the heirs. 30-60 days. Then the heirs sell as owners.
  3. Dependent administration. No will, or a will that doesn't grant independent powers, or heirs who fight. Every sale needs a court order, a bond, and a hearing. 9-18 months. This is the slow path and it's what people are thinking of when they say "probate takes forever."

Which one applies to you is mostly already decided by what the will says. Pull the will. If it contains the words "independent executor" or "independent administration" or "without bond and without further action of any court," you're on the fast path.

How Dallas County Probate Actually Works

Dallas County has two statutory probate courts (Probate Court No. 1 and No. 2) plus Probate Court No. 3, all at the George L. Allen Sr. Courts Building downtown. They handle nothing but probate and guardianship, which is why they move faster than county courts in rural Texas that mix probate with everything else.

Step 1: File the application (week 1)

Your attorney files an Application for Probate of Will and Issuance of Letters Testamentary. Filing fee in Dallas County runs about $350-$400. The original will must be filed with it. If the will is lost, there's a separate and harder process.

Step 2: Posting period (10 days minimum)

Texas requires the county clerk to post notice at the courthouse for at least 10 days before a hearing. In Dallas County, realistic hearing dates are 3-5 weeks after filing because of docket volume.

Step 3: The hearing (week 4-6)

Usually 5-10 minutes. The executor testifies that the deceased died, lived in Dallas County, left a valid will, and that the executor is qualified. The judge admits the will and orders letters testamentary issued. Dallas probate courts handle many of these via Zoom now, which speeds things up.

Step 4: Letters issue, executor takes the oath (week 5-7)

Letters Testamentary are the document that proves the executor's authority. A title company will not close a sale without them. Once letters issue, an independent executor can list, sign a contract, and close — no further court orders.

Step 5: Inventory (within 90 days of letters)

The executor files an inventory of estate assets, or an affidavit in lieu of inventory if all beneficiaries have received their accounting. This does not block a sale.

Step 6: Creditor notice (within 1 month of letters)

Publish a notice to creditors in a Dallas County newspaper of general circulation. Known secured creditors (the mortgage lender) get direct notice within 2 months. Unlike some states, Texas does not make you wait out a creditor period before selling real estate under independent administration.

The number that matters: with a valid will naming an independent executor, a Dallas County estate can be ready to sign a sale contract 45-60 days after filing. The two-year horror stories are almost always dependent administrations or will contests.

Muniment of Title: The Dallas Shortcut Most Heirs Never Hear About

Texas Estates Code §257 lets you probate a will as a muniment of title when the estate owes no debts (other than debts secured by real estate, like a mortgage) and there's no need for an administration. The court admits the will and the will itself becomes the deed — it transfers title to the named beneficiaries without an executor ever being appointed.

When it fits: the deceased's only significant asset was the house, credit cards were paid off or small, and the beneficiaries agree. Filing to final order can be 30-45 days in Dallas County. Attorney fees are typically $1,500-$3,000 because there's no ongoing administration.

When it doesn't fit: there are unsecured debts, there's a dispute among heirs, or Medicaid estate recovery is a factor (see below). A muniment also doesn't work if the deceased died intestate — no will means no muniment.

After the muniment order, the heirs own the house as tenants in common. Any one of them can sell their undivided share. All of them together can sell the whole thing. This is where the co-owner rules take over from the probate rules.

What If There's No Will?

Texas intestate succession decides who inherits. Simplified: if the deceased was married and all children are from that marriage, the spouse gets the community property and a life estate in a third of the separate real property. If there are children from a prior relationship, the children get the deceased's half of community property. If unmarried, children inherit equally; if no children, parents and siblings.

Procedurally, no will means either a Determination of Heirship proceeding (court names the heirs, usually combined with an administration) or a Small Estate Affidavit if the estate is under $75,000 excluding homestead and exempt property. Dallas County judges require an attorney ad litem in heirship proceedings to represent unknown heirs, which adds $500-$1,500 and 30-60 days. Budget 4-8 months for an uncontested heirship with independent administration. For the mechanics, see what happens to a house when there's no will.

The Dallas-Specific Costs of Waiting

Every month the house sits in an estate costs real money. Dallas numbers:

  • Property tax. Dallas County combined rate is roughly 2.2-2.5% of appraised value per year depending on the city and school district. A $350,000 house carries $650-$730/month in taxes. The homestead exemption and the over-65 freeze die with the owner — the next tax bill will be higher than the last one the deceased paid.
  • Insurance. Standard homeowner's policies typically lapse or deny claims on a vacant house after 30-60 days. A vacant-dwelling policy in Dallas runs $150-$300/month and still excludes a lot.
  • Foundation. Dallas is built on expansive Blackland Prairie clay. A house with a slab foundation that isn't getting watered along the perimeter will shift. Foundation repair in Dallas runs $8,000-$25,000 and is the single most common reason inherited Dallas houses sell below expectation.
  • HOA. Plano, Frisco, and much of north Dallas carry HOA dues of $50-$200/month that keep accruing, and Texas HOAs can foreclose for unpaid assessments.
  • Vacancy risk. Copper theft, squatters, and code enforcement citations from the City of Dallas for tall grass ($250+ per citation) are all real in a house that sits 6+ months.

A $350,000 Dallas house held for 12 months during a slow probate burns roughly $12,000-$18,000 in hard costs before you count the foundation.

Selling During Probate vs. After

You can sign a contract to sell before letters issue. The contract is contingent on the executor receiving authority to sell; closing happens after letters. Cash buyers do this routinely. Retail buyers with mortgage lenders generally won't — the lender's underwriting can't handle an open-ended contingency. This is one of the practical reasons heirs who want speed end up with a cash buyer: they're the only ones who can lock in a price before the court acts.

After letters, an independent executor sells like any owner. After a muniment, the heirs sell like any owners. Either way, the title company will want certified copies of the court order and letters (or muniment order), a death certificate, and in most cases an affidavit of heirship.

Medicaid Estate Recovery in Texas

If the deceased received Medicaid long-term care benefits after age 55, Texas Health and Human Services can file a claim against the estate to recover what was paid. Texas MERP only applies to estates that go through probate — which is one reason some families use a Transfer on Death Deed or Lady Bird deed before death. If it's too late for that, know that MERP has exemptions: it won't recover if there's a surviving spouse, a child under 21, a disabled child, or if recovery would cause undue hardship. If the deceased was on Medicaid, tell your attorney before filing anything.

When Heirs Disagree About the Dallas House

Three siblings, one house, one wants to keep it, one wants to sell, one won't return calls. Under independent administration, the executor has the authority to sell regardless — but a sibling who objects can file to remove the executor or contest the sale, which turns a 60-day process into a year. After the house passes to heirs as tenants in common, any one heir can sell their share to a third party without the others' consent, and any heir can file a partition suit in Dallas County district court to force a sale of the whole thing. Partition in Texas takes 6-12 months and costs $10,000-$25,000 in legal fees. We cover the whole decision tree in what to do when heirs can't agree on selling.

The shortcut we see work most often: the heir who wants out sells their share to a cash buyer. The remaining heirs are now co-owners with an investor instead of a sibling, and the family conflict is no longer the thing blocking the sale.

Step-by-Step: What to Do This Week

  1. Find the will. Check the deceased's papers, their attorney, and the Dallas County Clerk — Texas lets people deposit wills with the clerk for safekeeping.
  2. Read it for "independent executor." That phrase determines your path.
  3. Pull the deed from Dallas County Clerk records (dallascounty.org, free search). Confirm the house is in the deceased's name alone and check for a Transfer on Death Deed, which would bypass probate entirely.
  4. Call the mortgage servicer if there is one. Tell them the borrower died. Federal law (Garn-St. Germain) prevents them from calling the loan due, and the CFPB successor-in-interest rules require them to work with you.
  5. Get a vacant-dwelling insurance policy if the house is empty. Do this before anything else.
  6. Talk to a Texas probate attorney. Most Dallas probate attorneys do a flat-fee independent administration for $2,500-$4,000. Ask for the flat fee up front.
  7. Decide: list or cash. Get a real cash offer and a real agent CMA. Compare the net after costs and time. Don't guess.

Bottom Line

Dallas probate doesn't have to take years. With a will naming an independent executor, you can be under contract in 60 days. With a clean estate, muniment of title gets you there in 45. Frequently Asked Questions

How long does probate take in Dallas County, Texas?

With a will naming an independent executor, letters testamentary typically issue 4-7 weeks after filing, and the executor can then sell without further court approval. Muniment of title (no debts, real estate only) can be done in 30-45 days. Dependent administration or heirship proceedings without a will take 6-18 months.

Can I sell an inherited house in Dallas before probate is finished?

Yes, under independent administration. Once letters testamentary issue, the executor can list and sell without court approval for each step. You can even sign a contingent contract before letters issue; closing happens once the executor has authority. Cash buyers routinely do this; mortgage-financed buyers usually can't.

What is a muniment of title in Texas?

A probate proceeding under Texas Estates Code §257 that admits the will to probate without appointing an executor. It's available when the estate has no unsecured debts and no need for administration. The will itself transfers title to the named beneficiaries. It's faster and cheaper than full administration — typically 30-45 days and $1,500-$3,000 in attorney fees.

What does it cost to probate a will in Dallas County?

Filing fees around $350-$400. Attorney fees for an uncontested independent administration typically $2,500-$5,000 flat fee. Muniment of title $1,500-$3,000. Dependent administration or contested matters $8,000-$20,000 or more. Plus publication costs for the creditor notice, roughly $100-$200.

Who inherits a Dallas house if there's no will?

Texas intestate succession applies. Surviving spouse and children share depending on whether the children are from that marriage. If unmarried, children inherit equally; if no children, parents and siblings. The heirs are established through a Determination of Heirship proceeding in probate court, which requires an attorney ad litem and typically takes 3-6 months.

Do I have to pay property taxes on an inherited house during probate?

Yes. Dallas County property taxes keep accruing and the estate is responsible. The homestead and over-65 exemptions end at the owner's death, so the next bill will be higher. Unpaid taxes become a lien and Dallas County can eventually foreclose. Most estates pay them from estate funds or at closing from sale proceeds.

Can one heir sell a Dallas house without the other heirs?

After title passes to the heirs (via muniment or distribution), each heir owns an undivided share as a tenant in common and can sell that share to a third party without the others' consent. No single heir can sell the whole house alone. Any heir can file a partition suit to force a sale of the entire property.

What is Medicaid estate recovery and does it affect a Dallas probate sale?

If the deceased received Medicaid long-term care benefits after age 55, Texas HHS can file a claim against the probate estate. The claim must be addressed before distribution to heirs. There are exemptions for surviving spouses, minor or disabled children, and hardship. Tell your attorney immediately if the deceased was on Medicaid.

Should I fix up an inherited Dallas house before selling it?

Only if you have the cash, the time, and the heirs agree. Dallas foundation and roof repairs often cost $20,000-$50,000 and take months. Retail buyers will discount for them anyway. A cash buyer purchases as-is. Run both numbers: fixed-up retail price minus repairs, commission, and carrying costs, versus an as-is cash offer today.

How much will a cash buyer pay for a probate house in Dallas?

Typically 70-85% of the house's as-is market value, depending on condition, location, and complexity. The discount reflects repair costs, carrying costs during the hold, and the buyer's margin. On a clean house in a strong neighborhood, listing with an agent usually nets more. On a house needing major work or with heirs in conflict, the cash offer often nets the same or better after costs and time.
