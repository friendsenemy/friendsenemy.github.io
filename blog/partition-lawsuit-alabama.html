Partition Lawsuit in Alabama: Probate Judge or Circuit Court, and What Happens After You File

Alabama lets a co-owner ask the county probate judge, not only a circuit judge, to divide land or sell it for division, but the probate route stops cold the moment anyone asserts an adverse claim to title. Which courthouse you start in, and one sentence in your first pleading, shape everything after. Ask us a question about your Alabama property free, and we'll give you a straight answer.

By Dustin Ray, Founder of Pages of Purpose LLC. Not legal advice — Alabama statutes and county court practice change, laws differ by state, and every situation is different. Before you sign or file anything, talk to a real estate litigation attorney licensed in Alabama, where the property is.

Most guides to partition treat "the court" as one place. In Alabama there are two doors. Title 35, Chapter 6 of the Code of Alabama gives the circuit court a partition article and gives the county probate court a separate one, with its own application, its own commissioners and its own way of running a sale. Picking the wrong door is one of the more expensive early mistakes a family can make, because the probate version has a built-in off switch that the circuit version does not.

This page is about the lawsuit itself: where it gets filed, what the papers have to say, who values and divides the land, how a court-ordered sale is run, and which bills come out of the common pot. If you are still deciding whether to sue at all, our guides to selling a co-owned house in Alabama and Alabama heirs who can't agree cover deeds, survivorship and the family buyout in more depth. For how partition works nationally, start with partition lawsuits explained.

🏛️ Alabama Lets You Ask the Probate Judge, Until Someone Disputes Title

Section 35-6-40 says property held by joint owners or tenants in common "may be divided among them, on the written application of one or more of them to the probate court of the county in which the property is situated." The application can also come from the executor or administrator of a deceased co-owner, or from the guardian of a minor. That is unusual. In most of the Southeast, partition belongs to the general trial court or a chancery court. In Alabama, the elected judge of probate, the same official whose office records your deed, can hear it.

The probate article moves on a fixed, fairly quick clock. Under Section 35-6-43, once the application is filed the court sets a hearing at least 30 days out, and everyone with an interest must get at least 10 days' notice. If the judge is satisfied the property is jointly owned, Section 35-6-45 requires a decree of partition and the appointment of "suitable commissioners, not more than five, any three of whom may act." If the commissioners report that a fair division can't be made, Section 35-6-57 lets the court order a sale instead.

Here is the off switch. Section 35-6-55 says no division, partition or sale for distribution can be made in probate court "when an adverse claim or title is asserted by anyone, or brought to the knowledge of the commissioners, or of the judge of probate." One relative who says he owns the whole tract by adverse possession, one disputed deed from 1987, one heir who claims a bigger share than the others accept, and the probate judge can no longer finish the job.

The circuit court has no such limit. Section 35-6-20 gives it original jurisdiction to divide or sell co-owned property "whether the defendant denies the title of plaintiff or sets up adverse possession or not," and Section 35-6-23 lets the circuit judge try every title question, remove clouds on title, apportion mortgages and other liens, and adjust the equities among the owners in the same case. That is why contested Alabama partitions end up in circuit court even though the probate route exists.

⚠️ Before you file in probate court, ask whether anyone could claim title against you. If a co-owner, a long-term occupant or a distant branch of the family might say "that land is mine, not ours," Section 35-6-55 can halt a probate partition after you have already paid for notice, a hearing and commissioners. In that situation, file in circuit court from the start and let the judge try the title question inside the partition case.

⚖️ Probate Court and Circuit Court, Compared Line by Line

Neither forum is better in the abstract. The probate route suits a quiet family where everyone agrees on who owns what and simply needs a court to divide or sell. The circuit route suits a fight. Here is how the two statutory tracks differ on the points that usually matter.

The probate article also says plainly, in Section 35-6-56, that it does not prevent "a resort to any other lawful mode of obtaining partition." So starting in probate court does not lock you out of circuit court later; it may simply cost you the time and money spent getting there.

🗂️ A Circuit Court Partition From Complaint to Distribution

Because most contested Alabama cases land here, it helps to see the sequence in order. Every circuit has its own scheduling habits, so treat the timing as a range.

  1. Complaint and filing fee. The plaintiff files in the circuit court of the county where the land sits. Partition complaints usually don't state a dollar amount, so they fall into the higher filing tier. In the Birmingham Division of Jefferson County, the circuit clerk's posted fee for one plaintiff and one defendant over $50,000 or an unspecified amount is $351, with additional charges for extra defendants and sheriff service. Other counties post slightly different totals.
  2. Lis pendens. Section 35-4-131 requires a party suing over an interest in land to file a notice of the action with the judge of probate in each county where the land lies. Once it is recorded, any buyer or lender takes subject to the case.
  3. Service on every owner. Section 35-6-20 tells the circuit court to follow the Rules of Civil Procedure, including service by publication where necessary. Old family land often has owners who have died or moved away, and finding them is frequently the longest step.
  4. Answers. Under Alabama Rule of Civil Procedure 12(a), a defendant has 30 days after being served to answer. A defendant who says nothing can be defaulted, but read the next section before assuming default helps the plaintiff.
  5. Heirs property question. Under Section 35-6A-3(b), the court must decide whether the land is "heirs property." If it is, the 2014 Alabama Uniform Partition of Heirs Property Act takes over wherever it conflicts with the older chapter.
  6. Value. For heirs property, Section 35-6A-6 has the court appoint a disinterested licensed appraiser, send notice of the appraised value within 10 days after the appraisal is filed, allow 30 days for objections, and hold a value hearing before reaching the merits.
  7. Buyout windows. The heirs property act and Article 4A of the partition chapter each give co-owners a chance to buy out the people who want a sale. The details and deadlines are on our Alabama heirs page; what matters here is that both can end the case before any division or sale.
  8. Division or sale. If nobody buys, the court decides between splitting the land and selling it, appoints commissioners or a broker, and supervises the result.
  9. Distribution. Costs come off the top, credits among owners are applied, and the balance is paid by share. Under Section 35-6-1, money belonging to an owner who is a nonresident or unknown and doesn't come forward goes into the county treasury until that person proves the right to it.

Realistic timing: a case where everyone is found quickly and nobody fights the value can finish in six to ten months. A case with unserved heirs, a value objection and a contested in-kind question commonly runs 14 to 24 months, longer with an appeal to the Alabama Supreme Court.

📝 The Sentence in Your First Pleading That Heirs Cases Turn On

Alabama's version of the heirs property act contains a procedural rule that is easy to miss and hard to fix later. Section 35-6A-7(a) says each petitioner "shall state whether the petitioner requests partition by sale," and a petitioner who fails to say so "shall be deemed to have not requested partition by sale." Subsection (b) puts the same duty on every co-owner who files an answer, with the same consequence for silence.

Why that sentence carries so much weight:

  • It decides who can be bought out. The buyout under the heirs act reaches only the co-owners who asked for a sale. Ask for one and your share is on the table at the court's value. Stay silent and it isn't.
  • It can end the case with nothing. Under Section 35-6A-7(d), if no co-owner, including the plaintiff, has requested partition by sale, the court looks only at dividing the land. If the land can't be split, the court must dismiss the action without prejudice. A plaintiff who wanted a sale but left the request out of the complaint can spend months and an appraisal fee to end up where they started.
  • Defaulting owners are treated as wanting to keep the land. Under Section 35-6A-7(c), a co-owner who was served but never appears is deemed not to have requested a sale. The other owners then have only 14 days after the court's buyout notice to ask the court to treat that absent owner's share as available for purchase, and the court decides after a hearing whether to allow it.
  • The election window is short. Once notice of value goes out, the owners who did not request a sale have 30 days to elect to buy, and the court sets a payment date at least 60 days after that window closes.

The practical point is simple. Whether you are filing or answering, have your Alabama lawyer state your position on sale in the first paper, in plain words, and calendar the 14-day and 30-day dates the day the value notice arrives.

🌳 Splitting the Land Itself: Commissioners, Owelty and a Fairhope Case

Alabama courts take division in kind seriously, and that matters more here than in states where nearly every case ends in a sale. A lot of Alabama co-owned property is not a single house on a city lot. It is a few acres with a house at the road, a tract of timber, or a family homeplace with lots carved out over the years.

Three statutes control how the land gets split:

  • Commissioners. In a probate case, Section 35-6-45 calls for up to five commissioners, any three of whom may act. When the heirs property act applies, Section 35-6A-5 adds that each commissioner must be disinterested, impartial and not a party to or participant in the case.
  • Owelty. Under Section 35-6-24, the circuit court can skip commissioners entirely if it can make an equal division by ordering one owner to pay another a sum to even out the shares. That cash adjustment is called owelty. It is how a court can give one sibling the house parcel and another the larger back acreage without forcing a sale.
  • Annulment for fraud. A probate partition vests fee simple title in each owner's allotted share as if they had deeded it to each other. Under Section 35-6-50, if fraud or undue influence produced an unfair allotment, the circuit court can annul it on a complaint filed within five years.

The Alabama Supreme Court showed how hard it can be to get a sale of land in Washington v. Johnson (2024). Two sisters sued their brother to sell 2.8 unimproved acres in Fairhope, arguing the L-shaped parcel had only about 87 feet of road frontage and the back lots would be landlocked. The brother came to trial with a plan showing separate lots and an easement for access. The trial court found the sisters had not proved the land couldn't be fairly divided, and the Supreme Court agreed, sending the case back with instructions to actually order the partition in kind. The court repeated two rules worth remembering: the party asking for a sale for division carries the burden of proving the property can't be equitably divided, and under the heirs act a partition in kind is required unless it would cause "great prejudice" to the owners.

For a single-family house on a quarter-acre lot, none of this changes much; a sale is still the likely outcome. For rural or suburban acreage, the owner who wants a sale should come to trial with a surveyor's opinion and a broker's analysis of what the split parcels would be worth, not just an argument.

🔨 How an Alabama Court Sale Is Actually Run

"The court ordered a sale" can mean very different things in Alabama, depending on which statute governs.

In a probate partition, Section 35-6-62 has the judge appoint a commissioner to make the sale, and the sale, collection of the money and the deed are handled "in every respect as is done" when an executor or administrator sells property for distribution. Under Section 35-6-63, the commissioner pays the money to the judge of probate after deducting sale costs and a commission at the executor's rate, capped by statute at $100, and the probate judge distributes the net proceeds by share.

For heirs property, Section 35-6A-10 lets the court choose an open-market sale with or without a broker, sealed bids, a public auction or a private sale, whichever is "more economically advantageous" for the owners as a group. If the court picks an open-market sale and the owners agree on a broker within 10 days, the court appoints that broker; otherwise it appoints a disinterested Alabama broker. The listing price can't go below the court's value without good cause. If no buyer at that price appears within a reasonable time, capped at 180 days, the court can accept the best offer, extend the listing, or switch to sealed bids or an auction. An auction, unless the parties agree otherwise, is held at the front door of the county courthouse between 11 a.m. and 4 p.m. A co-owner who buys gets credit against the price for their own share of the proceeds.

That last rule is worth noticing. A co-owner with a one-third share who wins the property at a court sale pays only the other two-thirds in cash, which can make bidding realistic for a family member who couldn't afford a full buyout.

💳 Fees Taxed as Costs: What the Common Pot Pays and What You Pay

Section 34-3-60 lets the probate court or circuit court set a reasonable attorney's fee and tax it as part of the costs in a sale for distribution or a partition in kind, paid like other costs. In a partition in kind, that fee becomes a lien on each owner's allotted piece. The heirs property act expressly leaves this rule in place: Section 35-6A-3(d) says the act does not change fees as costs under Section 34-3-60.

What that means in a real case:

  • Work that benefits the property (drafting the complaint, getting the decree, running the sale, distributing the money) is the kind of fee courts tax as costs and pay from the proceeds before the split.
  • Fighting over who gets what (credits for taxes paid, a title contest, a value objection) is usually on each owner's own bill.
  • Court-appointed help such as the appraiser, commissioners, a surveyor and a guardian ad litem for minors or unknown heirs adds to the costs. The statutory commissioner pay in Section 35-6-53 is still written as $2 per day plus expenses, so in practice the larger numbers are the survey, appraisal and attorney time.

Rough planning ranges for an Alabama residential partition: $351 or so to file in a large county, $400 to $900 for a residential appraisal, $1,500 to $5,000 for a boundary survey if division is in play, and attorney fees that commonly run $5,000 to $12,000 per side for a cooperative case and $15,000 to $40,000 or more per side when title or division is contested. Ask your lawyer which parts of their bill they will seek to tax as costs.

🧮 A Birmingham House, Priced Three Ways

Take a 1950s brick house inside the City of Birmingham, owned in equal halves by two cousins who inherited it from their grandmother. Fully updated it would sell for about $210,000; today it needs a roof, a panel upgrade and cosmetic work, roughly $30,000. Nobody lives there. One cousin wants out; the other ignores letters.

Carrying cost while it sits. A house no owner occupies is assessed in Alabama at 20% of value (Class II) rather than the 10% for an owner-occupied home. The Department of Revenue's October 2025 millage book lists 6.5 state, 13.5 county, 24 school and 28.5 city mills for Birmingham, about 72.5 mills in total. On a current value near $175,000, that's $35,000 assessed times 0.0725, or about $2,540 a year, roughly $210 a month. Add a vacant-dwelling policy at $125 to $200 a month, $120 for utilities kept on, and about $100 for lawn and upkeep. Call it $555 to $630 a month, or $6,700 to $7,600 a year.

Path 1: a contested circuit case ending in a court-supervised listing. Sixteen months from filing to closing. The house lists at the court's value and sells as-is for $180,000. Subtract 6% commission ($10,800), about $2,500 in closing costs, $14,000 in fees taxed as costs (plaintiff's lawyer for the common work, appraisal, guardian ad litem for a missing heir), and about $9,600 in carrying costs. Net to divide: about $143,100, or $71,550 each, before each cousin pays their own lawyer for the contested parts. At $6,000 apiece, each nets about $65,500.

Path 2: settle and list together in month two. The non-responsive cousin answers once served and agrees to list as-is. A buyer pays $185,000 four months later. Subtract commission ($11,100), $2,500 closing, about $2,000 in combined legal costs to dismiss, and $3,500 in carrying costs. Net: about $165,900, or $82,950 each. This is the best outcome if the two can cooperate.

Path 3: a cash sale of the whole house. Cash buyers usually pay 60% to 80% of repaired value, which here is $126,000 to $168,000. A typical offer near 70% is about $147,000, closing in three weeks. Subtract about $1,500 in legal and closing costs and $600 in carrying. Net: about $144,900, or $72,450 each. That beats a fought-out lawsuit by several thousand dollars per cousin and closes a year sooner, but it loses to a cooperative listing by more than $10,000 each.

The numbers are illustrations, not quotes. Millage differs by district inside Jefferson County, and Bessemer Division filings, suburban school districts and unincorporated areas carry different rates.

🧭 Four Moves, Depending on Where Your Case Stands

Probate application, everyone agrees on shares

Cheapest court route if nobody disputes title

Works for a family that agrees who owns what and wants a court to divide acreage or authorize a sale. Quick hearing schedule, local judge, modest cost. Abandon it the moment an adverse claim surfaces, because Section 35-6-55 won't let the probate judge finish.

Circuit complaint with a clear sale request

The right tool for a real dispute

Handles title fights, liens, missing heirs and credits in one case. State your request for a sale in the complaint if you want one, and bring evidence that the land can't be fairly split if it's more than a single house lot.

Buy at the court sale with your share as credit

Underused by family members

In a heirs property sale, a co-owner who buys is credited with their own share of the proceeds, so the cash needed is only the others' portion. Pair it with a lender pre-approval before the sale date.

Sell your undivided share and step out

Fastest exit, lowest price

You can sell your fractional interest at any point, before or during the case. The buyer inherits the lawsuit, which is why a share sells for well under its slice of full value. It makes sense when you can't fund a year of fees or carrying costs.

📋 What To Do This Week

  1. Pull the deed and the chain of title from the probate office in the county where the land sits, and list every person who might hold a share.
  2. Write down anyone who could plausibly claim the property adversely: an occupant of many years, a relative with an unrecorded deed, a disputed boundary. If the list isn't empty, plan on circuit court.
  3. Decide, with your lawyer, whether you want a sale or a division, and make sure the first pleading says so in plain words.
  4. Get a broker opinion or appraisal, and for acreage, ask a surveyor whether the land can be split with road access for each piece.
  5. Gather proof of tax, insurance, mortgage and repair payments by each owner for the credit accounting.
  6. If you might buy out the others, talk to a lender now; the court's payment deadline won't wait for underwriting.
  7. Book a consultation with an Alabama real estate litigator who handles partitions in that county, and ask which fees they will seek to tax as costs.

✅ Bottom Line

An Alabama partition starts with a choice most states don't offer: the probate judge or the circuit court. The probate route is quick and inexpensive for families that agree on ownership, and useless the moment anyone asserts an adverse title. The circuit route handles every fight but costs more. If the land is heirs property, say clearly in your first paper whether you want a sale, because silence counts as "no," and a case where nobody asks for a sale on land that can't be split simply gets dismissed. Expect Alabama judges to take division in kind seriously on acreage, expect fees for the common work to come out of the proceeds, and run the numbers on a cooperative listing before you file. If the house needs work or the lawsuit would cost more than it recovers, ask us for an honest number.

❓ Frequently Asked Questions

Can I file an Alabama partition in probate court instead of circuit court?

Yes. Section 35-6-40 of the Code of Alabama lets one or more joint owners or tenants in common apply in writing to the probate court of the county where the property is located, and an executor, administrator or guardian can apply for the person they represent. The probate route works best when everyone agrees on ownership. If anyone asserts an adverse claim or title, Section 35-6-55 bars the probate court from dividing or selling, and the case needs to be in circuit court.

What happens if a relative claims he owns the whole property by adverse possession?

In probate court, that claim stops the partition under Section 35-6-55. In circuit court, it doesn't: Section 35-6-20 gives the circuit court jurisdiction whether or not the defendant denies the plaintiff's title or sets up adverse possession, and Section 35-6-23 lets the judge try the title question inside the partition case and then go on to divide or sell.

Does Alabama's heirs property act apply to a house our parents left us?

Often, yes. The Alabama Uniform Partition of Heirs Property Act is Chapter 6A of Title 35 and applies to partition actions filed on or after January 1, 2015. It covers property held as tenants in common without a binding partition agreement where at least one owner took title from a relative and one of the act's 20 percent family-ownership tests is met. The court must decide whether the property qualifies, and if it does, the act controls unless all the co-owners agree otherwise in writing.

What if my complaint doesn't say I want the property sold?

Under Section 35-6A-7(a), a petitioner who doesn't state whether they request partition by sale is treated as not having requested it. If no co-owner requests a sale and the court finds the land can't be divided in kind, Section 35-6A-7(d) requires the court to dismiss the case without prejudice. Have your lawyer state your position in the first pleading.

A co-owner was served but never answered. Can the court still sell that person's share?

Under the heirs property act, a served co-owner who doesn't appear is deemed not to have requested a sale. Within 14 days after the court's buyout notice, any co-owner entitled to buy can ask the court to make the absent owner's interest available for purchase, and the court decides after a hearing. Outside the heirs act, an owner who defaults can still lose the property to a court-ordered sale under the general partition chapter.

Who decides whether Alabama land gets divided or sold?

The judge, often with help from court-appointed commissioners. The Alabama Supreme Court has held that the party seeking a sale for division must prove the property can't be equitably divided. In Washington v. Johnson (2024), it affirmed a finding that 2.8 acres in Fairhope could be split and sent the case back for an order of partition in kind, even though the sisters who sued wanted a sale.

Is every Alabama partition sale an auction on the courthouse steps?

No. For heirs property, Section 35-6A-10 lets the court choose an open-market sale with or without a broker, sealed bids, an auction or a private sale, whichever is better for the owners as a group, and a broker listing can't go below the court's value without good cause. If an auction is ordered, it is normally held at the courthouse front door between 11 a.m. and 4 p.m. In a probate partition sale, a court-appointed commissioner sells the property the way an estate administrator would.

Are lawyers' fees in an Alabama partition paid out of the sale money?

Partly. Section 34-3-60 lets the probate or circuit court set a reasonable attorney's fee and tax it as part of the costs in a sale for distribution or partition in kind, and in a partition in kind the fee becomes a lien on each owner's piece. Fees for work that benefits the common property are typically paid from the proceeds before the split, while fees for fights between owners usually stay with each owner.

What does it cost to file a partition complaint in Birmingham?

The Jefferson County circuit clerk in the Birmingham Division posts a $351 filing fee for one plaintiff and one defendant in a civil case over $50,000 or for an unspecified amount, which is how most partition complaints are filed, plus charges for extra defendants and service. Appraisal, survey, guardian ad litem and attorney fees are the bigger costs.

Can a completed partition in kind be undone in Alabama?

Only in narrow cases. A probate partition vests fee simple title in each owner's allotted share, but Section 35-6-50 lets the circuit court annul the partition if fraud or undue influence was used to obtain an unfair division, as long as the complaint is filed within five years after the allotment.
