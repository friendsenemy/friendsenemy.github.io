Heirs Can't Agree on a California House? An Objection Moves the Sale Into a Courtroom

In a lot of states, one heir who says no can freeze an inherited house for years. In a California probate, an heir who objects usually can't veto the sale. What they can do is force it into a court confirmation hearing, where the judge reviews the price and any qualified buyer in the room can outbid the contract you signed. Add Prop 19, which changed the math on one sibling keeping the house, and California family disputes play out very differently than they do anywhere else.

By Dustin Ray, Founder of Pages of Purpose LLC. Not legal advice. California probate, property tax and partition rules are technical, they change often, and laws differ by state. Before anyone files, objects, signs a purchase agreement or changes title, talk to an attorney licensed in California, where the property is.

Here's a typical California version of the story. A parent dies owning a house in Sacramento, Riverside or San Jose. There's no trust, so the house goes through probate. One sibling is appointed executor (California calls the role personal representative) and wants to sell. Another sibling wants to move in and keep it. A third lives out of state and mostly wants the fighting to stop. The executor signs a purchase contract. The sibling who wants the house sends an objection. Everyone assumes the sale is dead.

Usually it isn't. In California, the way the sale happens changes, not whether it can happen. Understanding that difference, and the property tax rule that now makes "let my brother keep it" far more expensive than it used to be, is most of what a California family needs to get unstuck.

🔨 In California, an Objecting Heir Doesn't Stop the Sale. It Sends It to Court.

Most California executors and administrators sell estate real estate under the Independent Administration of Estates Act (IAEA), which starts at Probate Code Section 10400. With "full authority" under the IAEA, the personal representative can sell the house without a court hearing, but only after giving heirs and beneficiaries a Notice of Proposed Action. The notice describes the sale and its price, and it has to be delivered at least 15 days before the action is taken.

That 15-day window is where a disagreeing heir has power. Under Section 10589, if someone entitled to the notice objects, the personal representative can't use the no-hearing route for that sale. Instead, the sale has to go through court supervision, which in practice means a confirmation hearing in the probate department of the county's superior court. A personal representative with only limited IAEA authority has to take every sale through confirmation anyway.

Confirmation is a public process with its own rules:

  • 📏 A price floor. Under Section 10309, the sale price generally has to be at least 90% of the property's appraised value, based on an appraisal by the court's probate referee made within a year before the hearing.
  • 📣 Open overbidding in court. At the hearing, anyone can show up and outbid the contract buyer. Under Section 10311, the first overbid has to beat the original price by at least 10% of the first $10,000 plus 5% of the rest. On a $500,000 contract, that's $1,000 plus $24,500, so the first overbid must be at least $525,500. The judge then takes further bids in increments the court sets.
  • ⚖️ The judge decides. The court confirms the sale to the highest qualified bidder, which may not be the buyer the executor signed with.
What this means for a family fight: an objecting California heir generally can't veto a probate sale by saying no. They can slow it by several weeks to a few months while a hearing is calendared, and they can make sure the price is tested in open court. If they want to stop the sale entirely, they generally need a separate petition to the court, or to remove the executor, and they need a reason the judge will accept. "I want the house" usually isn't one, unless they're also offering to pay for it.

🧮 What a Courtroom Overbid Does to Price, Time and Buyers

For heirs who are fighting about price rather than about whether to sell, court confirmation can actually help. The objecting sibling who thinks the house is worth more gets a referee appraisal, a 90% floor and an open auction against the contract price. If no one overbids, that's fairly strong evidence the price was right, and the judge's order ends the argument.

The costs are real, though:

  • 📅 Time. Confirmation hearings in busy counties such as Los Angeles, Orange and Alameda are often set four to eight weeks out, sometimes longer, and a continuance adds more. Escrow can't close until the order is signed.
  • 🏦 Fewer buyers. Many retail buyers with mortgage financing don't want to wait weeks for a hearing only to be overbid in a courtroom. Overbidders generally have to bring a deposit and be ready to close on the court's terms. Probate sales are sold as-is, and the buyer pool tilts toward investors and buyers with ready cash.
  • 💼 Fees. A contested hearing means more attorney time, and the extra hours come out of everyone's share.

One way families avoid the hearing is simple: get every heir to sign a waiver of the Notice of Proposed Action or a written consent to the specific sale. If the objecting heir's real issue is price, an independent appraisal or a second listing agent's opinion is often cheaper than a hearing.

🏷️ Prop 19: Why "Just Let My Brother Keep It" Got Expensive

The second California-specific fight is about property tax, and it's often the real reason one heir wants to keep the house. Under Prop 13, the parent's house was taxed on a base value that may date to the 1980s or 1990s, often a fraction of today's market value. Before 2021, children could generally inherit that low base. Proposition 19, which took effect for transfers on or after February 16, 2021, narrowed that sharply.

Under Prop 19, as county assessors and the Board of Equalization describe it:

  • 🏠 The child has to live there. The parent's low base carries over only if a child makes the house their principal residence within one year of the transfer and files for the homeowners' exemption within that year.
  • 💲 There's a value cap. The excluded amount is limited to the parent's factored base value plus $1 million, adjusted for inflation. Above that, the excess is added to the taxable value.
  • 👥 With several children, one occupant is enough for the exclusion to apply.
  • 🔁 Siblings buying each other out are not excluded. There has never been a parent-child-style exclusion for transfers between brothers and sisters. When the sibling who moved in buys out the others, the shares they buy are generally reassessed to market value.

Put that together and the classic California deadlock looks like this. One sibling moves in within the year, keeps the low base on the property, and then can't afford to buy out the others at market value, or can buy them out only by accepting a reassessment on the shares they buy. The siblings who don't live there own a share of a house that produces no income for them. The sibling living there has every reason to stall. If you're one of the siblings who wants out, the tax savings belong to the one who moved in, not to you.

Run the actual numbers with a California CPA or attorney before anyone moves in, buys out a share or records a deed. Changes in title are what trigger reassessment, and some mistakes can't be unwound.

💸 California's Probate Fees Are Set by Statute and Based on Gross Value

Few states set probate fees by formula. California does, and it's a big part of why heirs fight over whether to sell, and when. Under Probate Code Section 10810, the attorney's ordinary fee is 4% of the first $100,000 of the estate, 3% of the next $100,000, 2% of the next $800,000, and 1% of the next $9 million. The personal representative is entitled to the same amount under Section 10800, though family members often waive it. Both are calculated on the estate's appraised value, not its equity, so a mortgage doesn't reduce them.

On an estate appraised at $800,000, that's $4,000 plus $3,000 plus $12,000, or $19,000 for the attorney and another $19,000 for the executor if they take it, before the court filing fees, referee fee and bond. Heirs who are already suspicious of the sibling serving as executor often fight about this number more than any other.

There are ways around full probate for some estates. A surviving spouse can often use a spousal property petition under Section 13650 for community property. And when the death occurred on or after April 1, 2025, a primary residence worth up to $750,000 can pass to heirs through a simpler petition to determine succession under Section 13151, without a full administration. Ask a California probate attorney whether either fits before the family pays for a full estate. Neither helps much if the heirs can't agree on what happens after the title passes to them.

⚖️ After Probate Closes: Partition Under California's Partition Act

Once probate distributes the house, the heirs typically own it as tenants in common. From that point, the probate court is out of the picture, and the tool for a disagreeing co-owner is a partition action in superior court under Code of Civil Procedure Section 872.010 and the sections that follow.

Since January 1, 2023, partition cases involving tenants in common in California generally follow the Partition of Real Property Act, Sections 874.311 through 874.323. It began as California's version of the Uniform Partition of Heirs Property Act and now reaches most co-owned property held as tenants in common without a written partition agreement. The key steps:

  1. 🧮 Court-set value. Under Section 874.316, unless the owners agree on value, the court appoints an appraiser, and any party can object within 30 days of notice.
  2. 🤝 Buyout first. Under Section 874.317, co-owners who didn't ask for a sale get 45 days to elect to buy the interest of the owner who did, at the appraised value times that owner's share, with at least 60 days to pay.
  3. 🏡 In kind preferred, rarely practical. The court weighs factors under Section 874.319 before ordering a sale. For a single house, division is usually impractical.
  4. 🏷️ Open-market sale. Under Section 874.320, a court-ordered sale generally goes on the open market through a licensed broker, not a courthouse auction.

A contested California partition commonly takes 12 to 24 months and can cost $20,000 to $60,000 or more in combined attorney fees, appraisal and referee costs, often split among the owners in proportion to their shares. 🧩 Ways California Families Get Past the Deadlock

✍️ Get written consent to a specific sale

Fastest when the fight is about price, not about selling

A signed consent or waiver of the Notice of Proposed Action lets the executor close without a hearing. A second appraisal or a competing broker opinion is usually enough to bring a skeptical sibling around, and costs far less than a contested hearing.

🏛️ Let the court confirm it

Best when trust has broken down

If an heir won't trust anything the executor does, a confirmation hearing gives everyone a neutral price test. The overbid process means nobody can claim the house was given away. It costs weeks and fees, and it favors buyers with cash.

💵 The sibling who wants it buys it from the estate

Workable if they can finance it and understand the tax

A sibling can buy the house from the estate, or take it as part of their share with an equalizing payment to the others. Model the Prop 19 effect first. A buyout can make sense, but only with real numbers in front of everyone.

🏷️ Sell your share after distribution

For the heir who needs out and can accept a discount

Once the house is distributed, each heir can sell their undivided interest without the others' consent. The price is well below a proportional share, because the buyer takes on the co-owners and the possibility of a partition case.

⚖️ File for partition

The end of the line, not the start

After probate, any co-owner can file. The Partition Act gives the others a buyout window first. Many cases settle once the appraisal comes in.

💵 Carrying Costs and a Worked Example

While the family argues, someone pays. California property tax is about 1% of assessed value plus local voter-approved charges, so most bills run roughly 1.1% to 1.25% of assessed value. When an inherited house is reassessed at market value after the death, the bill can jump several times over. Vacant-home insurance is harder to find in California's current market and often costs $200 to $450 a month, more in wildfire zones. Utilities, yard care and basic upkeep add $250 to $500.

Example. A three-bedroom house in Sacramento would be worth about $520,000 updated and needs $60,000 of work: roof, electrical panel, kitchen. Reassessed at around $440,000 as-is, taxes at 1.15% are about $420 a month. Add $300 for insurance and $350 for utilities and upkeep, and the estate spends about $1,070 a month.

A cash buyer typically pays 60% to 80% of repaired value, depending on condition and complexity. On this house, a realistic cash range is roughly $350,000 to $375,000. Listed as-is, it might sell for $440,000. Take off about 6% in commission and closing costs ($26,400) and five months of carrying costs ($5,350), and the family nets around $408,000. That's $30,000 to $55,000 more than a cash sale. If every heir will consent and wait a few months, list it. The cash option earns its place when the house needs $100,000 of work, when a confirmation hearing would scare off retail buyers, or when the family would otherwise spend two years and $40,000 in a partition case.

We buy houses, and we'll still tell you not to sell to us in these cases: the heirs have agreed or can be brought to agree with a second appraisal; the house is in decent condition in a strong market; the estate has time and some cash to carry it; or one sibling genuinely wants the house and can finance a fair price. In those situations, a listing agent experienced in probate sales will usually net the family more.

A cash buyer makes sense when a court confirmation is likely anyway, when the house needs major work, when carrying costs are running down the estate, or when one heir needs to sell a share and the others won't move. 📋 What To Do This Week

  1. 🔎 Find out whether there's a trust. A house in a living trust skips probate entirely, and the trustee's powers come from the trust document, not the Probate Code.
  2. 📂 Get the case number and the letters. If probate is open, look up the case on the county superior court's site and check whether the personal representative has full or limited IAEA authority.
  3. 📨 Read any Notice of Proposed Action the day it arrives. The 15-day clock is short. Object in writing on the proper form if you mean to, or sign a consent if you agree.
  4. 🏷️ Before anyone moves in, call a CPA about Prop 19. The one-year occupancy window and the homeowners' exemption filing are easy to miss.
  5. 🧮 Get the referee appraisal and an agent's opinion. Comparing them usually ends half of all price arguments.
  6. 🛡️ Insure the vacant house. Confirm the estate has coverage that applies to an empty property.
  7. ⚖️ Compare a listing net to a cash offer after fees, time and the likelihood of a hearing, not just the headline price.

✅ Bottom Line

In California, a sibling who objects to selling an inherited house during probate usually can't stop the sale. They can push it into a confirmation hearing where a judge checks the price and anyone can outbid. The real leverage in California family disputes comes from elsewhere: Prop 19, which rewards the sibling who moves in and penalizes sibling buyouts, and statutory fees that make a long probate expensive. Get consent if you can, let the court confirm if you can't, and model the property tax before anyone changes title. If the house is sound and the family agrees, list it. ❓ Frequently Asked Questions

Can one heir stop a probate sale in California?

Usually not by objecting alone. Under Probate Code Section 10589, an heir who objects to a Notice of Proposed Action forces the sale into court supervision, which means a confirmation hearing. The judge can still approve the sale. Stopping it entirely generally takes a separate petition and a reason the court accepts.

What is a Notice of Proposed Action in California probate?

It is a notice the personal representative sends to heirs and beneficiaries before taking certain actions, such as selling real estate, under the Independent Administration of Estates Act. It must be delivered at least 15 days before the action. Recipients can consent, waive the notice, or object in writing.

What happens at a probate court confirmation hearing?

The judge reviews the sale, checks that the price is generally at least 90% of the probate referee's appraised value, and opens the floor to overbids. Anyone qualified can outbid the contract buyer, and the court confirms the sale to the highest acceptable bidder. Escrow closes only after the order is signed.

How is the minimum overbid calculated in a California probate sale?

Under Probate Code Section 10311, the first overbid must exceed the contract price by at least 10% of the first $10,000 plus 5% of the remainder. On a $500,000 contract, the first overbid must be at least $525,500. Later bids follow increments the judge sets.

Does Prop 19 let us keep Mom's low property tax base?

Only if a child makes the house their principal residence within one year of the transfer and files for the homeowners' exemption. The exclusion is capped at the parent's factored base value plus $1 million, adjusted for inflation. If no child moves in, the house is reassessed to market value.

If I buy out my siblings, will my property taxes go up?

Generally yes, on the shares you buy. Transfers between siblings are not covered by the parent-child exclusion, so the interests you acquire from brothers or sisters are typically reassessed to market value even if you kept your parent's base on your own inherited share. Talk to a California CPA before recording any deed.

How much are probate fees in California?

The attorney's ordinary fee under Probate Code Section 10810 is 4% of the first $100,000, 3% of the next $100,000, 2% of the next $800,000 and 1% of the next $9 million, calculated on the estate's appraised value rather than its equity. The personal representative is entitled to the same amount, though family members often waive it. An $800,000 estate means $19,000 for each.

Can we avoid probate on a house worth under $750,000?

Possibly. For deaths on or after April 1, 2025, Probate Code Section 13151 allows a petition to determine succession for a decedent's primary residence worth up to $750,000, which is much simpler than a full administration. A surviving spouse may instead use a spousal property petition for community property. A California probate attorney can tell you whether either fits.

Can I force my siblings to sell after probate closes?

Yes, by filing a partition action in superior court. Since 2023, most partitions of property held as tenants in common follow California's Partition of Real Property Act. The court sets a value, gives the co-owners who did not ask for a sale 45 days to elect to buy out the one who did, and orders an open-market sale through a broker if no buyout happens.

Should our family list the inherited house or take a cash offer?

If every heir will consent, the house is in decent shape and the estate can carry it for a few months, listing with an experienced probate agent usually nets more. A cash offer makes more sense when the house needs major work, when a confirmation hearing is likely to scare off retail buyers, or when the alternative is a long partition case.
