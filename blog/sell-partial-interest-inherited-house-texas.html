Selling Your Share of an Inherited Texas House: Start With the Price Chapter 23A Would Set

You can sell your undivided share of an inherited Texas house without your siblings' permission. The harder question is what it's worth. Texas law gives an unusually clear answer: if the share ever ends up in a partition case covered by the Heirs Property Act, the family can buy it out at the court-determined value of the whole house times your fraction, with no discount. That number should anchor every conversation you have, with your family and with any outside buyer, including us.

People who call us about selling "just my part" of an inherited Texas house usually have one of two problems. Either the family won't sell and they need their money, or the family will sell someday, but someday keeps moving. Texas law lets any heir sell their undivided interest on their own. What it doesn't do is tell you what that interest is worth to a stranger. This guide works through that question the Texas way, starting from the number the law itself would use.

🧮 Chapter 23A Sets the Benchmark for Your Share, and Gives Your Family First Shot at It

Texas adopted the Uniform Partition of Heirs' Property Act in 2017, in Chapter 23A of the Property Code. It covers most inherited family houses: property held as tenants in common, with no written agreement about partition, where at least one owner inherited from a relative and relatives hold at least 20% of the interests or make up at least 20% of the owners. If your parent died and left the house to you and your siblings, it very likely qualifies.

Two parts of the Act matter most to someone selling a share:

  • 📐 Court-set value. Under Section 23A.006, when a partition case is filed, the court determines the fair market value of the whole property, usually through an appraisal.
  • 🤝 A buyout at full proportional value. Under Section 23A.007, if an owner asks for a partition sale, the other owners can elect within 45 days to buy that owner's interest at the court-determined value of the whole property multiplied by that owner's fractional share. There's no discount for owning a fraction instead of the whole.

That second point is unusual, and it shapes your options. If you own a quarter of a house the court values at $240,000, the statutory buyout price for your quarter is $60,000. Your siblings can't be forced to pay it. But if they want to keep the house and the case gets that far, that's what it costs them. Before you take a discounted offer from anyone, ask whether your family would rather pay you the 23A number now than face a partition later.

Before you sell to an outsider: put a written offer to your co-owners first, at an appraised value times your share. If they say yes, you'll almost always get more than an investor would pay. If they ignore it, you've documented that you tried, which helps you in any later partition, and you lose nothing.

🏷️ Why an Outside Buyer Still Pays Less Than Your Fraction

If the law values your share at full proportion, why does every investor offer less? Because the 23A price is what your share is worth at the end of a lawsuit, and an outside buyer has to get there. A buyer of an undivided interest in Texas is pricing in:

  • ⏳ Time. A contested partition commonly takes 6 to 18 months, and the buyer's money is tied up the whole time.
  • ⚖️ Legal costs. Combined partition costs often run $10,000 to $40,000 or more. Texas courts generally divide costs among the owners in proportion to their shares, so the buyer carries their part.
  • 🧾 Title risk. If heirship hasn't been established, the buyer is paying for a share that may turn out smaller than you think (more on that below).
  • 🏠 The occupant. A sibling living in the house can't be evicted by a co-owner, because every co-owner has a right to possess the whole property. An occupant can drag out a case and may assert claims for taxes and repairs they paid.
  • 🧮 Reimbursement claims. In a Texas partition, a co-owner who paid more than their share of taxes, insurance or necessary repairs can ask to be repaid from the proceeds before they're split. If you haven't been paying, your share of the proceeds shrinks.
  • 🎲 Outcome risk. Partition in kind, a buyout, a sale through a broker, or a settlement: the buyer doesn't know which it'll be.

Put together, offers for an undivided interest in an inherited Texas house often land somewhere around 35% to 65% of the share's proportional as-is value, with the low end for messy title or an occupied house and the high end for a clean, vacant house where a sale is likely soon. That's a wide range, and it should be. Your facts move it more than anything else.

✍️ What You Need to Sell Only Your Interest in Texas

Selling a fraction of a house is still a real estate closing. In Texas, you'll typically need:

  1. 📜 Proof that you own what you say you own. Your share passed to you at death under Section 101.001 of the Estates Code, but the deed records still show your parent. A buyer's title company will want a probated will or muniment of title, a court determination of heirship, or a recorded affidavit of heirship. Some buyers of shares will close on an affidavit of heirship and deed without full title insurance; others won't. That choice affects price.
  2. 🖊️ A deed for your undivided interest. Usually a special warranty deed or a deed without warranty describing your fractional interest, signed before a notary and recorded with the county clerk.
  3. 💍 Your spouse's signature if it's your homestead. Under Section 5.001 of the Texas Family Code, a married person can't sell, convey or encumber the homestead without the other spouse joining, even if the property is that person's separate property, and inherited property generally is. If you live in the inherited house with your spouse, plan for both signatures.
  4. 🧾 A tax and lien check. Delinquent property taxes, a mortgage, IRS liens, child-support liens and judgments against you personally can attach to your share. A judgment lien against a sibling generally attaches to their share, not yours, but it complicates any sale of the whole.

You don't need your siblings' permission, and they don't get a right of first refusal under Texas law unless there's a written agreement giving them one. They also don't have to be told before you sell, though telling them first is usually wise for the reason in the callout above.

🛑 Texas Shares a Buyer Will Discount Heavily, or Won't Buy

Some Texas interests are hard to sell at any price, and you should know before you shop yours:

  • 👵 A surviving spouse is living in the homestead. Article XVI, Section 52 of the Texas Constitution and Estates Code Section 102.005 bar partition among the heirs while the surviving spouse uses the property as a homestead. A buyer of your share can't force a sale until that ends, which may be decades. The share still has value, but mostly to the family.
  • 🏚️ You only hold a remainder. If the house was your parent's separate property and they died without a will, the surviving spouse may hold a life estate in one-third of it under Estates Code Section 201.002. A share that's partly subject to a life estate is harder to value and slower to cash out.
  • ❓ Your fraction is genuinely in doubt. Unknown half-siblings, a second marriage, a will that was never probated (Texas generally bars probating a will with an executor more than four years after death under Section 256.003), or a parent who died before their own parent's estate was settled can all change the math. Buyers price that uncertainty in.

⏰ The Tax Clock: How Chapter 29 Can Push Your Share Out

If you're the heir who hasn't been paying property taxes, this section is about you. Chapter 29 of the Texas Property Code lets a co-owner who has paid another owner's share of property taxes for any three years in a five-year period, without being reimbursed for more than half, petition the district court to force that owner to sell their interest to them. Section 29.002 sets those conditions.

In practice, that means a sibling who has quietly paid the county every year may be able to force you to sell your share to them, on terms the court sets, instead of you choosing your buyer and your price. If you've been receiving reimbursement demands, take them seriously. If you plan to sell your share, do it, or reimburse your sibling, before the three-year mark. And if you're the one who's been paying, keep every receipt; it's leverage in a buyout conversation.

💵 A Worked Example: A Quarter Share in Houston

Say four siblings inherit a Houston house. It would be worth about $300,000 fully updated, needs $50,000 of work, and is worth around $250,000 as-is. One sibling lives there. One sibling has been paying the taxes. You own one-quarter and want out.

  • 📏 Proportional as-is value: $250,000 × 1/4 = $62,500.
  • 🤝 Family buyout at an appraised price: if the siblings agree to an appraisal at $250,000, your share is about $62,500, less any taxes you owe the paying sibling. This is usually the best result for you.
  • ⚖️ Partition and sale: if the house sells through a broker for $250,000 after a year of litigation, subtract about 6% in sale costs ($15,000) and $25,000 in combined legal and court costs, and the net to split is $210,000. Your quarter is $52,500, before reimbursement claims from the sibling who paid the taxes and your own attorney's time, and about a year later than a buyout.
  • 🏷️ Outside sale of your share: with an occupant and a sibling who will claim tax reimbursement, offers might come in around $22,000 to $35,000 for your quarter, closing in two to five weeks.

Carrying costs matter too. Harris County homeowners commonly pay combined rates of roughly 2% to 2.5% of appraised value once the parent's exemptions fall off. On a $250,000 house that's $415 to $520 a month for the whole house, so roughly $105 to $130 a month is your quarter's share, plus a share of insurance. Every year of waiting costs you about $1,500 to $2,000 in taxes alone, plus the reimbursement claim if a sibling covers you.

The honest read: the outside sale pays the least, by a lot. It's the right choice only when your family won't engage, when you need money soon, when the title is too messy for a family buyout to happen, or when you'd rather not be the one who sues your siblings. If any of your siblings can and will pay something close to $62,500, that beats us.

🧩 Which Route Fits Your Situation

🤝 Offer your share to the family first

Best for almost everyone

Get an appraisal, multiply by your share, subtract anything you owe for taxes, and put it in writing. Point out that a 23A buyout in court would cost them the same number plus a year of fees. Give a deadline.

🏷️ Join a sale of the whole house

Best when everyone will sign and the house is sound

If the family will sell together, listing the whole house with an agent nets everyone the most, and it costs you only your share of the commission. A share sale makes no sense if a full sale is weeks away.

⚖️ File for partition

When the family won't engage and you can wait

You don't need anyone's permission to file. If 23A applies, your siblings get a buyout window at full proportional value. Many cases settle once the appraisal is in.

💵 Sell your share for cash

When you need out now, or can't face suing family

Fast and final, at a real discount. You hand the dispute to someone whose business is resolving it. Get more than one offer and read the deed you're signing.

🧾 Taxes When You Sell a Texas Share

Texas has no state personal income tax, so there's no state capital gains tax on selling your share, unlike California or North Carolina. For federal tax, inherited property generally gets a basis equal to its value at the date of death, so if you sell your share soon after the death for about what it was worth then, there may be little or no taxable gain. Expect property taxes to be prorated at closing for your share. Ask a tax professional about your situation, especially if the death was many years ago.

🙅 When Selling Your Share Would Be a Mistake

Don't sell your share to an outsider if your family is willing to buy you out at anything close to appraised value, or if a sale of the whole house is realistically coming in the next few months. Don't sell if you're unsure of your fraction; get the heirship mapped first. Don't sell if a surviving parent or stepparent is living in the homestead and you can wait, because almost nobody but your family will pay fairly for that share. And don't sign a deed for your interest without reading it; some share buyers ask for terms, like a power of attorney or an assignment of claims, that go beyond buying the share.

📋 What To Do This Week

  1. 🗺️ Confirm your fraction. Have a Texas attorney or title company map the heirship, including any surviving spouse, children from other relationships and community versus separate property.
  2. 🔎 Pull the tax account from the county appraisal district and tax office. Note who has paid what for the last five years.
  3. 🧾 Settle up if you're behind. Reimbursing a sibling for your share of taxes can stop a Chapter 29 claim and raises what your share is worth to everyone.
  4. 📐 Get an as-is value. An appraisal or a broker's opinion of value gives you the number to multiply by your share.
  5. ✉️ Make a written offer to your co-owners at that value times your fraction, with a deadline.
  6. 💵 Get at least two offers for your share if the family declines, and compare them with a realistic partition net.
  7. 💍 If you're married and live there, make sure your spouse is ready to sign.

You can sell your share of an inherited Texas house without anyone's permission. Start with the number Chapter 23A would use, appraised value times your fraction, and offer it to your family first. ❓ Frequently Asked Questions

Can I sell my share of an inherited house in Texas without my siblings' consent?

Yes. Each heir owns an undivided interest and can sell it by deed without the other owners' permission, unless a written agreement says otherwise. If the property is your homestead and you are married, Texas Family Code Section 5.001 requires your spouse to join the deed.

What is my share of an inherited Texas house worth?

A useful benchmark is the as-is value of the whole house times your fraction. That is also what Texas Property Code Section 23A.007 uses if your co-owners buy you out in a partition case covered by the Heirs Property Act. Outside buyers typically pay well below that figure, often 35% to 65% of it, because they take on time, legal costs, title risk and any occupant.

Do my siblings get a right of first refusal if I sell my share?

Not under general Texas law, unless there is a written agreement giving them one. In a partition case under Chapter 23A, though, the owners who did not ask for a sale get 45 days to elect to buy out the owner who did, at court-determined value times that owner's share.

Does Chapter 23A apply to my family's house?

It generally applies when the property is held as tenants in common, there is no written agreement about partition, at least one owner got their interest from a relative, and relatives hold at least 20% of the interests or make up at least 20% of the owners. Most houses inherited by siblings from a parent qualify. A Texas attorney can confirm.

Can a buyer of my share force the house to be sold?

A buyer becomes a co-owner and can file for partition like any other co-owner. If the Heirs Property Act applies, the family can buy them out at full proportional value. A buyer cannot force a partition while a surviving spouse occupies the property as a homestead.

What documents do I need to sell my inherited interest in Texas?

Usually proof of heirship, such as a probated will, muniment of title, court determination of heirship or a recorded affidavit of heirship, plus a deed for your undivided interest signed before a notary. If it is your homestead and you are married, your spouse must also sign.

I haven't paid property taxes on the inherited house. Can my sibling take my share?

Possibly. Under Texas Property Code Chapter 29, a co-owner who paid your share of property taxes for any three years in a five-year period, and was not reimbursed for more than half, can petition the district court to force you to sell your interest to them. Reimbursing them, or selling before then, can avoid that.

Can I sell my share if my stepmother is living in the house?

You can, but few outside buyers will pay much for it. If she is the surviving spouse and occupies the house as her homestead, the Texas Constitution and Estates Code Section 102.005 bar partition among the heirs during her lifetime while she uses it that way. Your share is usually worth more to your family than to an investor.

Do I owe Texas capital gains tax when I sell my share?

Texas has no state personal income tax, so there is no state capital gains tax. For federal purposes, inherited property generally takes a basis equal to its value at the date of death, which often leaves little taxable gain on a sale soon after. Ask a tax professional about your situation.

Is it better to sell my share or file for partition?

If your family will buy you out at a fair appraised price, that usually beats both. Partition can net more than selling your share to an outsider, but it takes months, costs money and strains family relationships. Selling your share for cash is fastest and certain, at a real discount.
