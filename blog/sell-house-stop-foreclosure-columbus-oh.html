Stopping a Columbus Foreclosure by Selling: Ohio Gives You Time, Then Charges for It

Ohio lenders have to sue you in the Franklin County Court of Common Pleas to take your house, so there is a case number, a judge, deadlines you can use, and a right to pay off the judgment right up until the court confirms the sheriff sale. That time is valuable. But Ohio also lets lenders pursue you for the shortfall afterward. Here is how to use the months you have before they turn into a debt.

By Dustin Ray, Founder of Pages of Purpose LLC. General information only, not legal advice. Ohio foreclosure procedure, Franklin County court practice, and lender policies vary, and laws differ by state. If you have been served, talk with an attorney licensed in Ohio, where the property is, before your answer deadline.

The foreclosure complaint usually arrives by certified mail or with a process server, and it looks like the end of something. In Ohio, it's closer to the beginning. Unlike states where a trustee can auction a house without a judge ever seeing the file, Ohio requires the lender to prove its case in court. For a Columbus homeowner, that means the clock is measured in months, not weeks, and there are several points along the way where selling the house can end the case on your terms.

It also means you are a defendant in a lawsuit, with deadlines, and that Ohio lenders can keep collecting after the house is gone. This guide covers both sides: how to use the time Ohio's process gives you, and how to avoid ending up with a judgment that follows you for two years.

⚖️ Ohio Forecloses Through a Courtroom, and That Buys You Time

Every Ohio mortgage foreclosure is a civil lawsuit filed in the court of common pleas for the county where the house sits. In Columbus, that's the Franklin County Court of Common Pleas. Before the lender can file, federal servicing rules (12 C.F.R. § 1024.41) generally require that you be more than 120 days behind. Once you're served, you have 28 days to file an answer.

What that means in practice:

  • 📨 You get notice and a deadline. The answer deadline is the single most important date in the case. If you miss it, the lender can ask for a default judgment within days.
  • 🤝 You can ask for mediation. Ohio allows a homeowner to request foreclosure mediation in the common pleas case, and the Franklin County Law Library points homeowners to a form for a stay of up to 120 days while mediation proceeds. Mediation is where modifications, payment plans, and agreed short sales get worked out with someone neutral in the room.
  • ⏳ Even uncontested, it takes months. The Franklin County Treasurer describes an uncontested case as one that can run in under 180 days from filing to sale. Cases where the homeowner answers, requests mediation, or negotiates usually take longer.
  • 🚫 No automatic right to reinstate. Ohio law doesn't give homeowners a statutory right to cure the default and reinstate the loan, although your loan documents may. Read the mortgage, and ask the servicer in writing.

Put simply, a Columbus homeowner who acts in the first month of the case usually has enough time to list the house with an agent, take offers, and close through a title company before any sheriff sale. That's the single biggest difference between Ohio and a fast non-judicial state, and it's why we often tell Columbus callers to list rather than sell to us.

📅 How a Franklin County Case Moves, Step by Step

1️⃣ Complaint and service

The lender files in common pleas court. For houses with one to four units, it also has to file a preliminary judicial report on the title within 14 days and a final judicial report before the sale (R.C. § 2329.191). The cost of that title work is taxed as a court cost, which means it's added to what you owe.

2️⃣ Answer or default

You have 28 days to answer. If no answer is filed, the lender can move for default judgment. If you answer, the lender typically moves for summary judgment, which takes longer.

3️⃣ Judgment and decree of foreclosure

The court enters judgment for the amount owed and orders the property sold. The clerk sends the order of sale to the Franklin County Sheriff.

4️⃣ Appraisal and advertising

Under R.C. § 2329.17, three disinterested county residents who own property appraise the house; for residential property they have 21 days to report. The Sheriff's office advertises each property for three consecutive weeks, starting about five weeks before the sale. The Treasurer estimates roughly three months from the order of sale to the auction date.

5️⃣ The sheriff sale

Franklin County sheriff sales are held online through the state's sheriff-sale auction site, on Fridays at 9:00 a.m. Eastern. Bidders put down a deposit and pay the balance within 30 days after confirmation.

6️⃣ Confirmation, deed, and move-out

The judge confirms the sale, typically 30 to 60 days after the auction, and the deed issues about two weeks later. If you're still in the house, the buyer gets a writ of possession; the Treasurer notes owners are usually given 10 to 14 days to leave, sometimes extended to 30 days for hardship.

🔓 You Can Still Sell Until the Judge Confirms the Sale

Here's the Ohio rule that surprises people most. Under R.C. § 2329.33, the homeowner can redeem the property at any time before the court confirms the sale, by depositing the judgment amount, all costs, and 8% interest on the purchase money from the date of the sale. If you do, the court sets the sale aside.

In practice, homeowners rarely redeem with their own savings. What they do is sell. A buyer closes through a title company, the title company pays the judgment and costs, and the case ends. That's possible right up to confirmation, though every week after the sheriff sale makes it harder, more expensive, and more dependent on a buyer who can close in days.

Two cautions. First, "you can sell until confirmation" is not the same as "you should wait until confirmation." Confirmation can come quickly, and some buyers will walk away from a deal that close to the edge. Second, after the sheriff sale, redemption costs include interest on the winning bid, so the price of waiting goes up.

💣 The Ohio Risk: A Deficiency Judgment That Lasts Two Years

Ohio lenders can get a personal judgment against you for the full amount owed. If the sheriff sale brings less than that, the difference, called a deficiency, is still a judgment against you. The lender can try to collect it like any other court judgment, including bank levies and wage garnishment.

Ohio does put a time limit on it for homeowners. Under R.C. § 2329.08, if the property was a dwelling for not more than two families used as a home, the deficiency becomes unenforceable two years after the court confirms the sale. Collection efforts already started before then aren't wiped out by the deadline, and a homeowner can waive the protection in writing.

⚠️ The most important warning on this page: in Ohio, losing the house at a sheriff sale doesn't necessarily end what you owe. A deficiency judgment can be collected for up to two years after confirmation, and a lender that starts collection inside that window can keep going. Selling the house yourself for enough to pay off the judgment, or negotiating a short sale with a written waiver of any deficiency, is how you make sure the debt actually ends.

Ohio's homestead exemption, $182,625 under R.C. § 2329.66 for April 2025 through March 2028, protects equity in a home from many judgment creditors, including in bankruptcy. It does not protect you from the mortgage you signed, and it won't stop a deficiency judgment from being entered.

📉 Why the Second Sheriff Sale Is Where Equity Disappears

At the first sheriff sale, Ohio law sets a floor: under R.C. § 2329.20, the house can't be sold for less than two-thirds of its appraised value, and Franklin County's sheriff sale rules say bids start there unless the court orders otherwise. That floor offers some protection.

But if a residential foreclosure property doesn't sell at the first auction, R.C. § 2329.52 calls for a second auction 7 to 30 days later, and that one goes to the highest bidder without regard to the minimum bid, subject only to costs and taxes. That's where houses go for a fraction of their value, where deficiencies grow, and where any equity the owner had can evaporate.

The appraisal behind that two-thirds floor is done by three appraisers from the outside, usually without going inside. If the house is worth $240,000 and the appraisers say $180,000, the floor is $120,000. If nobody bids at the first sale, the second sale has no floor at all.

🏡 List with an agent

Usually best if your case is early

With months before a sale and a house in reasonable shape, listing beats a cash sale for almost every Columbus homeowner with equity. Tell the agent about the case number and the stage, price to sell in the first few weeks, and have the title company order the payoff and judgment figures early.

🤝 Mediation, modification, or a short sale

Best if you want to stay or you're underwater

If your income has recovered, mediation is the natural place to negotiate a modification. If you owe more than the house is worth, a short sale negotiated in mediation, with a written release of any deficiency, protects you from the two-year exposure described above. A HUD-approved housing counselor can help you prepare, at no cost.

💵 A fast cash sale

Best late in the case or for a house that needs work

When the sale is advertised, the house has a failing furnace or foundation issues, or you've already moved out, a cash buyer who can close in two to three weeks pays off the judgment, ends the case, and closes the door on a deficiency. You give up some price for that certainty.

🛑 Chapter 13 bankruptcy

Worth a consultation if you want to keep the house

Filing bankruptcy at any point stops the foreclosure case, and a Chapter 13 plan can spread the arrears over several years. It's a serious step with its own costs; talk to an Ohio bankruptcy attorney, many of whom offer free first consultations.

💵 Worked Example: A Hilltop House After Judgment

Consider a 1950s brick ranch on the Hilltop, worth about $260,000 fully updated. It needs a furnace, windows, and a kitchen, roughly $30,000 of work. Judgment has been entered for $158,000, and with title costs, attorney fees, and accrued interest, the payoff is about $165,000. The sheriff sale is roughly three months out.

  • 🏡 Listing as-is: perhaps $230,000 to a buyer who can finance an older house. After about 6% commission and closing costs, roughly $215,000, minus the $165,000 payoff: about $50,000 to the owner, if it closes before the sale.
  • 💵 Cash sale: cash buyers usually pay 60% to 80% of after-repair value, landing lower the more work a house needs. On this house that's around $175,000 to $190,000, netting the owner about $10,000 to $25,000 after the payoff, with a close in about three weeks.
  • 🔨 Sheriff sale: if the appraisers value it at $195,000, the first-sale floor is $130,000. If a bidder pays $140,000, the owner is left with a deficiency of $25,000 or more, enforceable for up to two years, and no equity.

On these numbers, with three months to go and a house a financed buyer will accept, listing wins by a wide margin. If the furnace were dead in January, the roof leaking, or the sale four weeks away, the cash sale would be the one that actually closes, and closing at all is what protects the owner from the deficiency.

🏚️ Vacant and Behind on Taxes? The Track Most Owners Don't Know About

Not every Columbus foreclosure is a mortgage case. If a house is behind on property taxes and appears abandoned, Ohio allows an expedited tax foreclosure before the county board of revision under R.C. § 323.65 and the sections that follow. A parcel can be presumed unoccupied when two or more signs apply, such as utilities shut off or windows boarded up. Inherited houses that sit empty while a family figures things out are the classic case.

Franklin County property taxes run around 1.6% to 1.7% of value by recent estimates, so a $200,000 house carries a bill of about $3,300 to $3,400 a year. The Franklin County Treasurer offers delinquent-tax payment plans of up to 60 months, and getting on one early is far cheaper than the alternatives. 💰 Excess Funds After a Franklin County Sheriff Sale

If the house sells at the sheriff sale for more than the judgment, costs, and other liens, the leftover money belongs to the former owner, but it isn't mailed out automatically. In Franklin County, you file a motion for distribution of excess funds with the Clerk of Courts and request a hearing. A magistrate decides, there's a short objection period, and after the judge signs the distribution order, the check is picked up from the Clerk's fiscal office with photo ID.

Since November 2019, the Clerk has notified former owners when money is left over. That change came after millions of dollars tied to thousands of properties had sat unclaimed for years. If someone calls offering to recover your excess funds for a large percentage, know that the process is designed so you can do it yourself or with an attorney for much less.

  1. Find your answer deadline. Count 28 days from the date you were served. Put it on the calendar in red.
  2. Talk to a lawyer or legal aid before that date, even if you plan to sell. An answer preserves your options.
  3. Request mediation in the common pleas case if you want to keep the house or negotiate a short sale.
  4. Call a HUD-approved housing counselor in Central Ohio. Their help is free.
  5. Get a written payoff figure that includes the judgment, court costs, and per-day interest.
  6. Look up the case on the Franklin County Clerk of Courts site so you know what's been filed and whether a sale is scheduled.
  7. Decide based on the stage: early in the case, interview listing agents; late in the case or with a house that needs major work, get a written cash offer with a firm closing date before the sale.

✅ The Bottom Line for Columbus Homeowners

Ohio's judicial foreclosure process gives Columbus homeowners something valuable: time, a judge, a mediation option, and the right to pay off the judgment until the sale is confirmed. Used early, that time is usually enough to list the house and sell it for close to full value. That's the best outcome for most people with equity, and we'll say so if you call.

The flip side is that Ohio lets the lender keep collecting a deficiency for up to two years, and the second sheriff sale has no minimum bid. Waiting until the end costs more every week. If the case is late, the house needs real work, or you need a closing you can count on, a cash sale that pays off the judgment before the sheriff sale is often the cleanest way to end both the case and the debt.

❓ Frequently Asked Questions

How long does foreclosure take in Franklin County, Ohio?

The Franklin County Treasurer says an uncontested foreclosure can move from filing to sheriff sale in under 180 days. Cases where the homeowner answers, requests mediation, or negotiates usually take longer. After the sale, confirmation typically comes 30 to 60 days later.

How long do I have to respond to a foreclosure complaint in Ohio?

Twenty-eight days after you're served. If you don't file an answer, the lender can ask the court for a default judgment shortly after that deadline passes.

Can I sell my Columbus house after the sheriff sale?

Possibly, until the court confirms the sale. Under R.C. 2329.33 you can redeem before confirmation by paying the judgment, costs, and 8% interest on the sale price from the sale date. A fast sale that pays those amounts at closing can accomplish that, but the window is short and uncertain.

Can an Ohio lender come after me for money after foreclosure?

Yes. Ohio allows deficiency judgments. For a home of one or two family units, R.C. 2329.08 makes the deficiency unenforceable two years after the sale is confirmed, but collection started before then can continue.

What is the minimum bid at a Franklin County sheriff sale?

At the first sale, bids start at two-thirds of the appraised value under R.C. 2329.20. If a residential foreclosure property doesn't sell, R.C. 2329.52 provides for a second sale 7 to 30 days later with no minimum bid, apart from costs and taxes.

Where are Franklin County sheriff sales held?

Online, through the Ohio sheriff-sale auction website, on Fridays at 9:00 a.m. Eastern, unless the court orders an in-person sale. Each property is advertised for three consecutive weeks before the sale.

Does Ohio let me catch up on payments to stop foreclosure?

Ohio law doesn't give a statutory right to reinstate, but your mortgage may, and many servicers will accept reinstatement or agree to a modification, especially through foreclosure mediation. Ask in writing for a reinstatement figure.

Should I sell to a cash buyer or list my house during an Ohio foreclosure?

If the case is early and the house is in reasonable shape, listing usually nets far more, and Ohio's court timeline often allows it. A cash sale makes more sense when the sale is only weeks away, the house needs major repairs, or a financed buyer is unlikely to close in time.

How do I claim excess funds after a sheriff sale in Franklin County?

File a motion for distribution of excess funds with the Franklin County Clerk of Courts and request a hearing. A magistrate decides the claim, there's a brief objection period, and after the judge signs the order you pick up the check with photo ID.

Can my house be foreclosed for unpaid property taxes faster if it's vacant?

Yes. Ohio's abandoned-lands procedure under R.C. 323.65 allows an expedited tax foreclosure before the board of revision for delinquent properties that appear unoccupied. The Franklin County Treasurer also offers delinquent-tax payment plans of up to 60 months.
