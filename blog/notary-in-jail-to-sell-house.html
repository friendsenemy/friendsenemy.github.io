Getting a Notary Into a Jail Is the Easy Part. Proving Who's Signing Is What Stops the Sale.

Booking takes the driver's license. Without acceptable ID, a careful notary has to refuse, however friendly the deputy is. Some states solved this by statute, and others leave you to find a workaround. Fix the ID problem first, then scheduling, witnesses and the packet, and one visit can do the whole job.

By Dustin Ray, Founder of Pages of Purpose LLC. Not legal advice — laws differ by state, notary rules change, and each facility sets its own procedures. Talk to an attorney licensed where the property is before anyone signs.

Here's how these visits usually go wrong. A family finds a mobile notary, takes off work and drives to the jail. The notary gets cleared, sits down across from the signer, and asks for ID. The license is in a property bag at booking, or in a closet at home, or expired. The visit ends there, and the family loses two weeks and a travel fee.

A notary's central job is to confirm that the person signing is who they claim to be. A document that's signed but not properly notarized can't be recorded, and a title company won't insure it. So before you think about anything else, work out how this person will be identified.

🪪 Step One: Solve the ID Problem Before Anyone Drives Anywhere

Each state lists what counts as acceptable evidence of identity. A few mention inmates directly, which is the best case, because the notary has a statute to point to.

  • Florida. Section 117.05 lists inmate ID cards issued by the Florida Department of Corrections and by the federal Bureau of Prisons as acceptable. It also allows a sworn, written statement from a sworn law enforcement officer that the person's ID was confiscated on confinement and that the person named is the signer. That last one is built for county jails.
  • California. Civil Code section 1185 accepts a Department of Corrections and Rehabilitation inmate ID if the person's in custody in prison, and any inmate identification issued by a sheriff's department if the person's in a local detention facility. It also permits identification by credible witnesses under oath.
  • Federal prisons. 18 U.S.C. section 4004 lets certain federal staff take acknowledgments from inmates, and those staff already know who they're dealing with. Our federal prison guide explains why that doesn't always work for real estate.

Most other states don't name inmate IDs specifically. There, the usual tools are these.

  • A government-issued ID brought to the visit. Ask whether the facility will release the license from the signer's property to the notary or staff for the visit. Some will, and it's the cleanest fix. Check the expiration date, because many states won't accept an expired ID, or only within limits.
  • Credible witnesses. Many states let a notary rely on the sworn word of one or two people who know the signer, and who have ID of their own. Rules differ on how many, and on whether the notary must personally know the witness. A family member who has no stake in the sale may qualify. An agent named in the POA or a buyer usually shouldn't try.
  • Personal knowledge. If the notary truly knows the signer, some states accept that. A staff notary who's dealt with this person daily may be able to rely on it. Hiring a stranger won't get you there.

⚠️ Ask the notary this exact question on the first call: "The signer has only a facility ID and no driver's license. What will you accept in this state?" If they don't know, or say "we'll figure it out there," find another notary. Then ask your title company what they'll accept, because they have the final say.

🚪 Step Two: Who Can Get Through the Door

Facilities generally fall into three groups, and the same state can have all three.

Call the facility and ask for the person who handles legal or professional visits, not the front desk. Ask whether a notary counts as a professional visitor, what they must bring in (stamp, journal, pens), whether papers can pass hand to hand, and whether a contact room exists. A glass-only booth can be a dead end, because the notary has to watch the signature and handle the paper, and slipping documents through a slot may not be allowed. A lawyer's visit often solves this. Attorneys can usually book a contact room, and bringing a notary along as part of a legal visit can open doors that a family member can't.

Staff notaries are often the best deal when available. They're inside, the signer's identity isn't in doubt, and the fee may be nothing. Ask how far out they're booked, whether they'll notarize a real estate document, and whether their commission covers the state where they're standing. Some have rules against notarizing anything outside legal matters.

💻 Why Online Notarization Rarely Works From Inside

Remote online notarization sounds like the perfect answer, and families ask about it constantly. Under most state programs, the signer goes through an identity check that scans a government ID and asks knowledge-based questions drawn from credit and public records. The session runs on live audio and video and is recorded. Someone in jail usually has no ID in hand, no private device and no way to take the quiz, and the facility's tablet and video systems aren't built for this. Even in the rare case where a facility cooperates, your title company would have to accept it. Treat it as a last resort, not a plan.

✍️ Witnesses: The Second Headcount

The notary may not be the only outsider you need. Where the house sits decides this, not where the jail is.

  • Pennsylvania: a POA needs a notary and two adult witnesses, and neither witness can be the agent or the notary.
  • Florida: POAs and deeds need two witnesses plus a notary.
  • Georgia: a deed needs a notary and one more witness.

Ask early whether staff may serve as witnesses, as many facilities won't allow it. Bringing your own means clearing each person through visitor approval, and that can add weeks. Details are in our Pennsylvania, Florida and Georgia guides. California adds one of its own: for a deed or a POA affecting real property, the notary has to take the signer's thumbprint in the journal, so make sure the facility's okay with that.

📦 Build a Packet That Only Needs One Trip

  • Get the name exactly right. The name on the deed, ID and signature line should match. If the deed says "Robert J. Hall Jr." and the booking sheet says "Bobby Hall," sort that out with the title company before the visit.
  • Have the title company review the draft first. Your POA might be perfectly legal and still fail their underwriter's review.
  • Leave nothing blank. A notary shouldn't notarize a document with empty fields. The date is the one exception, and it's filled in at signing.
  • Bring two originals. One can be kept by the attorney in case the first is lost in the recording process.
  • Include the right notarial wording for the state where the signing occurs, or bring a loose certificate.
  • Explain it to the signer in advance. A notary may refuse if the person seems confused, medicated or pressured. Let them read it beforehand, ideally through their lawyer.
  • Check the location that morning. Confirm the person hasn't been moved, sent to court, or placed on restriction. Lockdowns happen, and they cancel visits with no notice.

💳 What It Costs, and What a Lost Month Costs

States cap the fee for the notarial act itself: $10 per act in Florida and $15 per signature in California, for example. Travel and waiting time are usually billed separately, and that's where the money goes. Expect $75 to $300 for a local jail, and more for a remote prison or a long wait. A lawyer-drafted POA generally costs $200 to $800. Staff notarization inside may be free, and for federal staff, the statute requires that.

The bigger cost is delay. Take a paid-off house worth $220,000 repaired, sitting vacant. Property tax is about $185 a month at a 1% rate, a vacant-home policy about $175, utilities $90, upkeep $100. That's $550 a month, or $1,650 for three months spent on failed visits, and more if a mortgage is running.

📋 What To Do This Week

  1. Look up the state's accepted forms of notary ID, or ask a notary who works jails.
  2. Find out where the signer's license is, and whether it's expired.
  3. Call the facility's legal-visit contact and ask about staff notaries and outside notaries.
  4. Ask the title company which document they want signed and how they want it notarized.
  5. Check whether the property's state requires witnesses, and line them up.
  6. Book the visit and confirm the person's location that morning.
  7. Mail or record the original right away by a trackable method.

✅ Bottom Line

Most failed jail notary visits are failures of identification, and they're preventable by asking one question before booking. Get an answer on ID, then settle access, witnesses and the packet, with your title company signing off on each part. Do that and it's usually one visit. The hours of phone calls up front cost much less than a second trip, and the deed is only as good as the notary block on it.

❓ Frequently Asked Questions

Can a notary come to a jail?

Often yes, with approval. Each facility decides how outside notaries get in, and some treat them as professional visitors while others are stricter. Call the legal-visit contact, not the front desk, and plan on days to weeks.

How can an inmate be identified by a notary without a driver's license?

It depends on the state. Florida and California accept certain inmate IDs by statute, and Florida also allows a law enforcement officer's sworn statement. Elsewhere, the usual options are retrieving the license from property or using credible witnesses.

Is an inmate ID valid for notarization?

In some states, yes. Florida's section 117.05 lists Florida DOC and Bureau of Prisons inmate IDs, and California's Civil Code section 1185 lists CDCR and sheriff-issued inmate IDs. In states that don't list them, ask the notary and the title company before relying on one.

Do jails have their own notary?

Some do, usually among administrative staff, and many state prisons have one through case workers or the law library. Federal prisons have staff with notary-like powers. Ask whether they'll do a real estate document, since some limit it to legal papers.

How much does it cost to get a notary at a jail?

The act itself is capped in many states, at $10 in Florida and $15 per signature in California. Travel and waiting are extra, usually $75 to $300 locally and more for remote prisons. Staff notaries may be free.

Can an inmate use online notarization?

Rarely. Online notarization usually needs a scanned government ID, knowledge-based questions and a private recorded video session, and an inmate seldom has any of these. Expect to use an in-person notary or a staff notary.

What happens if the notary can't get in or the jail goes on lockdown?

The visit gets rescheduled, and you may still owe a travel fee. Ask about the cancellation policy and check that morning if the facility is open for visits and the person is still there.

Can a family member be a credible witness for an inmate?

Possibly, depending on the state. Credible witnesses need to know the signer and usually need their own ID, and they generally shouldn't have a financial interest in the document. Someone named as agent or receiving money from the sale is a poor choice.

Does the notary have to be from the state where the house is?

No, the notary acts under the law of the state where the signing happens. Most states recognize an out-of-state notarization, but the document has to meet the property state's requirements, including any witnesses. Confirm with your title company.

Is a POA or a deed the better thing to notarize in jail?

A POA is usually better. It takes one signing and lets an agent handle whatever comes up, while a deed signed inside has to be perfect and any correction means another visit. Some title companies prefer a specific form, so ask them first.
