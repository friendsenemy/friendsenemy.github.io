Heirs Can't Agree on a Texas House? In Texas You Already Own It — Proving It Is the Fight

Texas law hands an inherited house to the heirs the moment the owner dies. No court has to transfer it. That sounds like good news until the family disagrees, because then nobody can sell without every heir's signature, a title company won't insure a deed without proof of who the heirs are, and one sibling who won't sign can quietly hold the whole thing hostage. Here's how Texas families actually break that deadlock, and when they shouldn't sell to anyone like us.

By Dustin Ray, Founder of Pages of Purpose LLC. Not legal advice. Texas probate, homestead and partition law is technical and changes, laws differ by state, and every family is different. Before anyone signs a deed, an affidavit or a lawsuit, talk to an attorney licensed in Texas, where the property is.

We get the same call from Texas families every week. A parent died a few years ago. Nobody probated anything because nobody had money for a lawyer, or because someone said Texas doesn't require it. The house is still in the parent's name on the county appraisal district's website. One sibling lives there, or one wants to rent it out, or one simply stopped answering texts. The others want to sell and split the money. Then a title company tells them it can't close until "heirship is established," and nobody knows what that means.

Here is the part that surprises people: in Texas, the heirs already own that house. The fight isn't over whether you own it. It's over proving who owns it, in what shares, and whether one co-owner can stop the rest. That is a different problem from the one families in most other states face, and it calls for different tools.

🧾 In Texas, the House Passes at Death. The Paperwork Doesn't.

Section 101.001 of the Texas Estates Code says that when a person dies, the estate vests immediately: in the people named in the will if there is one, and in the heirs at law if there isn't. No court order is needed for ownership to change hands. The heirs become owners of undivided shares on the day of death, subject only to the estate's debts.

So why can't a family that agrees just sell? Because a buyer's title insurer has to be satisfied about who those owners are. Ownership vesting by law is invisible in the county deed records. The records still show the parent. To close, the title company needs some recorded or court-made proof that connects the dead owner to the living sellers. In Texas, that proof usually comes one of four ways:

  • 📜 Probate of the will, either with an executor or as a muniment of title, which records the will as the transfer document.
  • 🏛️ A court determination of heirship under Chapter 202 of the Estates Code, where a probate judge declares who the heirs are and in what shares. The court appoints an attorney ad litem to protect unknown heirs.
  • ✍️ An affidavit of heirship under Section 203.001, a sworn statement by a disinterested person who knew the family, recorded in the county deed records. After it has been of record for five years, the statute makes it prima facie evidence of the facts in it. Many title companies will insure sooner on a well-prepared affidavit, but each underwriter sets its own rules.
  • 💼 A small estate affidavit under Chapter 205, for estates under the $75,000 limit (not counting the homestead and exempt property). It can pass the homestead only in limited circumstances, and only when there's no will.

Every one of those routes needs either cooperation or a court. That is the leverage a holdout heir has in Texas. Ownership is already shared. A deed out of the estate needs every owner's signature. A sibling who won't sign doesn't have to file anything or hire anyone to stop a sale. Doing nothing is enough.

The most important thing on this page: no single Texas heir can sell the whole house, and no group of heirs can sell it over one owner's objection, without a court. Not a majority, not the oldest child, not the one paying the taxes. What any heir can do alone is sell their own share, or ask a district court to partition. Everything below flows from that.

🔑 How One Sibling Can Push the Estate Into Court Supervision

Texas probate is famous for being fast and cheap, and it is, when the family cooperates. The fast version is called independent administration. An independent executor or administrator can sell estate real estate without asking a judge for permission at each step.

When the will names an independent executor, that's usually settled. When there is no will, Section 401.003 of the Estates Code allows independent administration only if all of the distributees agree on it and on who should serve. Courts across the state implement this with a signed waiver and consent from every heir. If one heir refuses to sign, the family is generally left with a dependent administration. That's the court-supervised version, where the administrator usually has to post a bond and get a court order to sell the house, with hearings, appraisals and reports along the way.

The practical difference is large. An agreed independent administration in a Texas metro often costs a few thousand dollars in attorney fees and can have someone with authority to sell inside two or three months. A dependent administration commonly costs $8,000 to $20,000 or more in fees and bond premiums and can run a year or longer. A sibling who withholds a signature on a single consent form can push the family from the first track onto the second.

There's a second Texas wrinkle: the four-year rule. Under Section 256.003, a will generally can't be admitted to probate (with an executor) more than four years after the death unless the person offering it wasn't "in default." Texas courts are fairly forgiving about admitting late wills as a muniment of title, which passes the house to the named beneficiaries without an executor. But if Mom died in 2020 and nobody did anything, appointing an executor to sell the house may be off the table. The family is then down to a muniment, an heirship proceeding, or affidavits, and the cooperation problem comes back.

💍 Community Property and Stepchildren: Who Holds Which Share

Before anyone argues about selling, get the shares right. Texas is a community property state, and with no will that changes the math in ways that start family fights.

  • 👨‍👩‍👧 Community house, all children shared with the surviving spouse: under Section 201.003, the deceased's half of the community passes to the surviving spouse. The children inherit nothing in the house until the second parent dies.
  • 👨‍👧‍👦 Community house, deceased had a child outside the marriage: the deceased's half of the community goes to all of the deceased's children equally, stepchildren-to-the-spouse included. The spouse keeps only their own half. A widow can end up co-owning her home with her late husband's adult children from a first marriage.
  • 🏚️ Separate-property house (owned before the marriage or inherited): under Section 201.002, the children take the land, but the surviving spouse gets a life estate in one-third of it.

Most heirs' disputes we see in Texas trace back to one of those three patterns, usually the second. Get an attorney or a title company to map the shares before anyone makes an offer to anyone else. An heir who thinks they own a third and actually owns a sixth is about to make a very bad decision.

🛑 The Surviving-Spouse Homestead Right That Overrides Partition

Texas gives a surviving spouse something stronger than ownership: a right to keep living in the homestead. Article XVI, Section 52 of the Texas Constitution says the homestead descends like other real property but shall not be partitioned among the heirs during the lifetime of the surviving spouse, for as long as the survivor elects to use it as a homestead. Estates Code Section 102.005 repeats that rule. The same section protects minor children of the deceased while a court permits their guardian to use and occupy the home.

What that means in a family dispute:

  • 🚫 If a stepparent or surviving parent lives in the house as their homestead, the children cannot force a partition sale, even if they own most of the title.
  • ⏳ The right lasts as long as the spouse keeps using the house as a home. If they move into assisted living, remarry and move away, or rent it out and abandon it as a homestead, the protection can end. Whether it has ended is a fact question that families fight about.
  • 🧾 The occupying spouse is generally expected to maintain the property and keep up taxes and the interest on any debt against it. A spouse who lets the taxes go is a different conversation for a Texas lawyer.

If this is your situation, the realistic options are a negotiated deal with the spouse (they sign and take a share, often based on their age and the value of the right to stay), waiting, or one heir selling their own interest knowing it comes with a resident who can't be moved. No court, and no cash buyer, can remove that spouse's right.

💰 The Heir Paying the Taxes Has a Remedy Most Families Never Hear About: Chapter 29

Here's a Texas remedy almost no family knows about. Chapter 29 of the Texas Property Code lets a co-owner who has been carrying the property taxes go to court to force another co-owner to sell their share to them. Under Section 29.002, the paying owner can petition the district court in the county where the property is if they have paid the other owner's share of the property taxes for any three years in a five-year period, and the other owner hasn't reimbursed more than half of what was paid.

It's narrower than partition. It forces a sale of the non-paying owner's interest to the paying owner, not a sale of the whole house to the public. But it's exactly aimed at the most common Texas heirship standoff: one sibling dutifully pays the county every January while another owns a share, pays nothing, and refuses to sell. If you've been paying, start keeping every receipt and send written demands for reimbursement. If you're the one not paying, understand that silence can cost you your share.

⚖️ Partition in Texas: The Old Rules and the Heirs Property Act

When the heirs are co-owners and there's no protected homestead occupant, any co-owner can ask a district court to partition. Section 23.001 of the Property Code gives a joint owner that right, and Texas Rules of Civil Procedure 756 through 771 lay out the procedure. A Texas partition case runs in two stages. First the court decides who owns what share, settles reimbursement claims (for taxes, insurance and necessary repairs one owner paid), and decides whether the property can fairly be divided in kind. If it can, the court appoints commissioners to divide it, and parties get 30 days to object to their report. If it can't, which is the usual answer for a single house on a city lot, the court orders it sold, often through a receiver.

Since 2017, Texas has also had the Uniform Partition of Heirs' Property Act, in Chapter 23A of the Property Code. It applies to most inherited family houses: property held as tenants in common, with no written agreement on partition, where at least one owner got their share from a relative and relatives hold at least 20% of the interests (or make up at least 20% of the owners). When it applies:

  1. 🧮 Value first. Under Section 23A.006, the court determines the property's fair market value, usually with an appraisal.
  2. 🤝 Buyout right. Under Section 23A.007, heirs who don't want a sale get 45 days after notice to elect to buy out the heirs who asked for one, at that value times their fractional share. The court sets a payment deadline at least 60 days out.
  3. 🏡 In kind is preferred. Under Section 23A.008, the court orders physical division unless it would cause substantial prejudice to the owners as a group. With a house, it usually would.
  4. 🏷️ Open-market sale. Under Section 23A.010, a court-ordered sale is generally listed with a broker at no less than the determined value, not dumped at a courthouse-steps auction.

Budget realistically. A partition suit that the other side contests commonly takes 6 to 18 months in Texas and $10,000 to $40,000 or more in combined fees, appraisal and receiver costs, much of it paid out of the sale proceeds. Often the filing itself is what brings the holdout to the table. Our partition lawsuit guide walks through the general mechanics; the Texas-specific point is that Chapter 23A gives the heirs who want to keep the house a real chance to do it.

🧩 What Actually Breaks a Texas Heirship Deadlock

✍️ Clear the title first, then talk price

Start here in almost every case

Many standoffs are really confusion. Once an attorney or title company maps who owns what share, and an affidavit of heirship or heirship determination is in motion, the holdout often turns out to be worried about something solvable: a debt they think they owe, a share they think is bigger, or a parent's wish they want honored. Paper first, argument second.

💵 Buy out the heir who wants out, or the one who won't leave

Best when someone wants the house and can finance it

A sibling living in the house can refinance or borrow to buy the others' shares at an appraised price. If a partition suit is filed and Chapter 23A applies, the statute gives that buyout a deadline and a court-set value, which takes the haggling out of it.

🧾 Use Chapter 29 if you've carried the taxes

Narrow, but powerful when it fits

Three years of paying another owner's share out of five, with demands that went unanswered, can let you force them to sell their interest to you. Talk to a Texas real estate litigator about whether your payments qualify before you count on it.

🏷️ Sell your own undivided share

Best when you need out and the family won't move

Any Texas heir can sell their own interest without anyone's permission, unless an occupying spouse's homestead right makes it nearly worthless to a buyer. The price is far below a proportional share of the house, because the buyer is buying the dispute and the title work. It turns a share you can't use into money without a lawsuit. Be honest with yourself about the discount.

⚖️ File for partition

Last resort, but sometimes the only one

Available against co-owners, not against a surviving spouse living in the homestead. Expect months, shared costs, a court-set value and, under Chapter 23A, a buyout window for the family members who want to keep it.

💵 What the Money Looks Like on a Real Texas House

Every month an inherited Texas house sits while the family argues, it costs money, and Texas costs run higher than people expect.

  • 🏛️ Property tax. Texas has no state income tax and leans hard on property tax. Combined rates in most metro areas fall roughly between 1.6% and 2.5% of appraised value, depending on the county, city and school district. When the parent's homestead exemption and any over-65 or disability freeze drop off, usually the January 1 after no qualifying owner lives there, the bill jumps.
  • 🌩️ Insurance. A standard policy often won't cover a house that's been vacant 30 to 60 days. Vacant-dwelling coverage in Texas, where hail and wind claims are common, often runs $150 to $350 a month.
  • 💡 Utilities and upkeep. Keeping power on for A/C in the summer, plus water and yard care to avoid city code citations, is $200 to $450 a month. On expansive clay soil around Dallas, Austin and San Antonio, a dry, unwatered slab can move, and foundation work starts around $8,000.

A worked example. Take a three-bedroom house in San Antonio worth about $280,000 once updated. It needs a roof, HVAC and cosmetic work, roughly $35,000. At a 2.2% combined rate, taxes alone are about $510 a month. Add $250 for vacant-home insurance and $300 for utilities and upkeep, and the family is spending roughly $1,060 a month, close to $12,700 a year, to keep arguing.

A cash buyer typically offers 60% to 80% of repaired value depending on condition and how messy the title is. On this house, something around $185,000 to $205,000 is realistic. Listed as-is with an agent, it might sell for $235,000. Subtract about 7% for commissions and closing costs ($16,500) and four months of carrying costs ($4,200), and the family nets around $214,000. That's more than a cash sale, and if everyone will sign and wait, listing is the better call. The cash path wins when the house needs $60,000 or more of work, when heirship is messy enough that retail buyers walk, or when the alternative is a year of partition litigation at roughly $1,000 a month plus legal fees.

Legal costs to budget for in Texas: an affidavit of heirship prepared by an attorney or title company often runs $500 to $1,500; a court determination of heirship with independent administration commonly $3,000 to $6,000 including the attorney ad litem; a dependent administration $8,000 to $20,000 or more; a contested partition $10,000 to $40,000 or more.

🙅 When You Shouldn't Sell to a Cash Buyer at All

We buy houses for a living, so take this seriously coming from us. Don't sell to a cash buyer when every heir will sign, the house is structurally sound, and nobody needs money in the next few months. Clear the title, list it with an agent, and split the larger check. Don't sell when an occupying spouse's homestead right means any buyer is just waiting on them; your share is worth far more to the family in a negotiated deal. And don't sell your share in a panic before an attorney has told you what it actually is.

A cash sale makes sense when the house is the thing keeping the family apart and nobody can pay for repairs, when title problems would scare off a mortgage lender, when carrying costs are eating the equity, or when one heir needs out and the others won't budge. 📋 What To Do This Week

  1. 🔎 Pull the deed and the tax account. Search the county clerk's deed records and the appraisal district's site for the property. Confirm whose name is on it, whether taxes are current, and whether a transfer on death deed was recorded.
  2. 📜 Find out whether there's a will, and when the owner died. If it's been close to four years, call a Texas probate attorney now, not next month.
  3. 🗺️ Map the shares. Was the house community or separate property? Was there a surviving spouse? Children outside the marriage? Write it down and have a lawyer check it.
  4. 🏠 Ask who's living there and on what terms. A surviving spouse with homestead rights changes everything. An adult sibling living there rent-free does not; they're a co-owner, and their occupancy counts in a partition accounting.
  5. 🧾 If you're paying the taxes, document it. Keep receipts and send written requests for reimbursement. That record matters for Chapter 29 and for any partition accounting.
  6. 🛡️ Insure the house properly. If it's vacant, get vacant-dwelling coverage this week.
  7. ⚖️ Get two numbers before deciding. An agent's as-is and repaired-value estimate, and a written cash offer. Compare them net of time, repairs and legal costs, not gross.

✅ Bottom Line

In Texas, heirs own the house from the moment of death, so the deadlock is rarely about getting into probate court. It's about proving the heirship to a title company's satisfaction and dealing with the one owner who won't sign. Map the shares, check for a surviving spouse living there, start the heirship paperwork, and document who has been paying. Then the tools are real: a Chapter 23A buyout, a Chapter 29 forced sale for the heir who carried the taxes, a partition suit, or one heir quietly selling their share. If everyone ends up signing and the house is sound, list it. If not, ask us, and we'll tell you which of those paths fits before we tell you what we'd pay.

❓ Frequently Asked Questions

Do Texas heirs need probate before they own an inherited house?

No. Under Texas Estates Code Section 101.001, the estate vests in the heirs or will beneficiaries immediately at death. What heirs usually need is proof of that ownership a title company will accept before they can sell: a probated will or muniment of title, a court determination of heirship, or a recorded affidavit of heirship.

What is an affidavit of heirship and will a title company accept it?

It is a sworn statement, usually by a disinterested person who knew the family, describing the deceased's marriages, children and heirs. It is recorded in the county deed records. Under Section 203.001, after five years of record it becomes prima facie evidence of the facts stated. Many title companies will insure a sale on one sooner, but each underwriter sets its own requirements, and a disputed or incomplete family history can push them to require a court proceeding instead.

Can one sibling block an independent administration in Texas?

When there is no will, Section 401.003 allows independent administration only if all of the distributees agree. If one heir refuses to sign the consent, the estate generally has to proceed as a dependent administration, which needs court orders for a sale, usually a bond, and more time and money.

What happens if nobody probated Mom's will within four years?

Under Section 256.003, a will generally cannot be probated with an executor appointed more than four years after death unless the person offering it was not in default. Texas courts often still admit a late will as a muniment of title, which transfers the property to the named beneficiaries without an executor. If that fails, the property passes as if there were no will, through heirship.

Can my stepmother stay in the house and stop us from selling?

If the house was the homestead and she is the surviving spouse, yes, as long as she continues to use it as her homestead. Article XVI, Section 52 of the Texas Constitution and Estates Code Section 102.005 bar partition among the heirs during the surviving spouse's lifetime while she occupies it. The children can still own their shares, and can sell those shares, but cannot force her out or force a sale.

Who inherits a community property house when there are children from a prior marriage?

With no will, if the deceased had a child who is not also the surviving spouse's child, the deceased's half of the community property goes to the deceased's children equally under Section 201.003. The surviving spouse keeps only their own half. If all children are shared with the surviving spouse, the deceased's half goes to the spouse.

I've paid all the property taxes for years. Can I make my siblings sell to me?

Possibly. Chapter 29 of the Texas Property Code lets a co-owner petition the district court to force another co-owner to sell their interest to them if the petitioner paid that owner's share of property taxes for any three years in a five-year period and was not reimbursed for more than half. It forces a sale of the other owner's share to you, not a sale of the whole house to the public.

What is the cotenant buyout under Texas Property Code Chapter 23A?

When an inherited house qualifies as heirs' property and one owner files for partition, the court first sets the fair market value. Under Section 23A.007, the owners who did not ask for a sale then have 45 days after notice to elect to buy out the owners who did, at that value times their fractional share, with a payment deadline of at least 60 days.

Can I sell just my share of an inherited Texas house?

Yes. Each heir owns an undivided interest and can sell it without the others' consent. Expect a price well below a proportional share of the whole house, because the buyer takes on the other owners, the title work and any partition fight. If a surviving spouse has homestead occupancy rights, the share is worth even less to an outside buyer.

How much does a partition suit cost in Texas and how long does it take?

A contested partition commonly takes 6 to 18 months and $10,000 to $40,000 or more in combined attorney fees, appraisal, commissioner or receiver costs. Texas courts generally divide costs among the owners in proportion to their interests, and much of it comes out of the sale proceeds. Many cases settle once the suit is filed.
