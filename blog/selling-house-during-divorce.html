Can I Sell My House Before, During or After a Divorce?

Most people ask this when the house is the biggest thing left to split. The short version: you can sell at any stage, but who has to sign, where the money goes and what you owe in tax change with the timing.

By Dustin Ray, founder of Pages of Purpose LLC. Updated November 2, 2026. Not legal advice. Divorce and property law differ by state, so talk to a family law attorney where the house sits before you sign anything.

Yes. You can sell a house before, during or after a divorce. Before anyone files, everyone on the deed signs. After filing, many states automatically bar selling without your spouse’s written consent or a court order. After the decree, whoever the decree gives the house to can sell. If you can’t agree, a judge can order a sale.

Can I sell my house before, during or after divorce?

Yes, at all three stages. What changes is who signs, whether the court has a say, and how the tax exclusion works. Here is the side-by-side view.

Can I sell my house before filing for divorce?

Yes, if everyone on the deed signs. If the house is jointly titled, you both sign. If it is in your name alone, you may be able to sign alone, but your spouse can still have a marital claim to the equity.

A sale before filing is often the cleanest path when you both agree. There is no case yet and no restraining order. Put the split in a short signed agreement, sell at a fair price, and either divide the money at closing or hold it in an account that needs both signatures.

It goes wrong when one spouse acts alone. Selling below market, deeding the house to a relative or moving the money where the other spouse can’t see it looks like hiding marital assets. A divorce judge can count that money against the spouse who did it when dividing property, and in some cases a transfer can be undone.

Can I sell my house during a divorce?

Yes, with your spouse’s written consent or a court order. In many states, filing the case triggers automatic orders that bar either spouse from selling or transferring property without one or the other.

California is a clear example. Its Family Code prints a restraining order on every divorce summons that bars transferring or disposing of any property, community or separate, “without the written consent of the other party or an order of the court” (Cal. Fam. Code § 2040). Our California divorce home-sale guide shows how that order works in practice and how the proceeds get divided. Other states use different names, such as standing orders or a preliminary injunction. Some states have no automatic order, but a joint deed still needs both signatures.

There are three normal ways to sell mid-case:

  1. Written consent. You both sign an agreement to sell that says where the proceeds go until the decree. This is the fastest route.
  2. A court order. One spouse files a motion asking the judge to approve the sale. Judges often grant these when payments are behind, the house is deteriorating, or neither spouse can carry it.
  3. An emergency motion. If a foreclosure date is set, many courts will hear the request on a shorter schedule.

Expect the money to sit in escrow or an attorney trust account until the decree. Wiring proceeds to one spouse mid-case without agreement is how people end up in contempt hearings.

Can I sell my house after the divorce is final?

Yes. Whoever the decree awards the house to can sell it. If the decree keeps it in both names, both of you sign and split the money the way the decree says.

When the house was awarded to you, the title company will want a copy of the decree, and usually a deed from your ex if their name is still on title. If you owe your ex a buyout, it gets paid from your proceeds at closing.

When the decree orders a sale by a deadline and your ex stalls, go back to the family court and ask it to enforce the decree. Courts tend to move faster on this than on a new fight, because the sale was already ordered.

Do I have to sell my house in a divorce?

No. You can keep it if one spouse buys out the other, or if you both agree to keep owning it for a while. A sale becomes likely when neither spouse can afford a buyout and you can’t agree on anything else.

A buyout usually means the spouse who stays refinances the mortgage in their own name and uses the cash to pay the other spouse their share. That only works if one income qualifies for the new loan. If your current loan carries a low rate from a few years ago, a new loan at a higher rate can push the payment out of reach.

Some couples keep the house jointly until the youngest child finishes school, then sell. If you do that, write down who pays what, who handles repairs and what triggers the sale.

Can I be forced to sell my house in a divorce?

Yes. A divorce judge can order the house sold as part of dividing property, even if one spouse objects. After the divorce, a co-owner can usually ask a court to order a sale through a partition case.

Judges usually order a sale when nobody can buy the other out and the equity has to be split somehow. Some states let a judge give the parent with custody the right to stay in the home for a set time first, which can push a sale back. Maryland does this through use and possession orders, as our Baltimore divorce guide explains.

If the divorce is over and your ex is still on the deed and won’t sell, you have two more options:

  • Partition. A court orders the property divided, almost always by sale, and splits the money. It works but takes months and legal fees. See how a partition lawsuit works.
  • Sell your share. If you hold title as tenants in common, you can usually sell your share without your ex’s signature. The buyer becomes their co-owner. You get less than half of what a full sale would bring, because the buyer takes on the co-owner problem. See how selling a share works.

Is it better to sell the house before or after divorce?

Selling earlier usually leaves more money when you both agree, because you stop paying to hold a house neither of you wants. Waiting makes sense when one spouse plans to keep it, or when timing the sale affects your tax bill.

For example, a couple with $180,000 of equity might spend about $1,500 a month on mortgage interest, property tax and insurance, money that does not pay down the loan. If the divorce takes 12 months and the price doesn’t change, waiting costs about $18,000, or $9,000 less for each spouse. That example is hypothetical. Run your own numbers.

Waiting can also cost you if the house sits empty, repairs get skipped, or one spouse stops paying. It can help if prices are rising in your area or if the spouse who stays plans to refinance.

How does capital gains tax work when you sell during a divorce?

A married couple filing jointly can exclude up to $500,000 of gain on a main home. Single filers can exclude up to $250,000. Your filing status depends on whether you are still married on December 31 of the year you sell.

  • Joint exclusion. To get $500,000, one or both spouses must meet the ownership test and both must meet the residence test: generally owned and lived in the home 2 of the last 5 years (IRS Publication 523).
  • Filing status. If your divorce is final by the last day of the year, the IRS treats you as unmarried for the whole year (IRS Publication 504).
  • The spouse who moved out. If you still own part of the home and your ex lives there under a divorce or separation instrument, you can treat it as your residence for the exclusion (Publication 523).
  • Transfers between spouses. Deeding the house to your spouse, or to an ex incident to the divorce, generally triggers no gain or loss. The spouse who keeps it also keeps the original cost basis, so any future gain is theirs (Publication 504).

Most couples have less than $250,000 of gain each, so timing rarely changes the tax. If you bought decades ago in an area that boomed, ask a CPA before you choose a sale date.

What else slows a divorce sale down?

  • Mortgage payments. The loan stays in both names until it is paid off or refinanced. If one spouse stops paying, both credit reports take the hit, and a foreclosure can wipe out the equity you are dividing.
  • Who lives there. Being on title does not let you put your spouse out. A judge may decide who stays during the case, and an occupied house is harder to show.
  • Children. Courts weigh a child’s stability, and many parents choose to wait until a school year ends.
  • Underwater loans. If you owe more than the house is worth, any sale is a short sale that needs the lender’s approval. Bring in both a family law attorney and a real estate attorney.
  • Separate property. If one spouse owned the house before the marriage, part of the equity may be separate, which changes the split. Community property states, listed by the IRS in Publication 555, handle this differently from the rest.

What should I do right now?

  1. Pull the deed. Your county recorder or land records office has it, often online. Check how you hold title: tenants by the entirety, joint tenants, tenants in common or one name only.
  2. Estimate the equity. Value, minus the mortgage, any home equity loan, liens and back taxes. That is the pot you are dividing.
  3. Know your stage. Not filed, filed, or final. Each stage has different signing rules.
  4. Talk to a family law attorney before you sign a listing, especially after filing.
  5. Price every path. An agent’s price opinion, a cash offer, and what a buyout refinance would cost the spouse who wants to stay.
  6. Ask a CPA about the year of sale if your gain could be over $250,000 per person.
  7. Put the plan in writing: sale price range, who pays the mortgage until closing, and where the proceeds go.

Frequently asked questions

Can a divorce be finalized before the house is sold?

Yes. Many divorces end with the house still unsold. The settlement or decree then says who keeps it, the deadline to sell or refinance, who pays the mortgage until then, and how the money is split. Both names stay on the deed until the house sells or one spouse deeds it to the other.

How long do you have to sell a house after divorce?

No national law sets a deadline. The divorce decree or settlement usually does, often a set number of months to list or refinance. If the decree is silent and you both stay on the deed, you remain co-owners until you sell, one buys the other out, or a court orders a partition sale.

Can I refuse to sell my house in a divorce?

You can refuse to sign a listing or a contract, but that does not end the matter. A divorce judge can divide the house by awarding it to one spouse, ordering a buyout, or ordering a sale. After the divorce, a co-owner can usually file a partition case to force a sale.

How are proceeds from a house sale divided in a divorce?

By your settlement or by state law. Nine states use community property rules: Arizona, California, Idaho, Louisiana, Nevada, New Mexico, Texas, Washington and Wisconsin. Most other states divide marital property by what a judge finds fair. Money one spouse brought into the marriage may be treated as separate property.

Do I have to tell the divorce court I sold the house?

Yes. Divorce cases require each spouse to disclose assets and debts, and a house sale or its proceeds is part of that. Selling after filing without your spouse's written consent or a court order can violate automatic orders in states that have them. Hiding a sale can lead to sanctions.

Who pays the mortgage until the house sells?

Whoever your agreement or a temporary court order says. If no one pays, the lender can foreclose on both of you, because the loan stays in both names until it is paid off or refinanced. A late or missed payment can hurt both credit reports even if one spouse moved out.

Can I sell my share of the house without my spouse?

Sometimes. After the divorce, when you and your ex own the house as tenants in common, you can usually sell your share without their signature. The buyer becomes their co-owner. A share sells for less than half of a full-house sale. During the case, automatic orders in many states block this.

Bottom line

You can sell a house before, during or after divorce. Before filing, everyone on the deed signs. During the case, get written consent or a court order. After the decree, follow the decree. If you can’t agree, a judge or a partition case can force a sale.

The most common mistake we see is waiting because people think they have to. If you both want out, an agreed sale during the case is routine, and every month of waiting costs money.
