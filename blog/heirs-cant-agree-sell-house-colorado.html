Heirs Can't Agree on a Colorado House? The Fight Is Over Who Becomes Personal Representative, and Colorado Lets the Majority by Value Decide

A Colorado personal representative can usually sell an estate's house without a court order and without every heir's signature. So when siblings disagree, the real contest is over who gets appointed. Colorado's statute gives a tiebreaker most families don't know about: in a contested appointment, the court may choose the person acceptable to heirs whose shares add up to more than half the estate. Here's how that plays out, and the brakes an outvoted heir still has. Or ask us a question, free.

Someone in your family died owning a house in Colorado, and the heirs don't agree. One wants to sell now. One wants to move in, or rent it, or fix it up first. One lives in another state and just wants it over. In most states, the next question would be how to get everyone to sign. In Colorado, that's usually the wrong question.

Colorado follows the Uniform Probate Code. Its personal representative, the executor or administrator, has broad power to sell estate real estate without asking a judge and without collecting the heirs' signatures. That means the family argument isn't really about the sale. It's about who gets appointed to make it, and Colorado's appointment statute has a rule that decides many of those fights.

🗳️ Colorado's Tiebreaker: Heirs Holding More Than Half the Estate Pick Who Serves

Colorado's priority list for appointment, C.R.S. § 15-12-203, starts with whoever the will names, then a surviving spouse who is a beneficiary, other beneficiaries, a surviving spouse, and then "other heirs." Three parts of that statute matter when children inherit and disagree:

  • 🤝 Equal priority means joint consent. When two or more people share a priority, as siblings who are all heirs do, those who don't renounce "must concur in nominating another to act for them or in applying for appointment." One sibling can't quietly claim the job over the others' objection.
  • ⚖️ Objections go to formal proceedings. An objection to an appointment "can be made only in formal proceedings," meaning before a judge with notice to everyone.
  • 🗳️ The majority-by-value rule. When an heir or devisee with a substantial interest objects to someone whose priority doesn't come from the will, the court "may appoint a person who is acceptable to heirs and devisees whose interests in the estate appear to be worth in total more than half of the probable distributable value or, in default of this accord, any suitable person."

Read that last rule as a family. If three children inherit equally, any two of them together hold more than half. If they agree on one of themselves, or on a neutral professional, the court can appoint that person over the third sibling's objection. If no group reaches more than half, the court can appoint "any suitable person," which often means a professional fiduciary or attorney whose fees come out of the estate.

The rule is permissive ("may"), not automatic, and a will that names an executor changes everything: that person's priority comes from the will, and the majority tiebreaker doesn't override it. But in the common case of no will and adult children, it is the single most useful thing to understand.

⚠️ The warning that matters most: in Colorado, the person who controls the sale is whoever holds letters as personal representative, not whoever has the most heirs on their side in a group text. If you disagree with a sibling's plan, act at the appointment stage. Once someone is appointed and the house is under contract, undoing it is far harder than shaping who gets appointed in the first place.

🔑 Once Appointed, a Colorado Personal Representative Sells Without a Judge

Colorado's statute directs a personal representative to proceed with administration without court order unless supervised administration applies, and lists selling or leasing estate land among the transactions a personal representative may carry out. In practice that means:

  • 🖋️ The personal representative signs the listing agreement, the contract and the deed. The heirs don't.
  • 📈 They must act as a fiduciary: get a fair price, avoid self-dealing, and account to the heirs.
  • 🏠 A personal representative who wants to buy the house personally faces conflict-of-interest rules, and that sale can be set aside unless the will, the heirs' consent or the court authorizes it.

That's why this fight comes first. An heir who wants to keep the house, or who thinks the price is too low, has to use the tools in the next sections, because simply refusing to sign accomplishes nothing.

📮 The Notice Rules That Keep an Appointment From Happening in Secret

Colorado has two kinds of probate: informal, handled by the court's registrar without a hearing, and formal, before a judge. Informal appointment can be fast, which can make it feel like a race. Colorado builds in two notices that slow the race down:

  1. Before appointment. Under C.R.S. § 15-12-310, someone seeking informal appointment has to give notice of their intention to anyone with a prior or equal right to appointment who hasn't waived it. Siblings who are equal heirs are entitled to that notice.
  2. After appointment. Within 30 days of appointment, the personal representative must inform the heirs and devisees of the appointment and where the court file is.

If you get either notice and disagree, call a Colorado probate lawyer that week. That's the moment to file an objection and ask for formal proceedings, before the house is listed.

🛑 Brakes an Outvoted Heir Can Still Pull

Losing the appointment fight doesn't leave an heir powerless. Colorado gives interested persons several tools, each with a different cost and purpose.

🚧 A restraining order on a specific act

Targeted, fast

Under C.R.S. § 15-12-607, an interested person can ask the court to restrain the personal representative from a particular act, like selling the house below a stated price or to a relative, while the court hears the dispute. It's the tool for "stop this sale," not "stop this probate."

👩‍⚖️ Supervised administration

Puts the court back in charge

Colorado allows any interested person to petition for supervised administration, under § 15-12-501 and following. A supervised personal representative is subject to the court's direction and needs court approval for distributions, and the court can restrict the authority to sell. It adds time and expense, so it fits an estate where trust has broken down, not a disagreement about paint colors.

🔁 Removal for cause

For real misconduct

Under § 15-12-611, the court can remove a personal representative for cause, such as mismanaging the estate, failing to perform duties or misrepresenting facts in getting appointed. Living in the house rent-free while delaying the estate, or trying to sell it to a friend at a discount, are typical facts heirs bring.

🤝 A buyout before the sale

Often the cheapest

The heir who wants the house can offer to buy out the others at an appraised price, with the personal representative's agreement. It ends the fight without a hearing, if the buyer can finance it.

👨‍👩‍👧 Stepfamilies: What a Surviving Spouse Takes When the Children Aren't Hers

Many Colorado heir fights involve a stepparent. With no will, Colorado's intestacy statute, C.R.S. § 15-11-102, gives a surviving spouse the whole estate only if every surviving descendant of the deceased is also the spouse's descendant and the spouse has no other children. When the deceased had children from an earlier relationship, the spouse takes the first $150,000 plus half of the balance. When all the children are shared but the spouse has children from another relationship, it's the first $225,000 plus half. Both dollar figures are adjusted for cost of living each year, so the current amounts are higher.

The spouse also ranks ahead of "other heirs" for appointment. In a stepfamily, that often means the stepparent becomes personal representative, controls the sale, and receives a large share of a modest estate. The children's leverage is the notice and objection process, the restraining order, and a careful accounting, not a veto.

🏛️ Denver Probate Court and Everywhere Else in Colorado

Where you file depends on the county. Denver has a dedicated Denver Probate Court, one of the few stand-alone probate courts in the country. Everywhere else in Colorado, probate is handled by the district court for the county, with its own probate magistrates and registrars. Practices on informal filings, hearing dates and how fast letters issue vary from courthouse to courthouse.

Also check the recorder before anything is filed. Colorado allows beneficiary deeds under C.R.S. § 15-15-401 and following. If the deceased recorded one before death, the house passes directly to the named beneficiary, the personal representative doesn't control it, and the dispute becomes a co-ownership problem instead of a probate one.

🧾 After the Estate Closes: Co-Owning Heirs and Partition

Sometimes the family avoids a sale during probate, and the personal representative deeds the house to all the heirs together. Now they're co-owners, and the dynamics change: no one has power to sell the whole house, and a normal sale needs every owner's signature. Any co-owner can sell their own share or file a partition action in district court under Colorado's partition statute, C.R.S. § 38-28-101 and following, asking the court to divide the property or sell it. Our partition lawsuit explainer covers how those cases run.

For most families, selling during probate with a personal representative everyone can live with is cheaper and faster than distributing the house to five people and litigating later.

🧮 A Denver Bungalow, Three Siblings, Two Outcomes

Dad died without a will, leaving a 1940s bungalow in Denver's Harvey Park area to three adult children equally. Updated, it might bring $560,000; it needs a sewer line, windows and a kitchen, about $70,000. The oldest sibling filed for informal appointment; the other two think she's rushing a sale to a contractor friend.

  • 🗳️ Majority route: the two younger siblings, holding two-thirds by value, object, ask for formal proceedings, and propose a neutral Denver fiduciary. The court can appoint that person. Listed as-is, the house sells for around $470,000. After about 6.5 percent in commission and closing costs, and six months of carrying costs, each sibling nets roughly $144,000, less their share of the fiduciary's and lawyers' fees.
  • 💵 Cash route, agreed early: at 60 to 80 percent of the $560,000 after-repair value, offers would run $336,000 to $448,000. On this house we'd likely be around $395,000 to $420,000. That's less than listing, and the right answer only if the family values a quick, conflict-free closing over the difference, or the sewer and foundation issues scare off financed buyers.

Here, an early cash sale at that range works out to roughly $132,000 to $140,000 per sibling, with no carrying costs or fiduciary. The listing route nets a few thousand to about $12,000 more per sibling before the fiduciary's and lawyers' fees, which can eat much of that gap. If the siblings can agree on a personal representative without a contested hearing, listing is still the better deal; if it takes a court fight, the numbers get close.

📋 What To Do This Week in a Colorado Heir Dispute

  1. 📜 Find out whether there's a will that names a personal representative, and check the county recorder for a beneficiary deed.
  2. 🏛️ Search the district court's or Denver Probate Court's records to see whether anyone has filed for appointment.
  3. 🧮 Write down each heir's share and see whether any group adds up to more than half.
  4. 🗣️ Propose one personal representative in writing, or a neutral professional, and ask the other heirs to sign nominations.
  5. ⚖️ If someone filed without your agreement, or you received a notice you disagree with, call a Colorado probate lawyer about an objection or a restraining order now.
  6. 🔐 Keep the heat on, the insurer informed and the sidewalks shoveled.

✅ Bottom Line for Colorado Heirs

In Colorado, heirs don't vote on the sale; the personal representative makes it. So put your energy into the appointment. Siblings with equal priority must concur, objections go to a judge, and in a contested case the court may appoint the person backed by heirs holding more than half the estate's value, or a neutral professional if no one has a majority. After that, a restraining order, supervised administration or removal can protect an outvoted heir from a bad sale.

If the family can settle on a personal representative and the house is sound, list it. If it isn't, or the arguing is costing more than the house, ask us. We'll tell you which route fits before we talk price.

❓ Frequently Asked Questions

Who gets to be personal representative when Colorado heirs disagree?

The priority list in C.R.S. § 15-12-203 starts with the will's nominee, then spouses and beneficiaries, then other heirs. Heirs with equal priority must concur, and in a contested formal proceeding the court may appoint a person acceptable to heirs whose interests total more than half the estate, or any suitable person.

Can one sibling get appointed in Colorado without telling the others?

Not properly. A person seeking informal appointment must give notice of that intention to anyone with a prior or equal right to appointment who hasn't waived it, and a newly appointed personal representative must inform the heirs within 30 days. An heir who objects can demand formal proceedings.

Can a Colorado personal representative sell the house if some heirs object?

Generally yes. Colorado's probate code lets a personal representative proceed without court order and lists selling estate land among authorized transactions. Heirs who object have to go to court, for example to seek a restraining order, supervised administration or removal.

How can a Colorado heir stop a bad sale of the family house?

By petitioning the court. Under C.R.S. § 15-12-607 an interested person can ask for an order restraining the personal representative from a specific act, such as a sale below a stated price or to an insider, while the dispute is heard.

What is supervised administration in Colorado?

A form of probate, available on petition by an interested person, in which the personal representative is subject to the court's direction and needs court approval for distributions. The court can limit the power to sell. It adds cost and time and suits estates where trust has broken down.

Can a Colorado personal representative be removed?

Yes, for cause. Under C.R.S. § 15-12-611, the court can remove a personal representative for reasons such as mismanaging the estate, failing to perform duties, or misrepresenting facts to get appointed. Removal requires a petition and a hearing.

How much does a stepparent inherit in Colorado if there's no will?

If the deceased had children who aren't the surviving spouse's, the spouse receives the first $150,000 plus half of the balance under C.R.S. § 15-11-102, with that dollar figure adjusted annually for cost of living. The spouse takes everything only if all descendants are shared and the spouse has no other children.

Which court handles probate for a Colorado house?

In Denver, the Denver Probate Court, a court dedicated to probate. In every other county, the district court for the county where the deceased lived handles probate, usually through probate magistrates and a registrar for informal filings.

Can Colorado heirs force a sale after the estate deeds them the house?

Yes. Once the heirs own the house together, any co-owner can sell their own share or file a partition action in district court under C.R.S. § 38-28-101 and following, asking the court to divide the property or order it sold and split the proceeds.

Is it better for Colorado heirs who disagree to list the house or take a cash offer?

If the heirs can agree on a personal representative and the house is in decent shape, listing usually nets more. A cash sale tends to fit when the house needs major work, an occupying heir won't cooperate with showings, or the family wants one firm number quickly.
