Selling a Co-Owned House in Kentucky: Dower Means Your Co-Owner's Spouse Has a Say Even When They Are Not on the Deed

Kentucky is one of the few states that kept dower and curtesy. Under KRS 392.020 a surviving spouse can claim a life estate in one-third of real estate the other spouse owned at any time during the marriage, unless that right was relinquished. In practice that puts the husband or wife of every married co-owner at the closing table, whether or not they live in the house. Add a survivorship rule that only works when the deed says so, and a court that presumes a house cannot be divided, and Kentucky co-owners face their own set of problems. Or ask us a question, free.

💍 Kentucky Kept Dower, and It Attaches to Every Married Co-Owner's Share

KRS 392.020 gives a surviving spouse two separate rights in the other spouse's real estate. For property the deceased spouse still owned at death, the survivor takes a fee interest in one-half of the surplus real estate. For property the deceased spouse owned at any time during the marriage but not at death, the survivor keeps a life estate in one-third — "unless the survivor's right to such interest has been barred, forfeited or relinquished."

That second clause is the one that matters to buyers. If a married co-owner sells their share without their spouse releasing dower, the spouse can, on outliving the seller, claim a life estate in one-third of what was sold. The buyer would take the house subject to that claim. No title insurer wants that risk, so Kentucky closings routinely include the non-owner spouse signing the deed to release dower and curtesy.

  • 🏠 It does not matter whether the spouse lives there. Unlike homestead consent rules in some states, Kentucky dower attaches to real estate owned during the marriage — an inherited rental, a vacant house, a share of the family farm.
  • 🧾 It reaches fractional shares. A one-quarter interest inherited from a parent is real estate the co-owner owns during the marriage. The spouse's dower attaches to that quarter.
  • 🚪 Divorce ends it. A finalized divorce generally cuts off dower. A pending divorce does not, which is one reason co-owned sales stall while a co-owner's marriage is ending. See our guide on selling a house during a divorce.
⚠️ Ask this question first: which of the owners are married, and will each spouse sign a dower release? If one spouse refuses, the clean whole-house sale you agreed on may not close. Find out before you sign a listing agreement or accept an offer.

🖊️ When a Spouse Won't Release Dower

A non-owner spouse who refuses to sign usually has a reason: a bad marriage, a pending divorce, a belief that the price is too low, or a hope of leverage. You have a few options:

  • 🤝 Negotiate. Show the spouse the numbers. Dower is a contingent life estate in a third of the seller's share; its present value is small next to a cash closing that pays the household now.
  • ⚖️ Bring everyone into a court-ordered sale. A sale ordered in a Kentucky circuit court action with all interested parties joined, including spouses, can convey clean title and move each spouse's claim to the proceeds. That is slower and more expensive than a signature.
  • 💵 Sell to a buyer who will accept the complication. We sometimes buy a co-owner's share where a spouse will not sign, priced for the extra risk. That is rarely your best price; it can be your fastest exit.

📃 Survivorship Only Works If the Deed Says So

Kentucky's default rule, KRS 381.120, is that when a joint tenant dies, their share passes to their own heirs or devisees, not to the surviving co-owner. KRS 381.130 carves out the exception: survivorship applies "when it manifestly appears, from the tenor of the instrument, that it was intended that the part of the one dying should belong to the others." Deeds drafted for married couples in Kentucky usually say "for and during their joint lives, with remainder in fee simple to the survivor" or similar; deeds to siblings or partners often do not.

So before you assume your late co-owner's share went to you, read the deed. If it lacks clear survivorship language, that share passed through their estate — possibly to a spouse with a dower claim and to children who now co-own with you.

✂️ Ending Survivorship With One Recorded Deed

KRS 381.130(2) lets a joint tenant of real property sever their own interest during their lifetime "by deed or other instrument" that expresses the intent to partition the interest and is recorded with the county clerk. The severance takes effect when recorded and converts that owner's interest into a tenancy in common. Nobody else has to sign.

One firm exception: residential property owned exclusively by a husband and wife as joint tenants with right of survivorship and actually occupied by them as their principal residence cannot be severed this way. Spouses in that position need each other's agreement or a divorce court.

⚖️ If You Cannot Agree: Kentucky Presumes the House Cannot Be Split

Any co-owner who shares title "in such manner that a conveyance by them jointly would pass a fee simple title" can sue for sale or division in the circuit court of the county where the property lies, under KRS 389A.030. Kentucky's statute does something most states do not: it presumes indivisibility. Unless a party raises the issue in the pleadings and the court is satisfied the property can be divided without materially impairing any interest's value, the court orders a sale, conducted by the master commissioner at public auction. The case is tried without a jury.

Two limits matter to co-owners. The statute does not reach tenants by the entirety in residential property they actually occupy as a principal residence, so married owners living in the house are outside it. And a public commissioner's sale is a discounted way to sell a house. We cover the auction mechanics in our national partition lawsuit guide; for most Kentucky co-owners the existence of that forced sale is leverage for a negotiated exit, not a destination.

💸 What a Shared Kentucky House Costs to Hold

Kentucky property tax is moderate by national standards — roughly 0.8% to 1.2% of market value a year, depending on the county and any city or school levies. On a $220,000 Lexington house that is about $150–$220 a month. Add $100–$200 for insurance (vacant-dwelling coverage costs more and covers less), $120–$250 for utilities, and lawn care. Kentucky also charges a real estate transfer tax of fifty cents per $500 of value when the deed is recorded — about $220 on a $220,000 sale.

Older Kentucky housing stock brings its own costs: basements that take water, aging electrical in pre-1960 homes, and roofs that hail and wind wear down. A co-owned house that nobody lives in tends to accumulate exactly those problems.

💵 A Lexington House With Two Married Owners

A brother and sister inherited their father's three-bedroom in Lexington, half each. Repaired, it would be worth about $260,000. It needs a roof, HVAC and a kitchen — about $45,000. Both siblings are married. The brother's wife will not sign anything.

  • 🏷️ Everyone signs a listing: as-is maybe $210,000; minus 6% commission ($12,600), transfer tax and closing costs (about $2,500), roughly $195,000 — about $97,500 each. Best result, but it needs the brother's wife.
  • 🙋 Sister buys the brother out: about $105,000 for his half at a $210,000 value — and she still needs his wife's dower release on his deed.
  • ⚖️ Circuit court sale: 9–18 months, attorney and commissioner costs, and an auction price that could land well below a listing.
  • 💵 Cash: 60–80% of $260,000 is $156,000–$208,000; for this condition a realistic whole-house offer is about $170,000–$180,000. For the sister's half alone, expect less, because the buyer inherits the dispute.

The right answer here is usually to solve the dower signature, not to sell at a discount. We would tell the sister that before making an offer.

✅ Bottom Line

In Kentucky, a co-owned house has more stakeholders than the deed shows. Every married owner's spouse holds a dower interest that a buyer will want released, survivorship exists only where the deed clearly says so, and a joint tenant can end survivorship alone with a recorded deed. If agreement fails, a circuit court will presume the house cannot be split and sell it at a commissioner's auction. Start by lining up the spouses' signatures. If that works, list. If it does not, compare a buyout, a court sale and a cash offer side by side.

❓ Questions Kentucky Co-Owners Ask Us

Does my co-owner's spouse have to sign to sell a house in Kentucky?

Usually yes. Under KRS 392.020 a surviving spouse can claim a life estate in one-third of real estate the other spouse owned during the marriage unless that right was relinquished. Buyers and title insurers therefore ask each married owner's spouse to sign the deed to release dower or curtesy, even when the spouse is not on title.

Does dower apply if the spouse never lived in the Kentucky house?

Yes. Kentucky dower attaches to real estate a spouse owned during the marriage, whether or not the couple lived there. An inherited share of a parent's house or a rental property is covered the same way as the family home.

What happens if a spouse refuses to release dower on a Kentucky sale?

The sale of that owner's interest can stall, because a buyer would take subject to a possible future life estate claim. Options include negotiating with the spouse, a circuit court sale with all interested parties joined, or selling to a buyer willing to price in the risk. A finalized divorce generally ends dower.

If my co-owner dies, do I automatically get their share of a Kentucky house?

Only if the deed clearly provides survivorship. KRS 381.120 sends a deceased joint tenant's share to their heirs or devisees, and KRS 381.130 makes an exception only when it manifestly appears from the instrument that the survivor was meant to take.

Can one joint tenant end survivorship in Kentucky without the others?

Yes, in most cases. KRS 381.130(2) lets a joint tenant partition their interest by a recorded deed or other instrument expressing that intent, which converts it to a tenancy in common. It does not apply to residential property owned only by a husband and wife as joint tenants with survivorship and occupied as their principal residence.

Can one Kentucky co-owner sell only their share?

Yes. A tenant in common can sell their undivided interest without the other owners' consent, though a married owner's spouse will normally need to release dower. The buyer becomes a co-owner with the same rights the seller had.

Can a Kentucky co-owner force the sale of the whole house?

Yes. KRS 389A.030 lets any co-owner sue in circuit court for sale or division. The court presumes the property is indivisible unless a party raises the issue and proves it can be divided without materially impairing its value, and a sale is then conducted by the master commissioner. Tenants by the entirety in their occupied principal residence are excluded.

Is there a transfer tax when co-owners sell a Kentucky house?

Yes. Kentucky imposes a real estate transfer tax of fifty cents per $500 of value, collected by the county clerk when the deed is recorded. Who pays it is negotiable, though sellers commonly do.

How much are property taxes on a co-owned house in Kentucky?

Kentucky's effective property tax rates are moderate, commonly around 0.8% to 1.2% of market value a year depending on the county, city and school district. The bill is owed regardless of which co-owner lives there, and unpaid taxes can lead to a certificate of delinquency sale.

Should Kentucky co-owners sell to a cash buyer or list the house?

If the house is in decent condition and every owner and spouse will sign, listing usually nets more. A cash sale, typically 60–80% of repaired value, makes sense when the house needs major work, a required signature is missing, or the owners need to close in weeks.
