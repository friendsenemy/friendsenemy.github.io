In California, a Co-Owner Buyout Comes With a New Tax Bill. Run That Number First.

California taxes most long-held houses on an old purchase price. Buy out your co-owner and the assessor reprices the half that moved. On a house held for decades, that can double the annual bill, and it's often what really decides whether the buyout happens at all.

By Dustin Ray, Founder of Pages of Purpose LLC. Not legal advice or tax advice — laws differ by state, and California property-tax outcomes depend on exactly how a transfer is structured. Talk to an attorney licensed in California, where the property is, and a tax professional before you sign anything.

Most co-owner disputes in California look like a fight about fairness. One sibling lives in the house and wants to stay. The other wants their money. Each has a lawyer's opinion. Then someone finally asks the assessor's office what happens to the bill, and the whole conversation changes.

Under Proposition 13, a California house is generally taxed on its value when it last changed hands, with small annual increases. So the house your parents bought in 1995 may be assessed at a fraction of what it would sell for. That cheap tax base is a real asset, and it doesn't survive the buyout intact. Before you decide who's being unreasonable, put a number on it.

🧾 The Reassessment Math Nobody Runs Until It's Too Late

The Board of Equalization puts the rule simply: only the portion that changes ownership gets reappraised. If 50% of a property is transferred, the assessor reassesses 50% at current market value as of the transfer date. The other half keeps its old base.

Here's what that does in practice. Say a house in Torrance has a $900,000 market value and a Prop 13 assessed value of $260,000, and two siblings own it 50/50. With local add-ons, assume the tax runs about 1.2% of assessed value.

 TodayAfter a 50% buyoutAfter a sale to a stranger
Assessed value$260,000$580,000 ($130,000 old half + $450,000 new half)$900,000
Annual tax at about 1.2%About $3,100About $7,000About $10,800
MonthlyAbout $260About $580About $900

So on top of finding $450,000 (likely by refinancing at today's rates), the sister who stays takes on another $320 a month in tax, forever. That's about $3,840 a year. It doesn't kill the deal, but it means a fair-market buyout costs her more than it looks like, and she's likely to know it. Expect her to argue for a discount. Whether that's fair is up to you. Either way, it's not bad faith; it's arithmetic.

⚠️ Don't sign a quitclaim first. Deeds in California start the reassessment process, and exclusions have deadlines and filing requirements. A sibling who "just signs over" a share to fix the problem later can create the very reassessment you were trying to avoid. Get advice on the structure before anything is recorded.

🛟 Exclusions That Can Shrink the Bill, and the Ones That Won't

People assume family transfers are exempt. Since Proposition 19 took effect in 2021, that's much less true.

  • Parent to child. Prop 19 keeps an exclusion only if the child makes the house their principal residence within a year, files for it, and the value stays inside the cap, which starts around $1 million above the parent's assessed value and adjusts for inflation. A child who doesn't move in gets reassessed.
  • Sibling to sibling. There's no general exclusion for one sibling buying from another. This surprises nearly everyone. If you're still in a parent's trust or estate, a non-pro-rata distribution can change the analysis, so ask before the trust distributes anything.
  • Co-owner's death. Revenue and Taxation Code Section 62.3 excludes a transfer between two people who together own 100% as joint tenants or tenants in common, where both lived there as their principal residence for the full year before death, and the survivor ends up with 100%. The survivor signs an affidavit under penalty of perjury. It's narrow, but it helps unmarried partners and siblings who truly shared a home.
  • Very small slices. Section 65.1 skips reappraisal for an interest worth under 5% of the property and under $10,000, adding up all transfers in the year. That rarely fits a house at California prices.
  • Same proportions. A change in how you hold title that leaves each owner's percentage the same, like moving from joint tenants to tenants in common, is excluded. That's why you can often sever a joint tenancy without touching the tax.

One more trap: the BOE notes that a person who added joint tenants can be an "original transferor," and once that person is gone, the whole property may be reappraised. If a parent added a child to title, check that before you plan around the old base.

⚖️ If You Can't Agree: Partition After the 2023 Law

A California co-owner generally has the right to partition under Code of Civil Procedure Section 872.710. What changed is how sales happen. The Partition of Real Property Act, CCP Sections 874.311 to 874.323, covers actions filed on or after January 1, 2023, for property held in tenancy in common where no recorded agreement binds all the cotenants on partition. California began with an heirs-only law and broadened it, so it reaches most co-owned houses, not just inherited ones. That's a wider net than Georgia's or Florida's versions.

Here's the sequence for a cotenant asking for a sale:

  1. The court determines value under Section 874.316, usually after an independent appraisal, with a window to object.
  2. A buyout offer goes to the others. Under Section 874.317, once value is set, any cotenant except one who asked for a sale has 45 days to elect to buy all the interests of those who did.
  3. A payment date. The court sets a date at least 60 days after notice for electing cotenants to pay into court.
  4. If no one buys, the court decides between partition in kind and partition by sale. For a single-family house, it's almost always sale.
  5. Open-market sale. Section 874.320 makes an open-market sale the default, with a broker, unless the court finds sealed bids or an auction would serve the group better.

Note what's missing: the courthouse auction. A California partition usually ends with a listing, so the forced-sale discount that hurts in other states is smaller here. It also means the partition result can land reasonably close to a voluntary sale, less lawyers and time. And the buyout election is where the reassessment math resurfaces: a co-owner who elects still takes on the new assessment on the share they buy.

Joint tenants should note that the act speaks to tenancies in common, and under Civil Code Section 683.2 a joint tenant can sever as to their own interest without the others' consent, unless a written agreement among the joint tenants says otherwise, and recording matters. Ask your attorney about the order of operations. Spouses holding as community property generally deal with this in family court; see our guide on selling a house during a divorce and our California divorce guide.

What partition costs in California

Bigger numbers, longer clock

Plan on $15,000–$30,000 per side for a moderately contested case and $40,000–$75,000 or more if it's fought hard, and 12–24 months overall. Section 874.010 lists partition costs that can include attorney fees for work done for the common benefit, and Section 874.040 lets the court apportion them, generally by interests or as equity requires. Under Section 874.321.5, the court can't apportion costs to a party opposing partition unless that's equitable and fits the act's purposes, so don't count on your opponent sharing the bill. Referees, appraisers, title reports and broker commission all come out too.

🏡 The Co-Owner in Possession: What They Owe You

California generally lets a co-owner live in the house without paying the others for that alone. The path to rent runs through ouster. Civil Code Section 843 lets an owner out of possession establish one by written demand. The demand has to cite the section and state the time for offering concurrent possession, it's served the way a summons is served, and the ouster is established 60 days after service if the occupant doesn't offer unconditional concurrent possession. So in California, the letter itself matters. A casual text doesn't do this job, so have an attorney prepare it before you assume you're owed anything.

When the house eventually sells or a buyout closes, expect an accounting. Courts look at who paid the mortgage, taxes, insurance and necessary repairs. An occupant may claim credit for all of it. You may push back with the value of their exclusive use. Get these figures on one page before a mediator asks for them, since mediators are common in these cases and many settle there.

🔀 California Co-Owner Paths, Rated

Sell together on the open market

Usually wins in California

In most of the state, the highest net for both owners is a listing. Nobody takes on a reassessed tax bill, since the buyer does. You split real market value. If the occupant is resisting, show them what a buyout would really cost them per month.

Discounted buyout that shares the tax hit

Fairest when one owner must stay

Agree on an appraised value, then split some of the present value of the higher tax, or the commission you'd both have saved. If the occupant can refinance, this closes fast and quietly. Check for a Prop 19 route if a parent is involved.

Partition under the 2023 act

Reliable, slow and expensive

Use it when the other owner won't deal at all. You'll end up with a fair-market result, less a large legal bill and a year or two of waiting.

Sell your share to a buyer like us

For speed, not for price

A tenant in common can sell an interest without consent. A partial-interest buyer pays much less than your slice of market value, since they take on the partition and a co-tenant they didn't choose. It makes sense when you can't fund a lawsuit, or need to be done now.

✅ Bottom Line

A California co-owner dispute is rarely only about the house's value. It's about the old tax base, who loses it, and how much that's worth. Run the reassessment number first, check whether any exclusion truly fits, and then decide. For most sound houses, an open-market sale leaves both of you ahead. If the other owner won't sign, the 2023 act gives you a fair path, just not a fast one.

❓ Frequently Asked Questions

Will buying out my co-owner raise the property taxes in California?

Usually, yes. Only the transferred share is reassessed. If you buy a 50% interest, the assessor raises that half to current market value and the other half keeps its Prop 13 base, so the bill can double or more on a long-held house.

Is a transfer between siblings excluded from reassessment in California?

There's no general sibling exclusion. A parent-to-child transfer may be excluded under Prop 19 if the child moves in, but a sibling buying from a sibling is normally reassessed. A distribution through a parent's trust or estate can be different, so get advice first.

Does my co-owner's death trigger reassessment in California?

It can. Section 62.3 excludes a transfer between two people who together own 100% as joint tenants or tenants in common, if both lived there as their principal residence for the full year before death and the survivor signs an affidavit. Otherwise, expect reassessment of the transferred share.

Does severing a joint tenancy trigger reassessment?

Generally not, because a change in how title is held that leaves each owner's share the same is excluded. Watch for original transferor issues if a parent added a child to title.

Does the Partition of Real Property Act apply to me if I'm not an heir?

Likely yes. It covers actions filed on or after January 1, 2023, for real property held in tenancy in common with no binding record agreement on partition, not only inherited houses.

How long does my co-owner have to buy me out in a California partition?

Under CCP Section 874.317, an eligible cotenant has 45 days after notice to elect to buy, and the court sets a payment date at least 60 days after its later notice.

Will a California partition sale be an auction?

Usually not any more. Section 874.320 makes an open-market sale through a broker the default, unless the court finds a sealed-bid sale or auction would be better for the cotenants as a group.

Does my co-owner owe me rent for living in a California house?

Not automatically. An owner out of possession usually needs to establish an ouster. Under Civil Code Section 843, that takes a formal written demand served like a summons, and the ouster is established 60 days after service if the occupant doesn't offer concurrent possession.

Who pays attorney fees in a California partition?

The court can apportion common-benefit fees and costs under Sections 874.010 and 874.040, but under Section 874.321.5 it won't apportion costs to an opposing party unless that's equitable and fits the act's purposes. Expect to carry your own fees for a while.

Should co-owners in California list or take a cash offer?

For most sound houses, list. The buyer bears the reassessment, and you split full market value. A cash sale fits a seriously damaged house, an owner who can't be reached, or a hard deadline.
