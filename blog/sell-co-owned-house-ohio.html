In Ohio, a Partition Can End With Your Co-Owner Buying You Out on a Two-Year Payment Plan

Most states treat partition as a forced sale. Ohio puts an option in the middle: once a commissioner values the house, any owner can elect to take it at that price, and the default terms are a third down and the rest over two years. Decide whether you want to be the owner who elects or the owner who gets paid before anyone files.

By Dustin Ray, Founder of Pages of Purpose LLC. Not legal advice — laws differ by state, Ohio common pleas courts handle these cases differently from county to county, and your deed controls. Talk to an attorney licensed in Ohio, where the property is, before you act on anything here.

Here is the conversation we have with Ohio co-owners more than any other. Someone says, "I'll just file a partition and force the sale." Sometimes that works exactly that way. But an Ohio partition doesn't go straight from lawsuit to auction. It passes through a checkpoint that most states don't have, and at that checkpoint your co-owner can keep the house without a single bidder showing up.

If you're the one who wants out, that may mean you get paid, but in installments, at a number set by a court appointee. If you're the one who wants to stay, it may be the cheapest financing you'll ever find. Either way, the time to work out which side of that election you want is before the complaint is filed, because the decision shapes everything you do this month.

🗳️ The Election: How Ohio Lets One Owner Take the Whole House

Ohio partition law sits in Chapter 5307 of the Revised Code. Tenants in common and survivorship tenants can all be compelled to partition under Section 5307.01, and the case is filed in the court of common pleas in the county where the house sits. Here's the sequence once the court finds you hold an interest:

  1. The court appoints a commissioner. Section 5307.04 calls for one suitable disinterested person, and any party can ask for up to two more.
  2. The commissioner reports back. A single-family house can't be split into lots without hurting its value, so the commissioner returns that finding with a "just valuation," as Section 5307.09 puts it.
  3. Any party can elect to take the house at that value. If the court approves the return, one or more parties may elect to take the estate at the appraised value, paying the others their share of it.
  4. If nobody elects, it can go to auction. Under Section 5307.11, a sheriff or a licensed auctioneer sells at public auction. Section 5307.12 says it can't sell for less than two-thirds of the commissioner's value.

Step three is the Ohio feature that changes the strategy. And the payment terms are what make it bite. Section 5307.10 says that unless the court directs full cash for good cause, or all the parties agree, the terms are one-third cash, one-third in one year, and one-third in two years, with interest, secured to the court's satisfaction.

⚠️ Read this before you file. Filing a partition does not guarantee cash at closing. If your co-owner elects and the court applies the default terms, a third of your money could arrive two years from now. If you need all of it soon, ask your attorney about asking for full cash on "good cause" under Section 5307.10, and build that into any settlement offer.

What the election looks like with real numbers

Say two siblings each own half of a house in Whitehall, and the commissioner values it at $210,000. The sister who lives there elects. She owes her brother $105,000, on default terms: $35,000 up front, $35,000 in a year, and $35,000 in two years, plus interest. She takes the deed once the payments are made or secured. If she can't or won't elect, the floor at auction is $140,000 (two-thirds of $210,000), which would put her brother's half at $70,000 before costs. That $35,000 gap is why the commissioner's number and the election are the real battleground, not the lawsuit itself.

When more than one owner wants to elect, expect a harder fight and get counsel; the statute speaks of one or more parties electing, and courts sort out competing elections on the case's facts.

📜 Ohio Deeds: "Survivorship" Doesn't Work the Way You Were Told

Ohio replaced the old joint tenancy with its own statutory creature, the survivorship tenancy, in Section 5302.20. It has a feature that matters enormously if you were planning to sell just your share.

In many states, one joint tenant can deed their share to an outsider and that ends survivorship for that share. Ohio doesn't work that way. Under Section 5302.20(C)(2), a conveyance from fewer than all the survivorship tenants to an outsider vests title in the buyer "conditioned on the survivorship of the grantor." Translate that: if you sell your interest and then die before your co-owner, the buyer's interest is gone.

That makes a survivorship share close to unsellable to anyone careful. It's also why partition is often the only real exit for these deeds. Section 5307.041 says that when partition is granted among survivorship tenants, the court figures each share as if they were tenants in common. So the lawsuit does what a deed to a stranger can't.

That last row comes from Section 5302.20(C)(5): divorce, dissolution or annulment immediately turns a married couple's survivorship tenancy into a tenancy in common, unless the judgment expressly keeps it. Also, creditors matter here: a lien can break a survivorship tenancy too, under Section 5302.20(C)(4).

💍 Dower: The Ohio Signature Few People See Coming

Most states abolished dower long ago. Ohio didn't. Section 2103.02 gives a spouse a dower interest: a life estate in one-third of the real property the other spouse owned during the marriage, unless relinquished or barred. While both spouses are alive, it's mostly an inchoate claim, but it clouds title. If your married co-owner sells without their spouse signing, the dower claim can survive the sale, and any title company will catch it.

Practically, every married owner's spouse needs to sign the deed to release dower, even if they've never been on title and have no interest in the house. It applies to a married co-owner's share of a sale, a buyout between you, and a sale of one share to us. Dower ends with a divorce, and Section 2103.02 lists the rules for when it ends at death. In a partition, Sections 5307.17 and 5307.18 tell the court how to deal with it, so the spouse has to be named in the case. Count the spouses before you count the money.

⏳ Inherited a Share? Ohio Can Make You Wait a Year

If you got your interest by inheritance, one more clock applies. Section 5307.03 says that when title came by descent or devise from an Ohio resident, the court can't order partition within one year of the death, unless you show that the estate's claims are paid, secured, or covered by the personal property. An estate that has been properly settled can usually make that showing. An estate nobody opened usually can't, and that's the common situation when siblings each "own" a parent's house but nothing was ever filed. Our guide on what to do when heirs can't agree covers that fight.

🏠 When One Owner Lives There and Won't Talk to You

Section 5307.21 lets one tenant in common recover from another their share of rents and profits received, "according to the justice and equity of the case." If your co-owner is collecting rent from a tenant, you have a clean claim to your slice. If they simply live there, it's less clear. Generally, a co-owner has a right to possess the whole, and the owner who's out has a stronger claim where they were actually shut out, not just absent. Save your text messages, changed-lock photos and any written refusal of access.

Keep your own ledger too. Taxes, insurance, mortgage payments and major repairs each owner has paid are what gets argued about when the proceeds are split, whether in a settlement or in front of the court. A co-owner who has carried the taxes for six years will expect credit, and on the right facts should get it.

⚖️ What an Ohio Partition Costs, and Who Pays It

Ohio has an unusual fee rule. Section 5307.25 directs the court to tax the costs and expenses, including reasonable counsel fees, according to equity, and those fees go to the plaintiff's counsel unless the court awards part to other counsel for work that benefited everyone. Taxed fees usually come out of the proceeds. That's different from the states where each side simply pays its own lawyer, and it means the owner who files isn't necessarily carrying the whole bill. But a defendant who hires a lawyer to fight may pay that lawyer personally.

  • Attorney fees: $3,000–$8,000 if nobody fights, and $10,000–$25,000 or more if the value, the credits or an election is contested.
  • Court and title: a few hundred dollars to file, plus a title report that most lawyers will want early, plus commissioner and sheriff costs.
  • Timeline: roughly 6–12 months when uncontested and 12–24 months when contested, longer if an auction fails. Section 5307.15 lets the court order a revaluation or fix a new minimum and try again.
  • Conveyance fee: Ohio's mandatory fee is $1 per $1,000 under Section 319.54, and counties can add a permissive fee on top, so ask the county auditor.

The national picture is in our guide on how partition lawsuits work. One note for families: Ohio isn't on the lists we checked of states that have adopted the Uniform Partition of Heirs Property Act, so the extra buyout and notice steps that apply in Georgia or Illinois are not the rule here. Confirm the current law with your attorney.

🔀 Four Ohio Exits, Rated Honestly

List it together

Best price when everyone signs

A sound house, willing owners, and spouses ready to release dower: list it. A financed buyer will beat any cash offer, including ours, by a wide margin. The weakness is that every owner has to stay willing through closing.

Negotiate a private buyout

Usually the smartest first move

Offer your co-owner what the election would cost them, but with better terms for you: a full cash payment by refinance within 90 days, in exchange for a small discount off an agreed appraisal. They avoid fees and you avoid two years of installments. This works when the occupant can actually qualify for a loan.

File for partition

The guaranteed exit, slowly

There's no real defense to partition for an owner of an ordinary co-owned house. You will get out. But you may get out on the commissioner's value and the statutory payment plan, so file knowing that.

Sell your share to a buyer like us

Fast, at a real discount

Works for a tenant-in-common share. We take on the partition, the fees and the wait, so we pay well below your slice of market value. It rarely works for a survivorship share because of the conditional title problem, unless all the owners first convert the deed.

📋 What To Do This Week

  1. Pull the deed from the county recorder and look for survivorship language. That sets which exits are real.
  2. List each owner, their marital status, and any divorce, since each spouse is a likely dower signature.
  3. If you inherited, find out whether an estate was opened in the county probate court and when the owner died.
  4. Build a one-page ledger of who has paid taxes, insurance, mortgage and repairs.
  5. Get a real value: a broker's opinion or an appraisal, because a commissioner will have a number soon if you end up in court.
  6. Decide in writing: do you want to keep the house, or get paid? Then talk to an Ohio attorney about an offer before filing.

✅ Bottom Line

In Ohio, the question isn't only whether you can force a sale. You can. The question is who will hold the election when the commissioner's number comes back, and what terms you'll accept. Check the deed for survivorship, count the spouses, and price the election first. If everyone's willing, list it. If they aren't, a buyout offer built around those two-year terms is often the shortest way out.

❓ Frequently Asked Questions

Can my co-owner buy me out in an Ohio partition without my agreement?

Yes. Under Section 5307.09, once the commissioner returns a valuation and the court approves it, any party can elect to take the house at the appraised value by paying the others their share. You don't have to agree to the election, though you can challenge the valuation.

Do I get all my money at once if my co-owner elects to take the house?

Not by default. Section 5307.10 sets the terms at one-third cash, one-third in one year and one-third in two years, with interest and secured to the court's satisfaction. Full cash applies only if the court orders it for good cause or all parties agree.

How low can a house sell at an Ohio partition auction?

Section 5307.12 says it can't sell for less than two-thirds of the commissioner's value. If it doesn't sell, Section 5307.15 lets the court order a revaluation or fix a new minimum and try again.

Can I sell my share of an Ohio survivorship deed to someone else?

You can, but the buyer gets title conditioned on your surviving the other owners under Section 5302.20(C)(2). If you die first, the buyer's interest ends. That's why few buyers will pay anything meaningful for it.

Does a partition end survivorship in Ohio?

Effectively, yes. Section 5307.041 tells the court to determine survivorship tenants' shares as if they were tenants in common, so each owner gets a share of the value.

My co-owner's spouse isn't on the deed. Do they still need to sign in Ohio?

Almost always. Ohio keeps dower under Section 2103.02, a spouse's life estate in one-third of the other spouse's real property. The spouse signs the deed to release it, and title companies will require that.

I inherited a share of an Ohio house. How soon can I file for partition?

If you took by inheritance or will from an Ohio resident, Section 5307.03 bars a partition order within one year of death unless you show the estate's claims are paid, secured or covered by personal property.

Who pays the lawyers in an Ohio partition?

Section 5307.25 has the court tax costs and reasonable counsel fees according to equity, generally paid to the plaintiff's counsel unless other counsel's work benefited everyone. These usually come out of the proceeds. A lawyer you hire to contest the case may be your own expense.

Does a co-owner living in an Ohio house owe me rent?

If they're collecting rent from someone else, Section 5307.21 lets you recover your share. If they're only living there, it's harder and often turns on whether you were actually shut out. Ask an Ohio attorney about your facts.

Should we list our Ohio house or sell it for cash?

List it if it's in good condition and all owners and their spouses will sign. You'll net more. A cash sale fits a worn house, a co-owner who won't cooperate, or someone who needs to be done quickly.
