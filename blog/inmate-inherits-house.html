Inheriting a House While Incarcerated: Who Gets a Say, and Who Gets Paid First

When the heir is the one in jail or prison, the hard part is usually not the signature. It is the money. Restitution orders, child support liens and, in some states, the prison's own bill for room and board can all reach an inheritance before the heir sees a dollar. Here is how that works across the country, when the family can sell the house without the heir signing anything, and when they can't.

By Dustin Ray, Founder of Pages of Purpose LLC. Not legal advice — inheritance, probate and debt-collection laws differ from state to state, and federal rules add another layer when the heir is in federal custody. Before anyone signs, disclaims or distributes anything, talk to an attorney licensed in the state where the property is.

Mom dies. The will (or the state's intestacy law) splits the house among her three children. One of them is serving a sentence. The family's first question is almost always "do we need him to sign?" The more important question, and the one this guide starts with, is "who else has a claim on his share?" because that answer decides how the sale should be structured, who should hold the money, and whether turning the inheritance down is even an option.

🧾 The Inheritance Is Real, and So Are the Claims Waiting for It

A prison sentence does not cancel the right to inherit. In almost every situation the incarcerated heir takes the same share anyone else would. But an inheritance is exactly the kind of money that creditors with court orders are watching for, and several of them have rules written specifically for it.

  • ⚖️ Federal restitution and fines. The federal restitution statute says that if a person who owes restitution or a fine "receives substantial resources from any source, including inheritance," while incarcerated, they "shall be required to apply the value of such resources" to what they still owe (18 U.S.C. § 3664(n)). Separately, a federal restitution judgment is a lien on all of the defendant's property and rights to property, treated like a federal tax lien, and it generally runs for 20 years after release from prison (18 U.S.C. § 3613).
  • 👶 Child support. Federal law requires every state to have liens that arise automatically against the property of a parent who is behind on support. Child support agencies run probate and insurance matches, and an heir's share of a house sale is a frequent target.
  • 🏛️ State restitution, court costs and fees. State criminal judgments can become civil judgments that attach to real estate in the county where they are recorded. Once the inmate's name goes on a deed, those judgment liens can attach to that share.
  • 🧱 Cost-of-incarceration laws. Some states bill prisoners for their own confinement. Michigan's State Correctional Facility Reimbursement Act is a well-known example: prisoners must disclose their assets on a sworn form, the Attorney General can sue in the circuit court of the sentencing county, and up to 90% of a prisoner's assets can be taken toward the cost of care (MCL 800.401–800.410). The act defines assets to include real and personal property and exempts a homestead up to $50,000. We cover how that plays out for a Michigan house in our Michigan jail guide.

Why this matters to the family: none of these creditors cares whether the house is sold before or after the estate closes. They care where the inmate's share of the money goes. A sale that runs through the estate, with the administrator holding and paying out the inmate's share on paper, is the cleanest way to deal with them. A sale where cash ends up in a sibling's bank account "for safekeeping" is the version that leads to subpoenas.

⚠️ The warning most families need: do not quietly route the incarcerated heir's share to a relative to keep it away from restitution, child support or a reimbursement claim. Creditors can unwind transfers made to dodge them, and in many states an executor or administrator who pays out a share while ignoring a known claim can be personally on the hook for it. Find the claims first, then distribute.

⚔️ Can Someone in Prison Inherit at All? The One Big Exception

Yes. Being convicted of a crime, even a serious one, does not by itself take away the right to inherit. The old idea that a felon forfeited property ("civil death") is long gone in American law.

The exception is the slayer rule. Every state has some version of a rule that a person who intentionally and unlawfully kills someone cannot inherit from that person. The Uniform Probate Code's version, section 2-803, is typical: a person who "feloniously and intentionally" kills the decedent loses any share of the estate, and the estate passes as if that person had disclaimed it. A final criminal conviction settles the question. Without a conviction, a probate court can decide it on its own using a civil "more likely than not" standard, as Massachusetts does in its adoption of the rule (M.G.L. c. 190B, § 2-803).

The details vary: some states also bar people who caused a death through certain other felonies, and some treat elder abuse the same way. But the slayer rule is narrow. It is about the decedent's own death. An heir who is serving time for an unrelated offense inherits normally, and the family should plan on that.

🖊️ When the Estate Can Sell Without the Heir Ever Signing

This is the good news most families do not expect. If a probate estate is open and someone has been appointed executor or administrator (the generic term is "personal representative"), that person usually controls the house. The heirs, including the one in prison, generally do not have to sign the deed.

The authority comes from two places:

  • 📜 A power of sale in the will. Many wills expressly let the executor sell real estate. When they do, the executor signs the listing agreement, the contract and the deed.
  • 🏛️ State probate law. In states that follow the Uniform Probate Code, a personal representative "acting reasonably for the benefit of the interested persons" has a list of default powers that includes selling estate property, unless the will or a court order restricts it (UPC § 3-715). Other states require a court order or court confirmation for each real estate sale.

Two examples show how much the state matters. In Texas, a will that names an independent executor lets that executor sell without going back to the court for each step (Texas Estates Code chapter 401 covers independent administration). In California, an administrator granted full authority under the Independent Administration of Estates Act can sell a house by mailing a Notice of Proposed Action to the heirs instead of holding a court confirmation hearing. An heir, including one in prison, can object to that notice, which forces the sale into court, but cannot simply veto it.

What the incarcerated heir is still entitled to: notice of the probate, notice of the proposed sale where state law requires it, an accounting, and their share of the net proceeds. If they think the price is too low, their remedy is to object or sue the administrator, not to refuse a signature. That gives the inmate real protection without giving them a stranglehold on everyone else.

📝 When the Heir's Signature Is Needed, and How to Get It From Inside

The inmate's signature becomes necessary in three common situations:

  1. No probate was ever opened. In many states, heirs can try to sell using an affidavit of heirship or similar paperwork instead of a full estate. Title companies that accept that route almost always want every heir to sign the deed. One missing signature, from a prison cell or anywhere else, stops the closing.
  2. The estate already distributed the house. Once the administrator deeds the property to the heirs, they own it together, usually as tenants in common. From that point the incarcerated heir is a co-owner, and a sale of the whole house needs their signature like anyone else's.
  3. The will or the court limits the administrator's power. Some wills say the house may not be sold without the beneficiaries' consent, and some courts require written consents from all heirs before approving a sale.

When a signature is needed, there are two practical ways to get it.

A power of attorney signed in the facility. The heir names a trusted person as agent with authority over real estate. For use at a closing, the power of attorney must be notarized, and title companies usually want it recorded in the county where the house sits. Ask the title company to approve the form before it goes into the facility, because a rejected POA can cost weeks of mail turnaround. Some title companies want the POA to mention the specific property address.

A notary signing at the facility. State prisons and county jails each set their own rules: some have staff notaries for inmate legal papers, some require an outside mobile notary to be approved in advance as a visitor, and almost all require documents to be mailed or cleared before the visit. In federal facilities, a federal statute lets wardens, associate wardens and certain record clerks take acknowledgments from inmates at no charge (18 U.S.C. § 4004). The hardest part is often identification, since the inmate's driver's license is usually in a property room or expired. Our guide to getting a notary into a jail or prison walks through how notaries handle that.

Plan on two to six weeks for either route, longer for a facility that is locked down or several states away.

🚫 Turning Down the Inheritance: When a Disclaimer Works and When It Backfires

Some incarcerated heirs want to give up their share so it goes to their children or siblings. The legal tool is a disclaimer (older statutes call it a renunciation). A valid disclaimer treats the heir as if they died before the decedent, so the share passes to whoever is next in line under the will or intestacy law. The heir does not get to choose the recipient.

Most states base their disclaimer laws on the Uniform Disclaimer of Property Interests Act. Typical requirements: the disclaimer must be in writing, signed, delivered to the personal representative, and made before the heir accepts the property or any benefit from it. Separately, for the disclaimer to be ignored for federal gift and estate tax purposes, it must be a "qualified disclaimer" under 26 U.S.C. § 2518: written, irrevocable, delivered within nine months after the death (or after the heir turns 21), with no benefits accepted and no direction over where the property goes. State law may allow later disclaimers that simply do not get that federal tax treatment.

The trap is using a disclaimer to keep the money away from creditors. Results vary by creditor and by state:

  • 🏦 Federal liens. In Drye v. United States (1999), the Supreme Court held that disclaiming an inheritance did not defeat a federal tax lien that had already attached to the heir's rights. Because a federal restitution lien is enforced like a tax lien, families should expect a disclaimer to fail against it as well.
  • 👶 Child support. Some states bar the disclaimer outright. Texas, for example, says a disclaimer by a child support obligor is barred as to property that could be applied to support that has been administratively determined or reduced to judgment, and the obligee can enforce against that property by lien (Texas Property Code § 240.151(g)–(h)).
  • 🩺 Medicaid and benefits. Medicaid generally does not cover care while someone is incarcerated, but if the heir expects to apply for long-term care later, or the heir's spouse is on Medicaid, disclaiming can be treated as a transfer of assets for less than fair value, which can trigger a penalty period.
  • ⚖️ Bankruptcy and ordinary creditors. State law and bankruptcy courts split on whether a disclaimer is a fraudulent transfer. This one depends entirely on the state and the timing.

A disclaimer is a sound choice when the heir has no outstanding judgments and genuinely wants the share to go to the next people in line. It is a poor choice as an escape hatch. If the heir owes restitution or support, a lawyer should look at it before anything is signed.

🏦 Where the Inmate's Share of the Money Actually Lands

Suppose the house sells and the incarcerated heir's share is $55,000. Families are often surprised by what happens next.

Inmate trust accounts are not designed for this. Every prison system runs trust accounts for commissary, phone time and fees, and money deposited there is visible to the institution. In the federal system, the Bureau of Prisons' Inmate Financial Responsibility Program draws payments toward court-ordered debts from those accounts, and the Bureau proposed rule changes in 2024 to take a larger share of outside deposits. Many state systems take a percentage of deposits for restitution or fees. A large deposit can also trigger questions under a state reimbursement law like Michigan's.

Better places for the money: the administrator can hold the share in the estate account until the heir decides where it goes; the heir can direct payment to a bank account in their own name, managed by their agent under a power of attorney; or, where the heir wants to provide for someone else, a lawyer can set up a properly documented gift or trust after known debts are dealt with. The test is that every dollar is traceable and in the heir's name or paid out by their written direction.

Paying off what is owed may be the smart move anyway. Restitution, fines and support arrears do not disappear on release. A federal restitution lien can follow the person for 20 years after release. Paying it from an inheritance, with a clear record, removes a lien that would otherwise block a mortgage, a car loan or a job requiring a credit check later. Some heirs choose that on purpose.

🧩 Paths for the Family, Judged Honestly

🏛️ Open probate and let the administrator sell

Best when there is a will with a power of sale, or the state gives administrators broad authority. The inmate gets notice and a share, the creditors get paid in order, and nobody needs a visit to the facility. Costs a few thousand dollars and a few months.

Verdict: the default choice for most families.

🤝 Distribute the house, then sell with the inmate's POA

Works when the heir is cooperative, the facility is reachable, and nobody expects new liens. The risk is that the inmate's share becomes a co-owned interest that judgment creditors can attach before the closing, and every signature step depends on prison mail.

Verdict: fine with a cooperative heir and a clean judgment search.

⚖️ The heir won't cooperate or can't be reached

If the estate is open, the administrator may not need the heir at all. If the house has already been deeded to the heirs, any co-owner can ask a court to divide or sell it through a partition lawsuit. Courts appoint representatives for parties who cannot appear, and in states that adopted the Uniform Partition of Heirs Property Act, family co-owners get the first chance to buy out the share at appraised value before any open-market sale.

Verdict: slow and costly, but it ends the standoff.

💼 Another heir sells just their own share

After distribution, a co-heir who is tired of waiting can sell their own percentage to a buyer who takes on the problem. Expect a deep discount for a share of a house with an incarcerated co-owner. Our co-owner guide explains how share sales are priced.

Verdict: an exit for one heir, not a solution for the family.

💵 What Holding the House Costs, and What a Sale Actually Nets

While the family works through probate and prison paperwork, the house costs money every month. A national ballpark for a $240,000 house sitting empty:

  • 🏠 Property tax: the national average effective rate is roughly 0.9% of value, about $180 a month on this house, but it ranges from well under 0.5% in some states to above 2% in others. Check the county bill.
  • 🛡️ Vacant-home insurance: standard policies often stop covering a home that sits vacant beyond 30 to 60 days; vacant-dwelling coverage commonly runs $100 to $250 a month.
  • 💡 Utilities and upkeep: $150 to $350 a month for power, water, lawn care and winter protection.
  • ⚖️ Attorney time: a simple probate commonly runs $3,000 to $8,000 in total; add more if a court has to approve the sale or rule on a dispute.

That is roughly $450 to $800 a month before any legal bills. Over a nine-month probate, $4,000 to $7,000 comes out of everyone's share, including the inmate's.

A worked example. Three siblings inherit equally. The house would be worth $240,000 fixed up and needs about $30,000 of work. One sibling is in state prison and owes $18,000 in state restitution that has become a judgment.

  • 📈 Fix and list: $240,000, minus about 6% commission ($14,400), 2% closing costs ($4,800), $30,000 in repairs and four extra months of carrying costs (about $2,800) leaves around $188,000, or about $62,700 each.
  • ⚡ Cash sale as-is: cash buyers typically pay 60% to 80% of the repaired value depending on condition and complexity. At 70%, that is $168,000, minus about 1% in closing costs, roughly $166,300, or about $55,400 each.
  • 🧾 The incarcerated sibling's share in either case first pays the $18,000 judgment through the closing or the estate. That cost is the same no matter how the house is sold.

The honest conclusion: when the house is sound, the administrator has clear power to sell, and the family can handle repairs, listing it with an agent nets more, often by $5,000 or more per heir. A cash sale makes sense when the house needs heavy work, when the heirs cannot fund repairs or carrying costs, when the inmate's co-ownership is making retail buyers and lenders nervous, or when a partition fight is the alternative. 📅 What To Do This Week

  1. Find the will and read it for a power of sale. Look for language letting the executor sell real estate without the beneficiaries' consent.
  2. Confirm the heir's custody. Use the state department of corrections inmate locator or the federal Bureau of Prisons locator to get the facility, inmate number and mailing rules.
  3. Ask the heir what they owe. Restitution, fines, court costs and child support. Get case numbers. Pull a judgment search in the counties where the heir has lived.
  4. Talk to a probate attorney in the state where the house is about opening an estate and naming an administrator who is not incarcerated.
  5. Send the heir a plain-language letter explaining the plan, enclosing any probate notice, and asking whether they want to discuss a disclaimer or a power of attorney with a lawyer before signing anything.
  6. Secure the house. Vacant-home insurance, locks, utilities on at minimum levels, mail forwarded.
  7. Price both routes. Get an agent's opinion of the as-is and repaired value, and a written cash offer, and compare the net to each heir.

📌 Bottom Line

An heir in prison inherits like anyone else, unless the slayer rule applies. The family usually does not need that heir's signature if a probate estate is open and the administrator has a power of sale. What the family does need is a clear picture of the heir's debts, because restitution, child support and some state reimbursement laws are written to reach exactly this kind of money, and a disclaimer will not always stop them. Run the sale through the estate, pay the claims in order, and get the inmate's share into an account in their own name with a paper trail.

If the house is in good shape and the administrator can sell it, list it with an agent. If it needs work, if the heirs cannot carry it, or if the inmate's share is tangled in liens or a refusal to cooperate, ask us for a written number and compare.

Frequently Asked Questions

Can a person in prison inherit a house?

Yes. A criminal conviction does not take away the right to inherit, and an incarcerated heir generally takes the same share under a will or the intestacy laws as anyone else. The main exception is the slayer rule, which stops a person who intentionally and unlawfully killed the decedent from inheriting from that person.

Does the slayer rule apply if the heir is in prison for something unrelated?

No. Slayer statutes are about the death of the person leaving the inheritance. An heir serving time for an unrelated offense inherits normally. A final conviction for the decedent's killing settles the question, and in many states a probate court can also decide it without a conviction using a civil standard of proof.

Can the executor sell an inherited house without the incarcerated heir signing?

Usually, yes, if a probate estate is open and the will or state law gives the executor or administrator a power of sale. The heir is still entitled to notice, an accounting and their share of the proceeds, and in some states can object to the sale in court, but the administrator signs the deed.

When does an incarcerated heir have to sign the deed?

Typically when no probate was opened and the family is relying on an affidavit of heirship, when the estate has already deeded the house to the heirs as co-owners, or when the will or a court requires every beneficiary's consent. In those cases the heir signs through a notarized power of attorney or in front of a notary at the facility.

Does federal restitution take an inheritance received in prison?

It can. Federal law says a person who owes restitution or a fine and receives substantial resources from any source, including an inheritance, during incarceration must apply them to what is still owed. A federal restitution judgment is also a lien on the person's property, enforced like a tax lien, that generally lasts 20 years after release.

Can an incarcerated heir refuse the inheritance to protect it from creditors?

Not reliably. A disclaimer makes the share pass as if the heir had died first, but the Supreme Court held in Drye v. United States that a disclaimer does not defeat a federal tax lien, and some states, including Texas, bar disclaimers by parents who owe determined child support. A disclaimer meant to dodge creditors can be challenged.

How long does an heir have to disclaim an inheritance?

For federal gift and estate tax purposes, a qualified disclaimer under 26 U.S.C. section 2518 must be in writing and delivered within nine months after the death, or after the heir turns 21, before the heir accepts any benefit. State law sets its own rules for when a disclaimer is valid, and some states allow later disclaimers without the federal tax treatment.

Can the state charge a prisoner for the cost of prison out of an inheritance?

In some states. Michigan's State Correctional Facility Reimbursement Act lets the Attorney General sue to recover the cost of care from a prisoner's assets, including real property, up to 90% of their value, with a homestead exemption up to $50,000. Other states have their own reimbursement laws, so check the rules where the heir is held.

Should the inheritance money be deposited into the inmate's trust account?

Usually not as a lump sum. Prison trust accounts are visible to the institution, and federal and state systems can take part of deposits for restitution, fines or fees. Many families leave the share in the estate account until the heir decides, or pay it to a bank account in the heir's own name managed under a power of attorney.

What if the incarcerated heir refuses to cooperate with the sale?

If the estate is open and the administrator has a power of sale, the heir's cooperation may not be needed. If the house has already been deeded to the heirs, any co-owner can file a partition lawsuit asking a court to divide or sell the property, and the court can order a sale over the heir's objection.
