In Georgia, You Can See the Court's Price Before You Decide Whether to Leave

Three appraisers set a number. You get 15 days to stay in the case or pull out. The other owners get until day 90 to pay you in cash, or the house goes to a first-Tuesday public sale. Few states give a co-owner a look at the price before the commitment. Here's how to use it.

If you've read about partition lawsuits elsewhere, you were probably told it's a one-way door: you file, and the house gets sold. Georgia built something more careful for a house that can't be physically split. The owner who files gets to learn what the house will bring before deciding to go through with it, and the other owners get a defined chance to pay that price in cash. If they do, they keep the house and you've got your money. If they don't, the court orders a public sale.

That sequence changes how to approach a co-owner who won't budge. In Georgia, the lawsuit can work as a pricing tool, a buyout deadline and a last resort, all in a single procedure. It costs real money to run, so the job before filing is to figure out whether you'd accept the likely number and whether your co-owner could pay it.

⏱️ The Georgia Clock: Day 15 and Day 90

Georgia's statutory partition starts in the superior court of the county where the land sits, under O.C.G.A. § 44-6-160. Any common owner can file, whether they got their interest by inheritance, purchase or otherwise. When someone convinces the court that a house can't be fairly divided by metes and bounds, Section 44-6-166.1 sets a timetable that's unusually specific:

  1. Three appraisers. The court appoints three qualified people, and the average of their appraisals is the price. Notice of the price goes out within five days after it's set.
  2. The 15-day window. Within 15 days after the price is established, any petitioner can ask to withdraw and become a party in interest, and any party in interest can ask to become a petitioner. So you can change sides.
  3. Day 16 to day 90. The parties in interest must tender enough money to the court to pay the remaining petitioners their shares of the appraised price, or the property becomes subject to public sale.
  4. Day 95. If the money came in, the petitioners deed their interests over and get paid from the sums tendered.
  5. Public sale. If it didn't, Section 44-6-167 has the court appoint three commissioners. The sale happens on the first Tuesday of a month at the place of public sales, after being advertised once a week for four weeks, and the court reviews it.
⚠️ The withdrawal can cost you. If no petitioner is left after day 15, the case is dismissed and the withdrawn petitioners pay the costs, including the appraisals. The window is a real exit, but it isn't free, and the deadline is short. Know before the appraisal comes back what number you'd accept.

The clock with real numbers

Two cousins own a house in East Point 50/50. The appraisers come in at $215,000, $225,000 and $235,000, so the price is $225,000. The cousin who filed decides that's fair and stays in. The cousin in the house now has until day 90 to tender $112,500 to the court, in full. Compare that with Ohio, where the buyer can often pay over two years: in Georgia the statute calls for the money to be tendered to the court inside that window. Most people can't produce six figures in 75 days without a loan already in motion, which is why the deadline tends to force a real decision.

Don't assume this path is automatic. The statute comes into play when a party in interest convinces the court the property can't be fairly divided. The Georgia Supreme Court said in Stone v. Benton that a petitioner can still seek a public sale under Section 44-6-167 by making that same showing, even if the other owners don't pursue the buyout. Georgia courts also treat these procedures as mandatory, so an attorney who knows the statute is worth having from the first filing.

🧭 Which of Georgia's Three Tracks Are You On?

That clock is for the ordinary statutory case. Before anything else, find out whether the ordinary case is yours.

TrackWhen it appliesWho gets to buy, and how longHow it ends
Statutory partition, §§ 44-6-160 to 44-6-174Most co-owned houses bought by unrelated ownersParties in interest, day 16 to day 90 after the average of three appraisalsFirst-Tuesday public sale if unpaid
Heirs property, §§ 44-6-180 to 44-6-189.1Tenancy in common, no binding agreement on partition, title from a relative, and at least 20% relatives by one of the statute's testsAny owner who didn't ask for a sale, electing within 45 days of the court's noticeOpen-market sale by a broker unless another method would do better
Equitable partitionRare: the statutory remedy must be inadequate or special facts must make equity fit betterWhatever the court fashionsCourt's order

The heirs property track is the one families stumble into. Georgia adopted the Uniform Partition of Heirs Property Act in 2012, early among the states. If you and your siblings inherited, the court has to determine whether it's heirs property before going further. The Court of Appeals reversed a trial court that skipped that step. Under this track the buyout is at the court-determined value times the seller's fraction, partition by sale generally means an open-market listing rather than an auction, and the factors include how long the family has held it and any sentimental attachment. It's slower, and much better on price. Our guide to heirs who can't agree explains the family side.

Equity is a narrow door. In Pack v. Mahan, the Georgia Supreme Court threw out an equitable partition sale because the co-owner hadn't shown that the statutory remedy fell short. So don't count on a judge to improvise for you.

🛋️ Living There Rent-Free Is Legal in Georgia, Up to a Point

This is the question behind most of our Georgia calls. Section 44-6-121 answers it more directly than most states do. Every tenant in common has the right to possess the property, and as long as one occupies no more than their own share would be on partition and doesn't strip its essential value, they aren't liable to account for rent.

The same section lists when they do have to account: if they receive rent or other profit, commit waste, deprive you of your fair proportion, appropriate the property to their exclusive use, or use it in a way that's necessarily exclusive. A sibling who lives there quietly while you have a key is a weak rent case. A sibling who changed the locks and told you to stay out is a much stronger one. Put your request for access in writing and keep the reply.

Shares matter less than people think here. Section 44-6-120 presumes equal shares unless the contrary appears, and even an owner with a bigger slice gets no better right to possess the place while the co-tenancy lasts. A 90% owner can't lawfully lock out a 10% owner. Money is another matter: in an equitable case the Georgia Supreme Court said in Baker v. Baker that the accounts get adjusted after sale, taking in improvements, taxes, other expenses and rental income. Agree in writing what counts, because in Maree v. Phillips the word "contributions" in a private agreement didn't reach upkeep and repairs.

✍️ What Georgia Doesn't Require, and What It Does

Two points here surprise people moving from other states. First, Georgia isn't a community property state and has no dower, so a co-owner's spouse who isn't on the deed generally doesn't have to sign. The title attorney will still ask about any pending divorce. Compare that with Ohio or Texas, where the spouse can be the signature that holds everything up.

Second, survivorship is about words and it's easy to end. Section 44-6-190 recognizes survivorship when the deed says "joint tenants," "joint tenants and not as tenants in common," "joint tenants with survivorship," or "jointly with survivorship," or something essentially the same. It can be severed by recording an instrument that transfers that owner's interest during life. So a joint tenant who wants out can end survivorship without permission. Any deed you record should come from an attorney.

Two more Georgia quirks affect a sale. A mortgage here is usually a security deed, and under Section 44-14-60 that conveys legal title to the lender until it's paid. If one co-owner borrowed against their share, that's a lien you'll deal with at closing. And if you're behind on the loan, Georgia foreclosure is non-judicial, with sales on the same first-Tuesday schedule as partition sales. You can run out of time quickly; see our guide to stopping a foreclosure by selling. Also, real estate closings in Georgia are conducted by attorneys, so there will be a closing attorney on any deal.

💰 Georgia Costs: Who Pays for the Case

  • Your lawyer: plan on $3,500–$8,000 for a straightforward case, and $10,000–$30,000 if it's fought, especially on the heirs property track. An old Georgia Supreme Court case, Neal v. Neal, refused to pay the petitioners' lawyers out of the sale fund, so don't assume your fees come off the top.
  • Appraisers: three of them, with their cost split in proportion to ownership if the house goes to public sale.
  • Sale costs: Section 44-6-166.1 splits these by shares when a buyout closes.
  • Timeline: roughly 6–12 months uncontested, and 12–20 months if there's a heirs-property fight or an appeal. A tender order is itself appealable, as the court held in Lassiter Properties v. Gresham.
  • Transfer tax: Georgia's is modest at about $1 per $1,000, usually paid by the seller.

📋 What To Do This Week

  1. Pull the vesting deed from the clerk of superior court's records and note the exact wording.
  2. Work out how each owner got their share. Any title from a relative points toward the heirs property track.
  3. Ask for access in writing if you've been kept out, and save the answer.
  4. Get your own opinion of value, so the appraisers' average won't surprise you.
  5. Check with a lender on whether the occupant could realistically refinance. That tells you if a 90-day tender is likely.
  6. Choose your walk-away number and write it down before you spend a dollar on a filing.

✅ Bottom Line

Georgia gives a co-owner something rare: a court-set price, a short window to reconsider, and a hard cash deadline for the people on the other side. Find out which track you're on, know your number, and remember the withdrawal costs money. If the owners can agree, list it. If they can't, this statute will get you an answer within about a year.

❓ Frequently Asked Questions

What happens after the appraisal in a Georgia partition case?

Under O.C.G.A. § 44-6-166.1, the price is the average of three appraisals. Within 15 days a petitioner can ask to withdraw. From day 16 to day 90 the other owners can tender the petitioners' shares. If they don't, the property becomes subject to public sale.

Can I back out of a Georgia partition after I see the appraised price?

Yes, within 15 days after the price is established, with the court's permission. If no petitioner remains, the case is dismissed and the withdrawn petitioners pay the costs, including the appraisals.

How long does my co-owner have to buy me out in Georgia?

In the statutory track, the money must be tendered to the court no later than 90 days after the appraised price is set. On the heirs property track, an owner who didn't request a sale has 45 days from the court's notice to elect to buy.

Where and when is a Georgia partition public sale held?

Section 44-6-167 says it takes place on the first Tuesday of the month at the county's place of public sales, run by three commissioners the court appoints, after advertising once a week for four weeks.

Is an inherited house heirs property in Georgia?

Often. It's heirs property if it's held as a tenancy in common with no binding partition agreement, a cotenant took title from a relative, and at least 20% of the interests or cotenants meet the relative tests in § 44-6-180. The court must decide this before going further.

Does my co-owner owe me rent for living in the house in Georgia?

Not simply for living there. Section 44-6-121 says a tenant in common who occupies no more than their share owes no rent, but they must account if they collect rent, commit waste, deprive you of your fair share or take exclusive use.

Does a spouse who isn't on the deed have to sign in Georgia?

Generally no. Georgia has no community property and no dower, so the signature usually comes only from the owners on title. A closing attorney may still ask about a pending divorce.

How do I end survivorship on a Georgia deed?

Section 44-6-190 lets a joint tenant sever by recording an instrument that transfers their interest during life. Have a Georgia attorney prepare it.

Who pays attorney fees in a Georgia partition?

Plan on paying your own. Older Georgia Supreme Court authority refused to pay petitioners' attorneys from the sale fund. Appraisal and sale costs are split in proportion to ownership under the statute.

Should co-owners in Georgia list the house or sell it for cash?

List if the house is in fair shape and everyone will sign, because you'll net more. Cash fits a house that needs serious work, an owner who won't cooperate, or a deadline such as a pending foreclosure.
