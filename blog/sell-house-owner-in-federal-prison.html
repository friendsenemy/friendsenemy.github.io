Selling a House From Federal Prison: The Staff Notary May Not Be Enough, and the Government May Get Paid First

Federal law lets Bureau of Prisons staff notarize for inmates free of charge. A BOP handbook warns, in its own words, that some states won't take that notarization on real estate. Add a federal lien that works like a tax lien and a prison that may be hundreds of miles from the house, and a federal sale runs differently from a state prison sale.

By Dustin Ray, Founder of Pages of Purpose LLC. Not legal advice — laws differ by state, federal facilities set their own procedures, and restitution questions belong with a lawyer. Before you sign or file anything, talk to an attorney licensed where the property is.

Most guides on this subject treat a federal prison like a bigger county jail. It isn't. Two things differ, and they decide whether a sale closes smoothly: who may notarize the owner's signature, and who has a claim on the money before the family sees any of it.

🏛️ The Notary Question Has a Federal Answer, and a State Catch

Federal law gives prison staff a notary-like power. Under 18 U.S.C. section 4004, wardens, associate wardens, chief clerks and record clerks of federal institutions may administer oaths and take acknowledgments from inmates, and they can't charge for it. BOP handbooks commonly state that this authority lets a case manager notarize documents. Start there, because it's free and it doesn't require getting an outsider through the front gate.

Here's the catch. The inmate handbook for the BOP's Miami institution says some states may reject federal notarization for certain transactions, and it names real estate transactions as an example. In that case, it says, a state-commissioned notary has to be arranged. Whether your county recorder and title company will accept a section 4004 acknowledgment is a local question, and neither the prison nor a search engine can settle it.

⚠️ Ask the title company before anyone signs. Send them the exact wording the case manager will use and ask, in writing, whether they'll insure on it. A POA that's rejected at closing has to go back into the prison and may lose you a month. Ten minutes on the phone first is cheap.

Don't confuse this with the sworn-statement shortcut. Handbooks also note that many papers don't need a notary if the signer declares them true and correct. That works for court filings. It doesn't work for a deed, because recorders need an acknowledgment to record it.

📮 The Mail Rule That Slows Everyone Down

Under 28 C.F.R. section 540.19, legal mail gets special handling only if it comes from an attorney, shows that the sender is an attorney, and is marked "Special Mail — Open only in the presence of the inmate." Staff open everything else as general correspondence and may read it. Your title company isn't an attorney, and neither is a buyer. So a closing package sent the normal way is opened, inspected and sometimes returned if it looks like something the mailroom doesn't accept.

  • Use a lawyer as the channel when you can. A real estate attorney can send the POA as Special Mail, and an unmonitored legal call can be arranged through the case manager or counselor.
  • Tell the case manager the package is coming. Name the document and the date.
  • Don't send anything unusual. No binder clips, no staples if the facility forbids them, no prepaid envelopes unless the facility allows them.
  • Keep sensitive details off the phone and email. The regular phone and messaging systems are monitored, and the handbooks say phones can't be used to conduct a business.

🏢 "Inmates Can't Conduct Business," So Is This Allowed?

Families sometimes worry that selling a house breaks a prison rule, because BOP regulations do say that sentenced inmates may not run a business. Read the text. Under 28 C.F.R. section 540.14, an inmate may not direct a business while confined, but the same rule allows correspondence needed to protect property the inmate legitimately owned when committed, and it gives refinancing an existing mortgage as an example. Selling your own home fits that much better than it fits running a company.

Where the line gets real is rental portfolios, flips and contractors. Under section 540.45, a sentenced inmate is expected to hand that authority to someone else, and the Warden may allow a special visit for a major decision. So if the owner holds several houses or an LLC, the POA should be in place before anyone asks staff for favors. Pretrial detainees get more room here, so someone held at an MDC or FDC before sentencing has an easier time than someone who's already designated.

🗺️ The Prison Is in One State and the House Is in Another

BOP places people wherever it decides, sometimes hundreds of miles from home, and it can move them with little warning. Three consequences follow.

  • The house's state controls the paperwork. A Maryland deed needs to meet Maryland's rules even if it's signed at a prison in West Virginia. Some states require witnesses, and our Pennsylvania guide and Florida guide show how demanding those can be. Have the POA drafted by a lawyer in the house's state, not the prison's.
  • An outside notary must be commissioned where the prison is. Notaries act under the law of the state where they're standing. So you want a notary local to the prison, not one from home. Most states recognize a notarial act done properly in another state, though your title company should confirm that.
  • Transfers break plans. Check the BOP inmate locator the morning of any appointment. People in transit can be unreachable for weeks, which is one more reason to get the POA signed early. Once it's done, a transfer doesn't matter.

Federal sentences also have no parole for modern offenses. If the release date is years out, "wait until they're home" means years of taxes, insurance and upkeep, with nobody living there.

💰 The Federal Lien: Who Gets Paid Before the Family

This is the part state-prison families don't face in the same form. Under 18 U.S.C. section 3613(c), a federal fine creates a lien on all of the person's property, treated as if it were a tax lien, and section 3613(f) makes those tools available to enforce restitution orders. The lien arises when judgment is entered and runs for 20 years or until the debt is paid or otherwise ends, and the liability itself can run until 20 years after release. A title search will often find a recorded notice of it. Even if nothing's been recorded yet, the debt may be collected from the proceeds.

Three more federal rules matter.

  • Substantial resources. Under 18 U.S.C. section 3664(n), a person who receives substantial resources from any source during incarceration must apply them to restitution still owed. The statute names inheritance, settlements and judgments. Courts are still working out the edges, and prison wages have been held not to count, but proceeds from a house sale are the kind of money a prosecutor will ask about.
  • Change in circumstances. Section 3664(k) requires a defendant to tell the court and the Attorney General about a material change in economic circumstances. Ask counsel whether a sale triggers that.
  • Forfeiture. If the indictment or judgment names the house, stop. Under 21 U.S.C. section 853, the government's interest can relate back to the offense and courts can restrain the property, and the same is true under several other forfeiture statutes. No deed fixes that without the government's release.

In practice, the payoff is usually handled like a tax lien at closing. The title company or attorney contacts the U.S. Attorney's Office, specifically its financial litigation staff, for a payoff figure and a release, then pays it from the proceeds. Build in two to six weeks for that, as it doesn't move at a buyer's pace. Lawyers can sometimes negotiate a partial release when there isn't enough to pay it all, but nobody should promise that. Selling doesn't dodge the debt, and it isn't supposed to. Paying it can even help, because it stops the lien from following the person out of prison.

🏷️ List it through a family agent

Best when there's equity after the lien

With a valid POA and a sound house, a retail sale puts the most money on the table, and every dollar matters when a lien comes off the top. Our main guide explains how an agent under a POA works.

🏚️ Sell as-is for cash

Best when the house is empty or falling behind

When there's no one local to manage repairs, a mortgage is behind, or the house has sat since the arrest, a buyer who'll take it as-is and wait out the lien payoff may beat a listing nobody can tend. If the mortgage is already in default, see how a sale can stop foreclosure.

🔑 Keep it and rent it

Best for a short remaining term and a reliable manager

Rent can cover the carrying costs, but under the section 540.45 rule someone else must run it. Lien or no lien, the owner's agent has to be trustworthy and the rent still counts as income.

💵 A Worked Example: Restitution Comes Off the Top

A house is worth $300,000 fixed up. The owner is 30 months into a nine-year federal sentence. The mortgage is $90,000 and restitution is $85,000. It's been empty for two years. Carrying it runs about $1,520 a month: mortgage $800, tax $250 at a 1% rate, vacant-home insurance $200, utilities $120, upkeep around $150.

  • List it: $285,000 after $12,000 of cleanup and repairs, less about 8% in commission and costs, is $250,200. Subtract the mortgage and the lien and about $75,200 is left, minus three to five months of holding at about $6,000. Call it $69,000.
  • Sell for cash: at about 70% of repaired value ($210,000), with the buyer covering closing costs, about $35,000 is left after the mortgage and lien.

Cash buyers generally land at 60-80% of repaired value, so the honest read of this one is that the listing wins if a trusted person can run it. The cash number wins only if nobody can, or if a foreclosure date would wipe out both. And whatever's left may raise the section 3664(n) question if restitution isn't fully paid. The lawyer needs to see these numbers too.

📋 What To Do This Week

  1. Get the full name, register number and facility from the BOP inmate locator.
  2. Pull the judgment and look for restitution, fines and forfeiture. PACER or the defense lawyer will have it.
  3. Order a title search and look for a federal lien notice.
  4. Ask a title company in the house's county whether they'll accept a section 4004 acknowledgment.
  5. Have a lawyer in that state draft the POA and send it as Special Mail.
  6. Email or write the case manager to ask how notarizations are scheduled.
  7. Call the mortgage servicer and the insurer so neither lapses while the paperwork moves.

✅ Bottom Line

The federal system gives you a free notary and then warns you it may not work for real estate, so confirm with the title company first. Sending papers through a lawyer saves weeks. And before anyone counts on the proceeds, read the judgment: if there's restitution or a fine, the United States has a lien that behaves like a tax lien, and it gets paid at closing. Selling is allowed, and for many families it's still the right call, but the plan has to start from what's left after the government is paid.

❓ Frequently Asked Questions

Can a BOP case manager notarize a deed or power of attorney?

Under 18 U.S.C. section 4004, certain federal prison staff may take acknowledgments from inmates at no charge, and BOP handbooks commonly say case managers have this authority. Whether a county recorder and title insurer will accept it for real estate varies, so ask them before signing.

Do federal inmates have to pay for a notary?

Not for staff acting under section 4004, which says they can't demand or accept a fee. An outside state notary may charge for the act and for travel, subject to that state's fee limits.

Can an outside notary get into a federal prison?

Usually only with advance approval through the unit team or Warden, and procedures vary by institution. Start with the case manager and allow several weeks. Lockdowns and transfers can cancel an appointment with no notice.

Does selling a house violate the rule against conducting a business?

Generally a single sale of a home you already owned isn't running a business. 28 C.F.R. section 540.14 bars directing a business but allows correspondence needed to protect property owned at commitment. Running a rental portfolio or LLC is different, and that authority should go to someone outside.

Does a federal restitution order put a lien on the house?

Yes. Under 18 U.S.C. section 3613, a fine creates a lien treated like a tax lien, and the same enforcement tools apply to restitution. It lasts 20 years or until paid or otherwise ended. It typically gets paid from the proceeds at closing.

Will the government take the money from selling my house?

If restitution or a fine is unpaid, expect a lien payoff at closing. Any remaining money may be reached too, since section 3664(n) requires substantial resources received during incarceration to be applied to what's owed. A lawyer should review this before closing.

How long does a federal lien payoff take?

Allow two to six weeks for a payoff letter and release from the U.S. Attorney's Office. Tell your title company early, because a buyer's closing date doesn't speed it up.

What if the house is named in a forfeiture?

Stop and call the defense lawyer. Under 21 U.S.C. section 853 and similar laws, the government's interest can reach back to the offense and courts can restrain the property. A sale can't go ahead without the government's agreement or a court order.

Which state's rules apply if the prison is in a different state from the house?

The state where the house sits sets the requirements for the deed and POA. The notary must act under the law of the state where the signing happens. Use a lawyer in the property's state and a notary near the prison.

Is listing better than a cash sale for a federal inmate's house?

It often is when there's a reliable agent under a POA and a sound house, because a lien makes every dollar matter. A cash sale makes sense when nobody can manage repairs, the house has sat empty, or a foreclosure date is close.
