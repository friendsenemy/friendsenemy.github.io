Selling a Kentucky House From Jail or Prison: Dower, the Power of Attorney and the Master Commissioner

Kentucky is one of the few states that still gives every spouse a dower or curtesy interest in the other's real estate, so a husband or wife who isn't on the deed usually has to sign the sale. When one of them is in a county jail or a state prison, that changes whose signature has to come out of the facility. Here's how dower, Kentucky's power of attorney law and its court-run foreclosure sales fit together.

A wife calls about her house in Louisville. It's in her name only, bought before she married. Her husband is serving time, and she wants to sell. She assumes she can, because he isn't on the deed. The title company says no, not without his signature. That's dower, and in Kentucky it surprises families more than anything about prison logistics.

This guide starts with that rule because it decides who has to sign. Then it covers the power of attorney, how to get it signed in a Kentucky jail or prison, and what happens if the lender gets to the courthouse first. For the general rules that apply in every state, see our national guide to selling a house while incarcerated.

💍 Dower Follows Every Kentucky Deed, Whoever Is on the Title

Under KRS 392.020, when a married person dies without a will, the surviving spouse takes a fee interest in one-half of the surplus real estate the deceased spouse owned at death, and a life estate in one-third of any real estate the deceased spouse owned during the marriage but no longer owned at death, unless the survivor's right was barred, forfeited or relinquished. Kentucky still calls those rights "dower" (for a wife) and "curtesy" (for a husband).

That second piece is the one that matters for a sale. If a married owner sells a house without the spouse joining in the deed, the spouse who didn't sign can later claim a life estate in one-third of that property if he or she outlives the seller. No title insurer wants to insure a buyer subject to that claim, so Kentucky closings routinely require the non-owner spouse to sign the deed, releasing dower or curtesy.

Three features make Kentucky's version especially important:

  • 🏘️ It isn't limited to the homestead. Dower reaches real estate generally, so a rental house, a lot or an inherited share owned by one spouse is covered too.
  • ⏳ It survives the sale. The claim doesn't disappear when the deed is recorded; it waits until one spouse dies.
  • 🏦 It can survive foreclosure. A spouse who never signed the mortgage generally keeps dower rights that a foreclosure can't wipe out, which is why lenders name both spouses in Kentucky foreclosure suits.

A final divorce ends dower and curtesy. If the couple is mid-divorce, the court handling it may be the cleanest place to get the house sold; see our guide to selling a house during a divorce.

🔁 Two Directions: The Spouse Inside and the Spouse at Home

Dower works both ways, so an incarceration creates one of two signature problems:

  • 🔒 The incarcerated spouse owns the house. The owner signs (personally or through an agent), and the spouse at home signs to release dower. Usually straightforward.
  • 🏠 The spouse at home owns the house alone. The incarcerated spouse, who has no title, still has to release curtesy or dower. His or her signature has to come out of the facility, or an agent has to sign it under a power of attorney that clearly covers releasing those marital rights.

Families are often tempted to have the incarcerated spouse sign a quick quitclaim deed. Sometimes that works, but it should be drafted by a Kentucky attorney or the title company so it actually releases dower in a form the buyer's insurer will accept, and so the spouse inside understands exactly what he or she is giving up.

🖊️ What the Power of Attorney Must Cover in Kentucky

Kentucky adopted the Uniform Power of Attorney Act in 2018, codified in KRS Chapter 457. Under KRS 457.050, the principal signs the document, and a signature acknowledged before a notary is presumed genuine. The Act doesn't strictly require notarization, but for real estate you'll want it, because the document needs to be recorded.

For a house sale from prison, have a Kentucky attorney check that the power of attorney:

  • 🏠 Grants authority over real property and describes the house by address and the deed book and page.
  • 💍 Expressly authorizes the agent to release dower or curtesy in any real estate the other spouse sells. This is the clause that matters when the incarcerated person is the non-owner spouse.
  • ⚡ Takes effect immediately, rather than springing on a doctor's certification of incapacity, which an inmate will never have.
  • 👥 Names a successor agent, in case the first one can't finish the job.

Kentucky's Act also lists powers that need express authorization, such as making gifts. If the agent will be paid from the proceeds, or the house is going to a relative, have the attorney address that directly so the title company isn't left guessing.

🏚️ Many Kentucky State Inmates Are Held in County Jails

Kentucky is unusual in how many people serving state sentences are housed in county jails under contract with the Department of Corrections, rather than in state prisons such as the Kentucky State Penitentiary at Eddyville, the Kentucky State Reformatory at La Grange or the Eastern Kentucky Correctional Complex. For a family, that's usually good news: county jails are often closer to home and simpler to arrange a notary visit in.

Practical points either way:

  • 📞 Call the facility first. Ask whether an outside notary can see the inmate during professional visiting hours, how far ahead to schedule, and whether staff notaries will notarize personal legal documents.
  • 🪪 Ask what identification the inmate will have. The driver's license is usually in property; some facilities will produce the inmate ID card or confirm identity for the notary.
  • 📬 Expect paper to go in and come back out through the mail or property room, not hand to hand, at many jails. Allow a week each way.

🗂️ Recording With the County Clerk, Plus Kentucky's Deed Extras

Kentucky land records are kept by the county clerk. Under KRS 382.370, powers of attorney to convey real property may be acknowledged, proved and recorded in the proper office, and county clerks apply the same filing requirements they use for deeds. A title company will almost always record the power before, or together with, the deed the agent signs.

Kentucky deeds carry a few requirements families don't expect:

  • 🧾 A consideration certificate stating the price, signed by both the seller and the buyer, which the agent can sign for the incarcerated owner.
  • ✍️ A statement of who prepared the deed.
  • 💲 Real estate transfer tax of 50 cents per $500 of value, collected by the clerk when the deed is recorded; on a $160,000 sale that's $160.

While you're at the clerk's office (or its online index), search the owner's name for judgment liens, child-support liens and any lis pendens from a foreclosure. Judgments from the criminal case that have been recorded as liens will be paid from the sale proceeds at closing.

⚖️ Master Commissioner Sales and the Two-Thirds Redemption Rule

Kentucky forecloses through the courts. The lender files suit in circuit court, names both spouses because of dower, and after judgment the court refers the house to its master commissioner for sale. Before the sale the property is appraised, and under the Kentucky Supreme Court's rules for judicial sales the master commissioner sells within a reasonable time, generally not more than 90 days after the order of referral. From the first missed payment to the courthouse sale is commonly nine months to a year or more, which gives a family more time than in many states.

Kentucky's redemption right is tied to that appraisal. Under KRS 426.530, if the house sells for less than two-thirds of its appraised value, the owner and the owner's representatives may redeem it within six months of the sale by paying the purchase price plus 10% annual interest and the buyer's reasonable costs for upkeep, utilities, insurance, association fees and taxes. The buyer gets an immediate writ of possession and a deed with a lien reflecting the redemption right.

⚠️ Don't count on redemption. If the bidding reaches two-thirds of the appraisal, there's no redemption at all. And even when it applies, someone has to come up with the full price plus interest within six months while the owner is inside. Selling before the commissioner's sale nearly always keeps more of the equity.

If a foreclosure suit has been served, the incarcerated owner can still respond and can still sell. A sale can close while the case is pending, with the lender paid at closing. Our guide to stopping foreclosure by selling the house explains payoffs and timing.

🛡️ Kentucky's $5,000 Homestead Exemption

Families sometimes hope the homestead exemption will protect the house from fines, restitution or other judgments while the owner is in prison. In Kentucky it won't do much. KRS 427.060 exempts only $5,000 of a debtor's interest in a permanent residence from sale under execution, and it doesn't apply against a mortgage. A recorded judgment lien will usually be paid from the sale proceeds, so get the payoff figures early and compare offers on what you'll actually net.

🏷️ When Listing Beats a Cash Offer

If the house is in good shape, payments are current, and the spouse at home or a relative can work with an agent, list it. The dower release and the power of attorney are paperwork, not a reason to take a discount. A cash sale makes sense when the house needs serious work, a foreclosure suit is moving, or nobody outside can manage a sale for months.

If the incarcerated person co-owns the house with siblings or an ex rather than a spouse, each co-owner's share can be handled separately; see whether a co-owner can sell without the other, and our partition lawsuit guide if a co-owner refuses to cooperate.

  2. 💍 If there's a spouse, plan for the dower or curtesy release no matter whose name is on the deed.

  4. 🖊️ Have a Kentucky attorney draft an immediately effective power of attorney covering the house and the release of dower or curtesy.

✅ Bottom Line

In Kentucky, the signature that holds up a sale is often the spouse's. Dower and curtesy reach real estate either spouse owns, so a husband or wife who isn't on the deed normally has to release those rights, even from a county jail or a state prison. A recorded power of attorney that expressly covers releasing dower solves it with one notary visit. If a foreclosure suit gets ahead of you, the master commissioner's sale offers redemption only when bids fall below two-thirds of the appraisal. Get the signatures in order, then list if you can, or ask us for a written number if you can't.

❓ Frequently Asked Questions

What is dower in Kentucky and why does it matter when selling a house?

Under KRS 392.020, a surviving spouse can claim a life estate in one-third of real estate the other spouse owned during the marriage but sold without the survivor's release. Title insurers therefore require a married seller's spouse to sign the deed, even if that spouse isn't on the title.

My husband is in prison and the Kentucky house is only in my name. Does he have to sign?

Usually yes. His curtesy right attaches to real estate you own during the marriage, so the title company will want his signature releasing it, either on the deed itself or through an agent under a power of attorney that expressly covers that release.

Can a Kentucky power of attorney be used to release dower or curtesy?

It can, if the document clearly authorizes the agent to release dower or curtesy in real estate. Have a Kentucky attorney include that language, because a general form may not satisfy the title company.

Does a Kentucky power of attorney have to be notarized?

Not for validity. KRS 457.050 requires the principal's signature, and a signature acknowledged before a notary is presumed genuine. For selling real estate, notarize it anyway, because it needs to be recorded with the county clerk.

Where do you record a power of attorney to sell a house in Kentucky?

With the county clerk in the county where the house is. KRS 382.370 provides for recording powers of attorney to convey real property, and clerks apply the same filing requirements used for deeds.

Does a divorce end dower rights in Kentucky?

Yes. A final divorce ends dower and curtesy between the former spouses. If a divorce is underway, the court can also award the house to one spouse or order it sold, which may avoid the need for an incarcerated spouse's signature.

How long does a Kentucky foreclosure take?

Kentucky foreclosures go through circuit court. After judgment the master commissioner generally sells within 90 days of the order of referral, and the whole process commonly takes nine months to a year or more from the first missed payment.

Can I redeem my Kentucky house after a master commissioner sale?

Only if it sold for less than two-thirds of its appraised value. Then KRS 426.530 lets the owner redeem within six months by paying the purchase price plus 10% annual interest and the buyer's reasonable costs.

Does Kentucky's homestead exemption protect a house from restitution or fines?

Very little. KRS 427.060 exempts only $5,000 of a debtor's interest in a permanent residence from sale under execution, and it doesn't apply against a mortgage. Recorded judgment liens are usually paid from sale proceeds.

Do we have to fix up a Louisville house before selling it while the owner is incarcerated?

No. You can list it as-is or sell to a cash buyer. Repairs only make sense if someone has the money and time to manage them. On a sound house with payments current, listing without major repairs usually still nets more than a cash sale.
