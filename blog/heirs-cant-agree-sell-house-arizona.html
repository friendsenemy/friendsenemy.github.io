Heirs Can't Agree on an Arizona House? Arizona's Two-Year Probate Deadline Decides Who Can Still Sell It

Arizona gives families two years from the death to open an ordinary probate. Siblings who spend that time arguing can find that no personal representative can be appointed with the power to sell, and the house simply belongs to all of them, in shares, with nobody in charge. Add Arizona's community property rules, which often leave a stepparent owning half, and the 2024 heirs property act, and the order you do things in matters. A parent died in Arizona and left a house. One sibling wants to sell it, another wants to move in, a third lives out of state and won't return calls, and maybe there's a second spouse who still sleeps there. Everyone is waiting for someone else to blink.

In most states, waiting is merely expensive. In Arizona it can also change the legal tools available to you, because the state puts a firm outer limit on ordinary probate. This guide starts there, then walks through the Arizona rules that most often cause the deadlock in the first place, the ways a court can break it, and how to judge whether listing the house or taking a cash offer leaves the family with more.

⏳ Arizona's Two-Year Limit on Opening Probate

Under A.R.S. § 14-3108, an informal probate or appointment, or a formal testacy or appointment proceeding, generally "shall not be commenced more than two years after the decedent's death." There are exceptions, and the one that matters most to feuding families is the fourth: if no court proceeding about the estate happened within those two years, a probate or appointment can still be opened later, but the personal representative then "has no right to possess estate assets" beyond what is necessary to confirm title in the rightful successors, and creditor claims other than administration expenses can't be presented.

In plain terms, a personal representative appointed late is there to paper the transfer to the heirs, not to take the house, market it and sign a deed over everyone's objections. The two-year limit does not apply to a proceeding to construe a probated will or to determine the heirs of someone who died without one, so the family can still get a court order saying who owns what. But after that order, the house belongs to the heirs together, and a sale needs every one of them or a partition case.

⚠️ The most important warning on this page: if a sale is likely to need someone in charge who can sign over a holdout's objection, get a personal representative appointed before the second anniversary of the death. Once that window passes with nothing filed, the practical choices shrink to unanimous agreement, buying out the holdout, selling individual shares, or a lawsuit to partition.

That's why the first question we ask Arizona families is not "who wants what" but "what's the date of death, and has anything been filed?" The answer tells you which set of tools you still have.

🔑 Inside the Window: A Personal Representative Can Sell Without Every Heir's Signature

Arizona follows the Uniform Probate Code. Once appointed, a personal representative, "acting reasonably for the benefit of the interested persons," may sell or mortgage real property of the estate under A.R.S. § 14-3715, unless the will or an order in a formal proceeding restricts that power. No separate court approval of the sale is needed in an unsupervised case, and the heirs don't each have to sign the deed. That is the single most useful tool for an Arizona family that can't agree, and it only exists if someone is appointed.

The fight therefore tends to move to who gets appointed. A.R.S. § 14-3203 sets the order: the person named in a probated will, then a surviving spouse who is a devisee, other devisees, the surviving spouse, then other heirs. When several children share the same priority as heirs and one of them hasn't renounced or agreed to the nomination, subsection E requires a formal proceeding with notice and a hearing; a clerk-level informal appointment isn't available. If an heir with a substantial interest objects to a sibling or stepparent who lacks priority under a will, the court may appoint someone acceptable to heirs holding more than half of the estate's probable value, or, failing that, any suitable person, which can mean a professional fiduciary.

For a family that is stuck, the formal route is slower and costs more in legal fees, but it produces a decision. In Maricopa County, the Superior Court's probate department hears these petitions; Pima, Pinal, Yavapai and the other counties handle them in their own superior courts.

💍 The Stepparent Problem: How Community Property Splits an Arizona House

A large share of Arizona heir disputes are not between siblings at all. They're between the children of a first marriage and a surviving second spouse, and Arizona's intestacy statute is often the reason.

Under A.R.S. § 14-2102, if a married person dies without a will and all of their children are also children of the surviving spouse, the spouse takes the entire intestate estate. But if one or more of the decedent's children are not the surviving spouse's children, the spouse gets one-half of the decedent's separate property and no interest in the decedent's half of the community property. The spouse still owns their own half of the community, because it was always theirs. The result for a house bought during a second marriage is frequently a 50/50 split: the stepparent owns half, and the decedent's children share the other half.

That is a recipe for a standoff. The stepparent may live in the house and see no reason to leave. The children may see half a house they inherited and no way to use it. Before anyone argues, though, pull the deed. Many Arizona couples take title as "community property with right of survivorship" under A.R.S. § 33-431, and in that case the surviving spouse owns the whole house at the first death, outside probate, and the children's claim to it disappears. A recorded beneficiary deed under A.R.S. § 33-405 can likewise send the house directly to the named beneficiaries. The deed often settles the argument faster than any lawyer.

🛑 Four Ways an Arizona Court Breaks or Slows a Deadlock

📜 Formal appointment

Use when the fight is over who's in charge

A petition with notice to every heir asks the court to pick the personal representative under § 14-3203. It's the cleanest way to get one person with authority to sell. Expect weeks to a few months, longer if someone contests a will.

🧷 Supervised administration or a restraining order

Use when you fear a rushed or self-dealing sale

An heir worried about a below-market sale can ask for supervised administration under A.R.S. § 14-3501, so distributions and closing run under court oversight, or for an order restraining a specific act under § 14-3607. Arizona also lets a court remove a personal representative for cause under § 14-3611.

🪓 Partition before the estate closes

Use when heirs will end up owning shares nobody wants to share

Under A.R.S. § 14-3911, when two or more heirs or devisees will receive undivided interests, the personal representative or an heir can ask the probate court to partition before the estate is closed. Since the 2024 amendment, that can proceed under Arizona's new heirs property act, described below.

🏚️ A civil partition case after distribution

Use when the two-year window has passed or the estate is closed

Co-owners who already hold title can sue to partition under A.R.S. § 12-1211 and the sections that follow, where court-appointed commissioners divide the land or report that it can't be divided fairly and should be sold. It works, but it is the slowest and most expensive way for a family to sell a house.

🧾 Arizona's 2024 Heirs Property Act: A Buyout Before Any Forced Sale

In April 2024 Arizona enacted the Uniform Partition of Heirs Property Act as Title 12, Chapter 27 (A.R.S. §§ 12-3401 through 12-3412), and amended § 14-3911 so a probate partition can run under it. The act reaches property held by tenants in common where someone got title from a relative and relatives hold a large share, which describes most inherited family houses.

When the act applies, the order of events is fixed. The court determines fair market value, normally through a disinterested Arizona-licensed appraiser, with 30 days for objections and a valuation hearing. If any co-owner asked for a sale, the others get 45 days to elect to buy out the sellers' shares at that value, and at least 90 days to pay. If nobody buys, the court must consider dividing the property before it orders a sale, weighing factors like how long the family has held it, sentimental attachment and who paid the taxes. And if there is a sale, it is an open-market listing through an Arizona broker at no less than the determined value, unless the court finds sealed bids or an auction would be better for the owners as a group.

For a sibling who wants to keep the house, this is powerful: file nothing, wait for the seller to ask for partition, then elect to buy at the appraised price. For a sibling who wants out, it means asking a court to sell may simply get you bought out at appraisal, which is often a fair result. The act's text ties it to partitions requested under § 14-3911; whether a separate civil partition filed after the estate closes gets the same treatment is a question to put to your Arizona lawyer before you file.

🌵 What a Standoff Costs on a Phoenix-Area House

Arizona property taxes are low compared with much of the country; on a typical Maricopa County single-family home the bill often runs about 0.5 to 0.7 percent of market value a year. Desert carrying costs make up for it. Take a 1990s stucco house in Mesa that would be worth $450,000 fully updated, with original HVAC, a tired pool and a dated interior:

  • 🧾 Property tax: about $2,400 to $3,000 a year, or $200 to $250 a month.
  • 🏠 Vacant-home insurance: roughly $125 to $225 a month once the standard homeowner's policy lapses.
  • ❄️ Electricity: $150 to $350 a month in summer to keep the house cool enough to protect finishes and keep pests out.
  • 🏊 Pool and yard: $120 to $200 a month, or a green pool and a city or HOA notice.
  • 🏘️ HOA dues: common in newer subdivisions, often $50 to $150 a month, and unpaid assessments can become a lien.

That's about $650 to $1,175 a month, or $8,000 to $14,000 a year, before the HVAC dies in July. A formal appointment fight typically costs a few thousand to $10,000 or more in legal fees per side; a contested partition can run $15,000 to $40,000 or beyond.

Listing route. If the family spends $40,000 on HVAC, pool resurfacing, paint and flooring, lists at $450,000 and sells at $440,000, commission and closing at about 6 to 7 percent take roughly $29,000 and six months of carrying costs take about $5,000 to $7,000. Net: about $364,000 to $366,000, split among the heirs.

Cash route. A cash buyer typically pays about 60 to 80 percent of after-repair value depending on condition and how much work the house needs. On this house that's $270,000 to $360,000, realistically around $310,000 to $335,000, with no repairs, no commission and a closing in two to four weeks once whoever has authority signs. Net: roughly $305,000 to $330,000.

When heirs can cooperate on repairs and a listing, the listing usually wins by $30,000 or more. The cash route wins when nobody will front the repair money, when the family can't agree on a contractor or a price, or when the two-year deadline is close and you need a clean signature soon.

📋 What To Do This Week

  1. 📅 Write down the date of death and count to the two-year mark. If it's within six months, call an Arizona probate lawyer this week.
  2. 📄 Pull the recorded deed from the county recorder (Maricopa, Pima, Pinal, Yavapai and the rest all have online search). Look for "community property with right of survivorship," "joint tenants," or a beneficiary deed.
  3. 📝 Find any will, and check whether anyone has already filed in superior court.
  4. 💡 Keep the power on, the pool running and the insurance in force, and keep receipts showing who paid.
  5. 👥 Ask each heir in writing whether they'll renounce or concur in one person's appointment. A signed nomination avoids a formal fight under § 14-3203.
  6. 💵 Get two numbers: a local agent's as-is and after-repair estimates, and a written cash offer. Share both with every heir.

✅ Bottom Line

Arizona heirs who can't agree are on a clock. Inside two years from the death, a personal representative can be appointed, formally if need be, and can sell the house for the estate without every heir's signature. After two years with nothing filed, that power is mostly gone and the heirs own the house together. Check the deed first, because a survivorship or beneficiary deed may end the dispute, and know that Arizona's 2024 heirs property act gives the relative who wants to keep the house a buyout at appraised value before any forced sale.

❓ Frequently Asked Questions

Is there a deadline to open probate in Arizona?

Generally yes. A.R.S. 14-3108 bars starting an informal or formal probate or appointment more than two years after the death, with exceptions. A late appointment is still possible if nothing was filed in those two years, but that personal representative can only confirm title in the heirs and has no right to take possession of estate assets.

What happens to an Arizona house if nobody opens probate within two years?

The heirs can still get a court determination of who inherits, because the two-year limit doesn't apply to proceedings to determine heirs. But the house then belongs to the heirs together, so a sale needs all of them to sign, a buyout of the holdouts, or a partition case.

Can an Arizona personal representative sell the house if some heirs object?

Usually yes. Under A.R.S. 14-3715 a personal representative acting reasonably for the interested persons may sell estate real property without separate court approval, unless the will or a formal court order restricts that power. Heirs who object can ask the court for supervised administration or a restraining order.

My siblings and I can't agree on who should be personal representative in Arizona. What happens?

When heirs share the same priority and one of them hasn't renounced or agreed to the nominee, A.R.S. 14-3203 requires a formal proceeding. The court can appoint a person acceptable to heirs holding more than half of the estate's value or, if there's no such agreement, any suitable person.

Does my stepmother own half of Dad's Arizona house?

Often, if the house was community property and Dad left no will. Under A.R.S. 14-2102, when the decedent has children who aren't the surviving spouse's children, the spouse takes no part of the decedent's half of the community property but keeps her own half. If the deed says community property with right of survivorship, she owns the whole house.

What does community property with right of survivorship mean for an Arizona heirs dispute?

Under A.R.S. 33-431, the surviving spouse becomes sole owner at the first death without probate, so the decedent's children don't inherit any part of that house from the deceased spouse. Check the recorded deed before arguing about shares.

Has Arizona adopted the Uniform Partition of Heirs Property Act?

Yes. Arizona enacted it in 2024 as A.R.S. 12-3401 through 12-3412 and amended 14-3911 so a partition requested in probate can proceed under it. It provides a court valuation, a 45-day buyout election for co-owners who didn't ask for a sale, and a preference for an open-market listing over an auction.

Can Arizona heirs ask the probate court to partition the house before the estate closes?

Yes. A.R.S. 14-3911 lets the personal representative or one or more heirs or devisees petition for partition before the estate is closed when two or more of them will receive undivided interests. The court can divide the property or direct a sale.

How much does it cost to hold an inherited house empty in the Phoenix area?

Property tax is relatively low, often about 0.5 to 0.7 percent of market value a year, but vacant-home insurance, summer electricity, pool care and HOA dues commonly push monthly costs to roughly $650 to $1,175 before any repairs.

Should Arizona heirs who can't agree take a cash offer or list the house?

List it if the heirs can agree on a personal representative and fund basic repairs; the open market usually nets more. A cash sale tends to win when the house needs major work, a holdout won't cooperate, or the two-year deadline is close and the family needs one clean signature soon.
