Partition Lawsuits in Alaska: The Court Sale Is an Execution-Style Auction, So the Real Work Happens Before It

Alaska's partition statute tells the referee to sell at public auction to the highest bidder, in the same way property is sold on execution: notices posted in three public places, four weekly newspaper ads, 45 days online. There is no heirs-property buyout right and the loser can owe part of the winner's legal fees. That combination makes the filing a deadline more than a destination. You own part of a house, a cabin or a few acres in Alaska with someone who won't sell, won't buy you out, and won't answer the phone in any useful way. Maybe it's a sibling after a parent's estate closed, an ex-partner whose name is still on the deed, or three cousins scattered between Anchorage, Bethel and Arizona. Somebody has mentioned a partition lawsuit, and you want to know what actually happens if you file one here.

The short version is that Alaska's partition law is old, compact and blunt. If the court decides the property can't be divided fairly, the statute sends it to a public auction run the way a creditor's execution sale is run. That is rarely where anyone gets the best price, and it shapes every sensible strategy on this page.

🔨 Alaska's Court Sale Is an Execution-Style Auction

The partition chapter sits in Title 9 of the Alaska Statutes, starting at AS 09.45.260, which lets one or more tenants in common bring an action to divide the property according to their rights, and to sell it if a division "cannot be had without great prejudice" to the owners. When a sale is ordered, AS 09.45.420 is the instruction that matters most: every sale made by the referee happens "at public auction to the highest bidder in the manner required for the sale of real property on execution," and the notice has to state the time, place and terms of sale and any prior lien the buyer takes subject to.

"In the manner required for the sale of real property on execution" points to AS 09.35.140, the execution-sale notice statute. For real property, that means a written notice describing the property (with its street address, if it has one) posted in three public places at least 30 days before the sale, the same notice published once a week for four successive weeks in a newspaper of general circulation nearest the place of sale, and a notice on a qualifying free website starting at least 45 days before the sale date.

Read that as a buyer would. The audience for a posted, newspaper-and-website auction is people who follow foreclosure and execution notices: investors with cash who expect a discount, who usually can't get inside or inspect, and who bid accordingly. A three-bedroom in Muldoon or a dry cabin off the Parks Highway marketed that way does not draw the family that would pay full price after a walkthrough and an appraisal. The court can confirm the sale or set it aside and order a new one under AS 09.45.540, and a grossly inadequate price is a reason to object, but the starting point is still an auction.

⚠️ The warning that matters most: nothing in Alaska's partition chapter gives a co-owner a guaranteed right to buy out the others at an appraised price before the auction, the way states that adopted the Uniform Partition of Heirs Property Act do. If you want a buyout or a private listing instead of the courthouse-style sale, you have to negotiate it, usually by stipulation the judge then approves. Plan for that early.

🧭 What That Means for the Decision You Actually Face

Because the end of the road is an auction, an Alaska partition case is most useful as a clock. The filing tells a stalling co-owner that there is now a date, a judge and a referee, and that the default outcome is a sale nobody controls. People who would not answer a letter often answer a summons. The goal for most families is to use that pressure to get to one of three better exits:

  1. A negotiated buyout. One owner pays the other for the share, based on an agreed appraisal and agreed credits, and the case is dismissed.
  2. A stipulated listing. Both sides agree, on the record, to list with an agent at a set price with a price-reduction schedule, and to split proceeds per a formula. The court can approve that as the method of sale.
  3. A sale of one share. An owner who simply wants out sells an undivided interest to a third party, who then deals with the remaining owner.

Each of these beats an auction for nearly everyone. The case is still worth filing when the other owner will not engage at all, when someone is living in the house rent-free and running down its value, or when a share is held by an owner who has vanished and can only be reached by service by publication, which AS 09.45.280 allows for parties out of state or who can't be found.

📑 Who Can File, and Which Owners Can't Be Partitioned

The statute speaks of tenants in common and of property held subject to a life estate. In Alaska, that covers more people than it would elsewhere, because AS 34.15.130 abolished joint tenancy in real property. Two siblings whose deed says "joint tenants with right of survivorship" are, in Alaska, simply tenants in common with no survivorship, and either one can ask for partition. Our guide to selling a co-owned Alaska house covers that rule in detail.

The main group that cannot use partition against each other is a married couple holding as tenants by the entirety. A husband and wife who acquire real property together hold it that way unless the deed says otherwise, under AS 34.15.110, and one spouse cannot force a sale of entirety property while the marriage lasts. Their route is a divorce, where the court divides the marital estate, not a partition action. Spouses who opted into community property through an Alaska community property agreement or trust are also outside the ordinary partition picture during the marriage.

Two Alaska-specific land types need a lawyer's eye before anyone files:

  • 🪶 Restricted Native allotments and restricted townsite lots. Land still held under federal restrictions is generally outside a state court's partition power. Partition of a restricted allotment among heirs runs through a federal process, not the superior court. If the land came through an allotment, find out whether the restriction is still on it before you spend a dollar on a state case.
  • 🏔️ Unsubdivided acreage. Dividing land in kind usually means creating new lots, and that generally has to go through the local platting authority, such as a borough platting board. A referee can recommend a line on a map, but turning it into two legal parcels may require a plat, a survey and access that meets local standards.

🏛️ How an Alaska Partition Case Moves Through Superior Court

Partition is a civil action in the superior court for the judicial district where the property sits: First (Southeast, with Juneau and Ketchikan), Second (Nome, Kotzebue, Utqiagvik), Third (Anchorage, the Mat-Su, Kenai, Kodiak and Southcentral), or Fourth (Fairbanks, Bethel and the Interior). The court system's fee schedule lists $250 to file a civil complaint not otherwise listed, and each defendant who answers pays a filing fee too.

An uncontested case where the other owner simply doesn't respond can finish in under a year. A fight over shares, credits or whether the land can be divided commonly runs 12 to 24 months, and an appeal to the Alaska Supreme Court adds more.

💸 Rule 82: Losing a Partition Fight Can Cost Part of the Other Side's Lawyer

Most states follow the American rule: each side pays its own lawyer. Alaska is different. Civil Rule 82 generally awards the prevailing party part of its attorney's fees, and in a case without a money judgment the schedule is 20 percent of reasonable actual fees if the case ends without trial and 30 percent if it goes to trial. Judges have discretion to vary the award, and partition cases, where every owner ends up with something, don't always have a clean winner. But the risk is real, and it cuts both ways.

The partition statute adds its own cost rule. Under AS 09.45.620, the costs of partition, including referee fees and disbursements, are paid by the owners in proportion to their interests and can become a lien on each share. When the litigation is really between only some of the owners, the court can put the expense of that fight on those parties. So the cousin who forces a two-year contest over whether a lot can be split may end up carrying those costs alone.

The practical lesson: in Alaska, an unreasonable position costs more than it would in most states. A written, reasonable offer early, made through counsel, protects you if the case later goes badly for the other side. Ask your lawyer whether Alaska's offer-of-judgment rules can apply to the specific claims in your case.

🧾 Credits, Occupancy and the Winter Bills

AS 09.45.280 lets the court determine "the rights of the several parties" in the action, and in practice that is where money paid on the property gets sorted out. Owners who paid the mortgage, property tax, insurance or necessary repairs typically ask for credit against the others' shares; owners who were shut out while someone else lived there may ask for an offset for that occupancy. Alaska courts handle these accountings case by case, so bring records: bank statements, borough tax receipts, contractor invoices and texts showing who asked whom to pay.

Alaska adds a cost that people in milder states don't think about. An empty house can't simply be shut off for the winter. Heat has to stay on, or someone has to winterize the plumbing properly, or the pipes freeze and the claim against the co-owner turns into an insurance fight. In a partition case, who kept the heat on and who paid for it is often the biggest credit in the accounting.

📊 Your Exits Compared

🤝 Negotiated buyout

Best when one owner wants the property and can finance it

Agree on an appraiser, agree on credits, and close with a title company. Cheapest and fastest when the buying owner can qualify for a loan in their own name. In Alaska, check whether a lender will finance a cabin with no year-round road access or no well and septic approval before you count on it.

🏡 Stipulated listing

Best for a sound house in a working market

If the house is in decent shape in Anchorage, Eagle River, Palmer, Wasilla, Fairbanks or Juneau, a full-market listing will normally beat any cash offer, including ours. The partition filing gives you the leverage to get the other owner to sign a listing agreement; the court order makes it stick.

🔨 Referee auction

The fallback nobody should aim for

An execution-style public sale with investor-heavy bidding, little or no inspection, and fees off the top. Use it only when the other owner refuses every alternative, and even then, ask the court whether a referee can list privately or set a minimum bid.

💵 Sell your share or the whole house for cash

Best when the house needs work, the co-owner is unreachable, or you need out now

A buyer of an undivided share takes on the remaining owner and the case. Expect a share price well below the share's slice of full value, because the buyer is buying a dispute. A cash offer for the whole property, with every owner signing, usually lands around 60 to 80 percent of after-repair value depending on condition and location.

🧮 A Muldoon House With Two Owners, Run Three Ways

Take a 1970s split-level in Anchorage's Muldoon area that would be worth about $400,000 fixed up, with a tired roof, a failing boiler and a dated kitchen. Two siblings own it 50/50. One lives in Kenai and wants to sell; the other lives out of state and won't answer.

Carrying cost while they argue. At the Municipality of Anchorage's Tax District 001 rate of about 16.145 mills from the 2024 levy, property tax on a $400,000 assessment runs about $6,458 a year, or roughly $538 a month. Add vacant-home insurance at about $150 to $250 a month, heat and electricity at $250 to $450 a month through the winter, snow removal and upkeep at $100 to $250, and the house costs somewhere around $1,050 to $1,500 a month to hold. A two-year contested case burns $25,000 to $36,000 before legal fees.

Path 1, stipulated listing after filing. Repairs of about $35,000, listed at $400,000, sold for $390,000. Commission and closing at roughly 6 to 7 percent ($25,000), eight months of carrying costs ($10,000), and legal fees of perhaps $8,000 to $15,000 for a case that settles. Net to split: about $305,000 to $312,000.

Path 2, referee auction after a contested case. If the auction brings $270,000 (investors pricing in the roof, the boiler and no inspection), the referee's fees and sale costs come off the top, two years of carrying costs ate $30,000, and legal fees for a contested case can reach $25,000 to $50,000 for each side. Net to split might be $175,000 to $215,000, before any Rule 82 award.

Path 3, cash sale of the whole house as-is. At 60 to 80 percent of the $400,000 after-repair value, a cash offer would fall between $240,000 and $320,000, with the realistic number for this condition near $280,000 to $300,000. No repairs, no commission, closing in weeks once both siblings sign. Net to split: roughly $275,000 to $295,000.

The honest read: if the siblings can cooperate long enough to repair and list, Path 1 wins by a lot. The cash sale wins over the auction, and it wins when nobody has $35,000 for repairs or the patience for eight months of winter bills.

📋 What To Do This Week

  1. 📄 Pull the recorded deed from the State Recorder's Office online index and confirm how title is held. If the deed says "joint tenants" and you aren't married to the other owner, you are a tenant in common.
  2. 🪶 If the land came from a Native allotment or a restricted townsite lot, find out whether federal restrictions still apply before anything else.
  3. 🧾 Gather every receipt for taxes, insurance, mortgage, heating fuel and repairs, organized by who paid.
  4. 🔥 Make sure the heat is on or the house is properly winterized, and that the insurance policy covers a vacant dwelling.
  5. ✉️ Have an Alaska attorney send a written offer: a buyout number, a listing proposal, or both, with a response date. Keep a copy; it matters under Rule 82.
  6. 📞 Get two numbers: a broker's opinion of value as-is and fixed up, and a cash offer. Then you know what the auction would be competing against.

✅ Bottom Line

An Alaska partition case ends, if nobody settles, in a referee's public auction noticed like an execution sale, with no statutory buyout right and some fee-shifting risk for whoever overreaches. That makes the filing a tool for forcing a decision, not a plan for getting top dollar. Use it to get a buyout, a stipulated listing or a clean cash sale on the table, and keep the heat on while you do.

❓ Frequently Asked Questions

How is a court-ordered partition sale conducted in Alaska?

Under AS 09.45.420 the referee sells at public auction to the highest bidder in the manner required for execution sales. That brings in the AS 09.35.140 notice rules: notices posted in three public places at least 30 days ahead, newspaper publication once a week for four weeks, and a website notice starting at least 45 days before the sale.

Does Alaska give co-owners a right to buy out the others before a partition sale?

Not as a statutory right. Alaska has not adopted the Uniform Partition of Heirs Property Act, which gives co-tenants an appraised buyout option in states that use it. In Alaska a buyout happens by agreement, usually written into a stipulation the court approves.

Can I ask for partition if our deed says joint tenants with right of survivorship?

Usually yes. AS 34.15.130 abolished joint tenancy in Alaska real property, so unmarried co-owners hold as tenants in common regardless of that wording, with no survivorship, and either one can bring a partition action under AS 09.45.260.

Can my spouse and I partition a house we own together in Alaska?

Generally not while you are married if you hold it as tenants by the entirety, which is the default for spouses under AS 34.15.110 unless the deed says otherwise. The house is divided in a divorce case instead.

Which Alaska court hears a partition case?

The superior court for the judicial district where the property is located. The civil filing fee in the court system's fee schedule is $250 for a complaint not otherwise listed, and defendants who answer pay a filing fee as well.

Could I have to pay my co-owner's attorney fees in an Alaska partition case?

Possibly. Alaska Civil Rule 82 generally awards the prevailing party part of its reasonable fees, typically 20 percent without trial and 30 percent after trial when there is no money judgment, and judges can adjust that. AS 09.45.620 also lets the court put litigation costs on the owners who caused the fight.

Can an Alaska court divide a large parcel instead of selling it?

Yes. AS 09.45.290 directs a partition by referees unless division would cause great prejudice to the owners. Splitting land into new lots generally also has to go through the local platting authority, which can add survey, access and approval requirements.

Can a restricted Native allotment be partitioned in Alaska state court?

Land still under federal restrictions is generally outside the state court's partition power, and partition among heirs follows a federal process instead. Confirm the land's current status with an attorney before filing anything.

How long does a partition lawsuit take in Alaska?

An uncontested case can finish in under a year. A case with disputes over shares, credits or whether the land can be divided commonly takes 12 to 24 months, plus at least 45 days of sale notice and a confirmation hearing after any auction.

Is it better to sell my share for cash than to go through an Alaska partition?

Sometimes. If the house is sound and the other owner will agree to list, a negotiated listing usually nets the most. A cash sale of the whole property or of one share makes sense when the house needs work, an owner can't be found or won't engage, or carrying costs are eating the equity.
