Selling a House From a Michigan Jail or Prison: Know the State's Reimbursement Claim Before You Sell

A Michigan prisoner can sell a house, through a power of attorney or by signing a deed inside. But Michigan is one of the states that can sue a state prisoner to recover the cost of their imprisonment, and its law treats a home differently from the cash a sale produces. That makes the timing and the decision to sell more important here than almost anywhere. Here's how it works, plus the POA, notary, tax and foreclosure rules. Or ask us a question, free.

By Dustin Ray, Founder of Pages of Purpose LLC. Not legal advice. Michigan's reimbursement, property tax and power of attorney laws are technical, they change, and laws differ by state. Before anyone signs a power of attorney, a deed or a purchase agreement for an incarcerated owner, talk to an attorney licensed in Michigan, where the property is.

Families usually find us a few months after a Michigan sentencing. The person who owned the house is at one of the Department of Corrections' 26 facilities, the mortgage has stopped getting paid, and a parent or spouse is trying to figure out whether selling is even allowed. It is. Michigan doesn't take away a prisoner's right to own or sell real estate, and a sale can be done through an agent under a power of attorney or with a deed the owner signs inside.

What makes Michigan different is a law most families have never heard of until it's in their mailbox: the State Correctional Facility Reimbursement Act. It lets the State sue a state prisoner to recover part of what it costs to keep them in prison, and it treats a prisoner's home very differently from the money a sale turns it into. That doesn't mean "don't sell." It means the order of decisions matters, and it's worth an hour with a Michigan attorney before the house goes on the market.

🏛️ Michigan Can Bill a State Prisoner for the Cost of Prison

The Reimbursement Act, 1935 PA 253 (MCL 800.401 through 800.406), is still in force, and the Attorney General's office reports on its collections. In outline:

  • 📋 Who it covers. People sentenced to the Michigan Department of Corrections. It does not apply to someone held in a county jail.
  • ⚖️ When the State sues. Under MCL 800.403, the Attorney General files when a prisoner's assets are enough to pay at least 10% of the estimated cost of their care (or 10% of two years' cost, if that is less).
  • 💯 How much it can take. The State can recover no more than 90% of the prisoner's assets.
  • 🛑 It can freeze property. Under MCL 800.404a, the court can issue an ex parte order restraining the prisoner and others from selling or transferring property, and can appoint a receiver.

With about 32,000 people in MDOC custody, most prisoners never see a reimbursement case because they have little in the way of assets. A paid-down house, or a pile of cash from selling one, is exactly the kind of asset that gets the State's attention.

🏡 The $50,000 Homestead Carve-Out, and Why a Sale Changes the Picture

The Act's definition of "assets" in MCL 800.401a includes real property but excludes the prisoner's homestead up to $50,000 in value. And MCL 800.404a says a reimbursement judgment can't be enforced by execution against the homestead. In other words, a modest home the prisoner owns and lives in (or lived in) has some protection that a bank account doesn't.

Now think about what a sale does. The house becomes cash. Cash in the prisoner's name is an asset under the Act, and depending on the amount, it can be large enough to justify a lawsuit. Families sometimes sell a house to "protect" a loved one and end up making the money easier to reach. Other families, facing foreclosure, have no choice but to sell, and are better off knowing the risk so they can plan for it with an attorney rather than discover it later.

The questions to bring to a Michigan attorney before selling:

  1. Is the owner in an MDOC facility, or still in a county jail awaiting trial or sentencing?
  2. Was the house the owner's homestead, and what is it worth relative to the $50,000 exclusion?
  3. What debts will the sale pay off first (mortgage, taxes, child support, restitution), and what will be left?
  4. Where will the net proceeds go, and on what legal basis? A spouse who co-owns the house has their own share; money that belongs to the prisoner remains the prisoner's.
The warning that matters most: don't move a Michigan prisoner's house or sale proceeds into a relative's name to keep them away from the State. The Reimbursement Act gives the court power to restrain transfers, and moving assets to defeat a creditor can be undone and can create new legal problems for the relative. Sell openly, pay what's owed, and let an attorney advise on what the prisoner can lawfully do with the rest.

🖋️ Michigan's Power of Attorney Law Changed in July 2024

Michigan adopted the Uniform Power of Attorney Act (2023 PA 187, MCL 556.201 and following), effective July 1, 2024. If the family has an older POA, it may still be valid, but any new one for a house sale should follow the current act:

  • ✍️ Signing. Under MCL 556.205, for a durable power of attorney the principal signs, and the signature is either acknowledged before a notary or witnessed by two people who also sign. The agent can't be a witness; the notary can be one of the two.
  • 🔏 Notarize anyway. Only a notarized signature is presumed genuine, and a title company recording a deed signed by an agent will want an acknowledged POA. Inside a prison, plan on a notary.
  • 🏠 Real estate authority. Under MCL 556.304, granting general authority over real property lets the agent sell, convey, mortgage and quitclaim. The POA doesn't have to include the legal description, though including it removes doubt.
  • 📁 Recording. MCL 565.36 says a power to convey land, once acknowledged, may be recorded in the county where the land is. Title companies in practice record it with the deed.

The Michigan Attorney General publishes a statutory form power of attorney, which is a sensible starting point. Have a Michigan attorney check that it's filled in for a real estate sale before it goes into the prison, because a mistake costs another round of mail and notary appointments.

🏢 Getting Documents Notarized Inside an MDOC Facility

MDOC's Policy Directive 05.03.116, "Prisoners' Access to the Courts," says all prisoners shall have access to a notary public. Housing unit staff and librarians serve as notaries for legal documents. The fee is $1.00 per document, and a prisoner who can't pay can get a loan from the Prisoner Benefit Fund. Prisoners in segregation get notary service within two business days. Staff can decline to notarize something that doesn't require notarization, so the cover letter should make clear the document is a power of attorney or deed that must be acknowledged.

A practical routine that works:

  1. Prepare the POA or deed completely, with every blank filled in except the signature and notary block.
  2. Mail it to the prisoner using MDOC's mail rules and include a stamped return envelope addressed to the attorney or title company.
  3. Ask the prisoner to request a notary appointment through the housing unit and to sign only in front of the notary.
  4. Track the return. Two to four weeks from mailing to receipt is common.

County jails are different. Each sheriff sets its own rules for notaries, visits and mail, and some jails send an outside notary only by appointment. Call the jail before you mail anything. Our guide to getting a notary in jail or prison covers the general options.

🧾 The Principal Residence Exemption While the Owner Is Away

Michigan's principal residence exemption (PRE) removes most school operating millage from a homeowner's tax bill. The Department of Treasury's PRE guidelines address incarceration directly: an owner in prison can keep the exemption if they can show they intend to return to the home. Renting the house out signals that intent is gone, and a life sentence without parole means there's no intent to return. Each case turns on its facts.

If the home stops being a principal residence, the owner is supposed to file a rescission (Form 2602) within 90 days, and MCL 211.120 sets a penalty of $5 a day up to $200 for not doing so. The bigger cost is the tax: losing the PRE can raise a bill substantially, and a later denial can come with back taxes and interest. That's another reason to decide early whether the house will be kept, rented or sold.

A related point for buyers rather than sellers: under MCL 211.27a(3), a sale uncaps the taxable value the year after the transfer, so the buyer's taxes may be much higher than the seller's. That's normal in Michigan and shouldn't scare off a buyer, but it does affect what retail buyers can afford.

💍 Married Owners: Entireties Still Matters, Dower Doesn't

If a Michigan house is owned by a married couple as tenants by the entirety (MCL 557.71 gives spouses equal control of entireties property), both spouses normally have to sign to sell it. So a spouse at home generally needs either the incarcerated spouse's signature or a power of attorney from them. Our guide on selling when a spouse is in jail walks through those situations.

If the house is in the incarcerated spouse's name alone, Michigan used to require the wife to sign to release her dower rights. That's gone: 2016 PA 489 abolished dower effective April 6, 2017. A spouse's signature is no longer needed for that reason, though a title company may still ask questions about marital status.

⏳ Foreclosure in Michigan: A Six-Month Cushion, Then a Hard Stop

Most Michigan mortgage foreclosures happen "by advertisement," without a lawsuit. Michigan then gives owners a redemption period after the sheriff's sale. Under MCL 600.3240, for most one-to-four-unit homes it's six months. But if the house is found abandoned, the period can drop to as little as 30 days. An empty house with an owner in prison is exactly the kind of property a lender may try to prove abandoned, so don't count on six months unless someone is maintaining it.

Property tax foreclosure runs on its own calendar. Unpaid taxes forfeit to the county treasurer on March 1 of the second year, and if they're still unpaid on March 31 of the third year, the county forecloses. After Rafaeli, LLC v. Oakland County (2020), former owners can claim surplus proceeds from a tax-foreclosure sale, but only by filing a notice of intention to claim under MCL 211.78t on time. A voluntary sale before foreclosure almost always leaves more for the owner than a surplus claim after it. See our guide to selling to stop foreclosure.

🔀 Which Route Fits Your Family

Keep the house and hold it for their return

Worth a hard look in Michigan

When the sentence is short, the mortgage is manageable and a relative can cover it, keeping the home preserves the PRE (if intent to return is real) and the homestead exclusion. Put a POA in place anyway so someone can deal with the lender, insurer and tax office.

List it with an agent, signing through the POA

Best price if the house shows well

A clean, occupied or well-kept house can be listed like any other. The agent under the POA signs the listing, purchase agreement and deed. Expect four to eight weeks to find a buyer and another month to close, plus the mailing time to get the POA notarized.

Sell to a cash buyer

When speed or condition decides it

When the house is vacant, needs work, or is close to a sheriff's sale or tax forfeiture, a cash buyer who can close in two to three weeks after the POA arrives can protect equity that would otherwise be lost. Expect a lower price than a retail listing.

Get legal advice first, then decide

If equity is significant

If the house has meaningful equity and the owner is serving an MDOC sentence, spend the money on a Michigan attorney before listing. The Reimbursement Act, child support, restitution and the PRE all interact, and an hour of advice can change what the family does next.

💵 A Worked Example: A Vacant House Outside Detroit

Say the house would be worth $160,000 fixed up. It's been empty for five months, the furnace needs replacing and the kitchen and bath are original, about $30,000 of work. The owner owes $70,000 on the mortgage and is three payments behind.

Monthly cost of holding it empty (illustrative; use the actual bills):

  • 🧾 Property taxes: Michigan bills twice a year, and amounts vary widely by city and by whether the PRE still applies. On this house, assume about $3,600 a year with the PRE, or $300 a month, rising if the PRE is lost.
  • 🛡️ Vacant-home insurance: commonly $100 to $200 a month.
  • 🔥 Heat and utilities to keep pipes from freezing in a Michigan winter: $125 to $250 a month.
  • ❄️ Snow, lawn, security checks: $75 to $150 a month.
  • 🏦 Mortgage interest and late fees on top of all of it.

That's roughly $600 to $900 a month before the mortgage itself.

A cash sale. Cash buyers commonly offer 60% to 80% of after-repair value. Here, with $30,000 of work and a vacant house, a fair offer might be around $105,000 to $115,000, call it $110,000 (69%). Michigan's transfer tax is paid at closing: the state tax is $3.75 per $500 ($825 on $110,000) and the county tax is $0.55 per $500 ($121; Wayne County can charge up to $0.75). After the mortgage payoff with arrears and fees, and closing costs, the owner might net roughly $33,000 to $36,000.

A retail listing might sell the house as-is for $125,000 to $135,000 to an owner-occupant or investor willing to wait, less about 6% in commissions and the same transfer taxes, netting perhaps $45,000 to $52,000 after payoff, if it sells before the lender gets to a sheriff's sale and someone can show it. That difference is why we tell families with time and a presentable house to list. And in both cases, how much is left and whose it is are exactly the questions to take to an attorney because of the Reimbursement Act.

🙅 When You Shouldn't Sell to a Cash Buyer

Don't sell to us if the house is in good shape and there's time to list. Don't sell at all if the family can carry the payments and the owner will be home in a year or two; a home and its PRE are hard to replace. And don't sell a house with significant equity for an MDOC prisoner without a Michigan attorney's advice on the Reimbursement Act, even if it slows things down.

📋 What To Do This Week

  1. Confirm where the owner is held: county jail or an MDOC facility. Use MDOC's offender tracking or call the jail.
  2. Pull the deed from the county register of deeds and note exactly how title is held.
  3. Gather the mortgage statement, the last two tax bills and any foreclosure or forfeiture notices.
  4. Book a consultation with a Michigan attorney about the POA and, for a state prisoner, the Reimbursement Act.
  5. Prepare a POA under the current Michigan act and mail it in with a return envelope; arrange the notary appointment through the facility.
  6. Secure the house: change locks, winterize if vacant, and get a vacant-property insurance quote.

✅ Bottom Line

A Michigan prisoner can sell a house with a notarized power of attorney under the 2024 act, and MDOC makes notaries available for a dollar a document. What sets Michigan apart is the State Correctional Facility Reimbursement Act: it protects a modest homestead up to $50,000 in value but can reach the cash a sale produces. Decide whether to keep or sell with that in view, move before a foreclosure redemption period gets cut short, and sell openly.

If the house is in good shape and there's time, list it. If it's vacant, needs work or is close to a sheriff's sale or tax forfeiture, ask us, and we'll tell you honestly which situation you're in.

❓ Frequently Asked Questions

Can someone in a Michigan prison sell their house?

Yes. Incarceration doesn't take away the right to own or sell real estate in Michigan. The owner can sign a deed and purchase agreement in front of a notary inside, or sign a power of attorney so an agent can handle the sale.

What is Michigan's State Correctional Facility Reimbursement Act?

It is a law, MCL 800.401 to 800.406, that lets the Attorney General sue a person sentenced to the Michigan Department of Corrections to recover part of the cost of their imprisonment. The State generally files when the prisoner's assets could cover at least 10% of the estimated cost of care, and it can recover no more than 90% of the assets. It doesn't apply to people held in county jails.

Does the Reimbursement Act let Michigan take a prisoner's house?

The Act's definition of assets excludes the prisoner's homestead up to $50,000 in value, and MCL 800.404a bars enforcing a reimbursement judgment by execution against the homestead. Other real estate, and cash from selling a home, can count as assets. Talk to a Michigan attorney before selling a prisoner's house with significant equity.

What does a Michigan power of attorney need to be valid for selling a house?

Under the Uniform Power of Attorney Act in effect since July 1, 2024, a durable POA must be signed by the principal and either acknowledged before a notary or signed in front of two witnesses who also sign. For a real estate sale, notarization is the practical choice because only a notarized signature is presumed genuine, and title companies record the POA with the deed.

How does a prisoner get a document notarized in an MDOC facility?

MDOC Policy Directive 05.03.116 requires that all prisoners have access to a notary. Housing unit staff and librarians notarize legal documents for $1.00 per document, with loans available from the Prisoner Benefit Fund for those who can't pay. County jails set their own rules, so call the jail first.

Can a Michigan homeowner in prison keep the principal residence exemption?

Sometimes. Treasury's PRE guidelines say an owner in prison can keep the exemption if they show an intent to return to the home. Renting it out ends that intent, and a life sentence without parole means there's no intent to return. If the home is no longer a principal residence, a rescission is due within 90 days.

Does a wife have to sign to sell a Michigan house in her husband's name?

Not to release dower. Michigan abolished dower effective April 6, 2017. If the spouses own the house together as tenants by the entirety, though, both normally must sign, so the spouse at home needs the incarcerated spouse's signature or a power of attorney.

How long is the redemption period after a Michigan foreclosure sale?

For most one-to-four-unit homes foreclosed by advertisement, MCL 600.3240 gives six months after the sheriff's sale. If the property is found abandoned, the period can be as short as 30 days, which is a real risk for a vacant house whose owner is incarcerated.

What are Michigan's transfer taxes when the house sells?

The state real estate transfer tax is $3.75 for each $500 of value, and the county tax is $0.55 for each $500. Wayne County can charge up to $0.75 per $500 if its board authorizes it. Both are collected at closing.

Should we list the Michigan house or sell it for cash while the owner is inside?

List it if the house is in good condition, someone can manage showings, and there's time before any foreclosure or forfeiture. A cash sale makes sense when the house is vacant or needs major work, a sheriff's sale or tax forfeiture is close, or nobody can manage a listing. If the family can carry the house and the owner will return, keeping it may be the better choice.
