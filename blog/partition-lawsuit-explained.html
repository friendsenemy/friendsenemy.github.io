Partition Lawsuit Explained: How a Co-Owner Can Force the Sale of a House

When co-owners can't agree on what to do with a property, any one of them can usually ask a court to end the co-ownership. This guide covers how a partition lawsuit works, what it costs, how long it takes, and the quieter ways out.

By Dustin Ray, founder of Pages of Purpose LLC. Updated November 2, 2026
Not legal advice. Partition law is state law, and the rules change from state to state. Talk to an attorney licensed in the state where the property sits before you file or respond.

What is a partition lawsuit? A partition lawsuit (also called a partition action) is a court case any co-owner of real estate can file to end the co-ownership when the owners can't agree. The judge either divides the land (partition in kind) or, far more often for houses, orders the property sold and splits the net proceeds by ownership share.

Partition is old law and exists in some form in every U.S. state. It is also widely misunderstood. It is not fast, it is not cheap, and a forced sale often brings less than a normal listing would. It is still the tool that ends a stalemate when nothing else will. This page is the national overview. If the property is in Maryland, read our Maryland co-owner guide instead.

What is a partition action, and who can file one?

A partition action is a civil lawsuit asking the court to divide or sell property that two or more people own together. Any co-owner can file without the others' consent, and in most states the right to partition is treated as close to absolute.

People who can usually file:

  • A tenant in common with any share, from 1% to 99%.
  • A joint tenant. Many states treat the filing as severing the joint tenancy; some expect a separate severance first.
  • An heir who inherited a share, sometimes even before the estate is fully settled.
  • Anyone who bought a co-owner's share, such as an investor who bought out one sibling.

Married couples who own as tenants by the entirety generally cannot partition against each other while married. Divorce ends that form of ownership, and the house is usually dealt with in the divorce case.

How does a partition lawsuit work, step by step?

Most residential partition cases follow the same basic path: a demand, a filing, service, a court order, a court-supervised sale, and a split of the money. Names and deadlines vary by state.

  1. Make a written offer first. Offer to buy the other owner's share or sell them yours. Keep copies. Courts like to see a good-faith attempt, and the letter alone settles some cases.
  2. Hire an attorney in the property's state. Where you live doesn't matter. Ask how many partition cases they have handled in that county and how they bill.
  3. File the complaint. It is filed in the county where the property sits. It names every owner, each share, the liens, and whether you want division or sale.
  4. Serve every co-owner. Owners who can't be found are usually served by newspaper publication. A deceased owner's share may require opening their estate or serving unknown heirs.
  5. Wait for answers or a default. A co-owner who doesn't respond in time can be defaulted. One who does respond can dispute shares, raise credit claims, or argue for division.
  6. Get the partition order. The court confirms each owner's share and decides between division and sale. Some states appoint commissioners or a referee to report on whether the land can be split.
  7. Court-supervised sale. A referee, trustee, or broker sells the property, by listing or by auction depending on the state and the order. Many states hold a hearing to confirm the sale price.
  8. Distribution. Liens and costs are paid first. The court then applies credits between owners and splits the rest by share.

What is the difference between partition in kind and partition by sale?

Partition in kind splits the land itself into separate parcels. Partition by sale sells the whole property and splits the cash. Courts traditionally prefer division, but a single house can't be cut in half, so homes are almost always sold.

  • Division can work for: large acreage or farmland with road access, or several separate lots that can be handed out one per owner.
  • Sale is the normal result for: single-family homes, townhouses, condos, small lots, and anything that would lose value if split.

The Uniform Law Commission's commentary on its heirs property act notes that state statutes have long favored partition in kind, yet courts order sales in most cases (Uniform Partition of Heirs Property Act, official text and comments).

How much does a partition lawsuit cost, and who pays?

A simple, mostly uncontested case commonly runs about $10,000 to $30,000 in legal costs, according to a California Lawyers Association article on partition costs. Contested cases cost more. In many states the fees that benefit everyone are paid from the sale proceeds and shared by ownership percentage.

That same article names the main things that drive the bill up: title disputes, the need to evict an occupant, the use of a lawyer as referee, and how familiar your lawyer is with partition work. Other costs to plan for:

  • Court filing, service and publication fees.
  • One or more appraisals, especially when the owners disagree on value.
  • Fees for the referee, commissioner or trustee who runs the sale, set by the court.
  • Real estate commission if the property is listed, plus normal title and closing costs.
  • Your share of taxes, insurance and upkeep while the case runs.

Who pays the attorney fees? It depends on the state. Florida's statute is a clear example: each party pays a share of the costs, including attorney fees that benefited the partition, "in proportion to the party's interest," and the court can take them out of the sale money (Fla. Stat. § 64.081). California has a similar common-benefit rule. Fees spent fighting each other are often not shared. Ask your attorney how your state handles this before you file.

A hypothetical example

This example is made up to show the math. It is not a real case. Two siblings each own half of an inherited house that might sell for $300,000 on a normal listing. One sues for partition.

  • Court-supervised sale price: $280,000
  • Minus shared legal and court costs: $25,000
  • Minus referee fee, commission, and closing costs: $25,000
  • Minus taxes and insurance carried during the case: $6,000
  • Net: $224,000, or $112,000 for each sibling, about a year after filing

A cash offer for one sibling's half share today would come in well under $150,000. Whether that beats $112,000 a year from now depends on the offer, the carrying costs, and how hard the other sibling fights. Run your own numbers both ways.

How long does a partition action take?

A fairly simple case often takes six to nine months from hiring a lawyer to the sale, according to California partition attorney Scott Talkov. Contested cases, missing owners, and appeals can push it past a year.

What stretches the timeline:

  • A co-owner who disputes the shares, the value, or every motion.
  • An owner who can't be found and has to be served by publication.
  • A deceased owner whose estate was never opened.
  • Someone living in the house who has to be removed before a sale.
  • A bankruptcy filing, which pauses the case.
  • Heirs property protections, which add an appraisal and buyout window before any sale.

What protections does the Uniform Partition of Heirs Property Act give?

The Uniform Partition of Heirs Property Act (UPHPA) adds an appraisal, a right for family co-owners to buy out the person who asked for a sale, and a preference for an open-market listing over a quick auction. It applies to inherited property owned among relatives, in states that have adopted it.

Under the model act, property counts as heirs property when there is no written agreement among the owners about partition, at least one owner got title from a relative, and relatives hold at least 20% of the interests (or a similar 20% test is met). Where it applies:

  1. Appraisal. The court sets the fair market value, usually through an independent appraisal.
  2. Buyout right. Owners who did not ask for a sale get 45 days after notice to elect to buy out those who did, at the appraised value times their share. The court then sets a payment deadline at least 60 days out.
  3. Division considered first. If nobody buys, the court must weigh whether the land can be split before ordering a sale.
  4. Open-market sale. A sale is made through a licensed broker at no less than the appraised value, unless the court finds an auction or sealed bids would be better for the owners as a group.

Those terms come from the official act text. Each state can change details when it adopts the act.

Which states have adopted the heirs property act?

The Uniform Law Commission's enactment tracker lists roughly 25 states plus Washington, D.C. Adopting states include Florida, Texas, California, New York, Georgia, Illinois, Washington, Maryland, Virginia and Michigan. Check the tracker for your state, since new states keep adding it. California, Virginia, Maryland and D.C. went further and apply the protections to all partition cases, not only heirs property, as reported by the Center for Public Integrity. Bills were still pending in Pennsylvania and North Carolina at this update. Check the tracker for your state before relying on this list.

Can a co-owner stop or avoid a partition lawsuit?

Usually a co-owner can't stop a partition outright, but they can slow it, shape it, or end it by buying out the person who filed. A few situations block partition entirely.

  • A written agreement not to partition. Some co-ownership agreements, LLC operating agreements, or deeds limit partition. Courts often enforce reasonable ones.
  • Tenancy by the entirety. A married couple holding title this way can't partition against each other during the marriage.
  • A buyout. In heirs property states, the non-filing owners can buy at the appraised value. Anywhere, the owners can settle on a price before the sale.
  • A settlement to list the house normally. Many cases end once the defendant sees the suit is real and agrees to a regular sale.

Delay tactics cost both sides money, often out of the same sale proceeds. That is why most partition cases settle.

What happens to the money after a partition sale?

The sale money goes to the court or an escrow agent first. Liens and the mortgage are paid, then shared costs and fees, then the court adjusts for what each owner paid or received, and the rest is split by share.

Common adjustments, which vary by state:

  • Taxes, mortgage and insurance: an owner who paid more than their share is often credited.
  • Improvements: major work that raised the value, such as a new roof, may earn a credit, often measured by the added value, not the cost.
  • Use of the property: an owner who lived there and kept the others out, or collected rent, may owe the others a credit.
  • Routine upkeep: usually not credited.

Keep paid tax bills, bank records and receipts. Credits are decided on evidence, and they can move the final split a long way from 50/50.

What are the alternatives to a partition lawsuit?

The two main alternatives are a buyout between the owners and selling your share to an outside buyer. Both can be faster and cheaper than court, but each needs something the lawsuit doesn't.

When partition is the better choice

  • Your share has high equity and a discount would cost more than the legal fees.
  • The other owner is likely to agree to a normal listing once served.
  • You can afford to wait a year and carry your share of the costs.

When selling your share is the better choice

  • The other owner is hostile, missing, incarcerated, or likely to fight every step.
  • The equity is modest, so legal fees would eat much of it.
  • You live far away, or suing a family member is not something you want to do.

Frequently asked questions

What is a partition lawsuit?

A partition lawsuit is a court case any co-owner of real estate can file to end the co-ownership when the owners can't agree. The court either divides the land or, for most houses, orders it sold and splits the net proceeds by ownership share after costs.

How much does a partition action cost?

A simple, mostly uncontested partition commonly runs about $10,000 to $30,000 in legal costs, per the California Lawyers Association. Contested cases cost more. Commission, appraisal, referee and closing costs come on top, and in many states shared costs are paid from the sale proceeds by ownership percentage.

How long does it take to force the sale of a property?

A simple partition often takes six to nine months from hiring a lawyer to the sale. Contested cases, missing or deceased owners, occupants who must be removed, heirs property buyout windows and appeals can push it past a year.

Who pays attorney fees in a partition action?

It depends on the state. In Florida and California, fees that benefit the whole partition are shared by the owners in proportion to their interests, usually from the sale money. Fees spent fighting each other are often not shared. Ask an attorney in the property's state how it works there.

Can a co-owner stop a partition lawsuit?

Usually not outright. A co-owner can slow the case, dispute the shares, or end it by buying out the person who filed. A written agreement not to partition or a tenancy by the entirety between spouses can block it. In heirs property states, non-filing owners get a set window to buy at the appraised value.

How do you win a partition action?

Partition is rarely won or lost in the usual sense, because the right to partition is close to absolute in most states. What owners actually win or lose are the details: credits for taxes and repairs they paid, the sale method, the price, and in heirs property states the right to buy out the filer. Good records matter most.

Can I be forced to sell my house?

If you co-own the house, yes. Any co-owner can usually file a partition lawsuit and ask the court to order a sale, even if you object. Exceptions include married couples who own as tenants by the entirety and owners bound by a written agreement not to partition. A sole owner cannot be forced to sell through partition.

Can I sell my share instead of filing partition?

Yes, if you own as a tenant in common. You can sell your share to a buyer who specializes in co-owned property without the other owner's signature. The price is lower than your slice of full market value, but there is no lawsuit, and closing can take weeks instead of months.
