Do All Heirs Have to Agree to Sell an Inherited House?

You inherited a house with your brothers and sisters, and now nobody agrees. One wants to sell. One wants to keep it. One lives there and won't talk about it. Here is what the law actually lets each of you do, and the ways families get unstuck.

By Dustin Ray, founder of Pages of Purpose LLC. Updated November 2, 2026. Not legal advice. Probate and partition rules vary by state, so talk with a probate or real estate attorney in the state where the house sits before you file or sign anything.

Your parent dies and leaves the house to the kids. On paper it looks simple: equal shares, one house. In real life one sibling needs the money now, another wants to keep the family home, and a third lives in it rent-free. Meanwhile the taxes, insurance and repairs keep coming, and nobody has the power to decide alone.

Short answer: No. While the estate is open, the executor can often sell without every heir's consent, subject to the will and court rules. Once the heirs own the house together, a normal sale needs every owner's signature. But one refusing heir can't block things forever. Any co-owner can sell their own share, or ask a court to order a partition sale.

Do all heirs have to agree to sell inherited property?

It depends on where the house is in the process. Who signs the deed changes once the estate is settled.

Most inherited houses end up owned by the heirs as tenants in common. Each heir owns an undivided percentage of the whole house. You can't sell the whole house without the others. But you can sell your own percentage, and you can ask a court to divide or sell the property. That is why a holdout heir can slow a sale but rarely stop it for good.

If probate was never opened at all, start there. The U.S. Department of Agriculture describes "heirs' property" as land where heirs have the right to use it but lack clear or marketable title because the estate issues remain unresolved. That is why a title company or lender will stop a sale or loan until the estate is sorted out.

What are your options when heirs can't agree?

There are four realistic ways out, from easiest to most contested:

  1. Buyout. One heir buys the others out at an agreed price. Fast and clean when someone has the money.
  2. Sell the whole house together. Everyone signs, the house sells, and proceeds are split by share. Works only if everyone actually agrees.
  3. Sell your own share. You sell your percentage to a buyer who purchases partial interests. No one else has to sign. The buyer becomes a co-owner with your family.
  4. Partition lawsuit. Any co-owner asks the court to divide the property or order it sold. Slow and costly, but it ends the deadlock.

Option 1: A buyout (one heir buys the others)

The heir who wants the house pays the others for their shares. Usually the price starts from an appraisal and gets split by percentage.

How it works: Order a professional appraisal and split the cost. Agree on any family discount. The buying heir pays cash or refinances. An attorney or title company prepares the deed.

When it fails: The buying heir can't get a loan, the sellers won't accept the number, or the heir who wants the house wants it without paying.

Option 2: Sell the whole house together

Everyone signs a listing agreement, accepts an offer and signs the deed at closing. This usually brings the highest total price.

When it fails: One heir refuses to sign, blocks showings, or argues over every offer. A real written cash offer can help here. It doesn't have to be accepted. It just gives the family a hard floor to argue from instead of guesses.

Option 3: Sell your share alone

Your inherited percentage is yours. As a tenant in common, you can usually sell it without your co-heirs' permission once title is in the heirs' names.

How it works: A buyer who purchases partial interests reviews the title, the other heirs and the condition. They make a written offer on your percentage only. You sign at a title company and get paid. The buyer steps into your spot as a co-owner.

When it fails: The estate is still open and nothing has passed to the heirs yet, the title has serious defects, or the house owes more than it's worth.

For the full mechanics, see our co-owner selling guide and our page on selling your share of a property.

Option 4: A partition lawsuit

You ask the court in the county where the house sits to divide it or sell it and split the money. Courts usually prefer dividing land when that's fair, but a house can't be cut in half, so most residential partitions end in a sale. California's statute, for example, tells the court to order a sale when sale and division of the proceeds would be more equitable than division of the property.

When it fails: Fees eat a real share of the equity, and court sales often bring less than a normal listing. For the full process, read partition lawsuits explained.

Can siblings force the sale of inherited property?

Yes. Any co-owner can usually file a partition action, and the court can order the house sold even if other siblings object. They can slow it down by contesting it, but they generally can't stop it.

Many states now add protections for inherited family property. The Uniform Partition of Heirs Property Act, written by the Uniform Law Commission, gives co-owners notice, an appraisal, a right to buy out the heir who wants to sell, and, if a sale is still needed, a court-supervised open-market sale instead of a quick auction. New York, for example, includes it in its partition law at Real Property Actions and Proceedings Law Article 9, section 993. Check whether your state has adopted it, because it changes how a partition case runs.

State details matter. In Florida, a surviving spouse's homestead life estate can block partition, as we cover in when heirs can't agree on a Florida house. In Ohio, almost every way out runs through the estate appraisal, from the 80% consent sale to the partition buyout, as explained in when heirs can't agree on an Ohio house.

Can I sell my share of an inherited house without my siblings?

Usually yes, once the house has passed to the heirs. You can sell your percentage to anyone willing to buy it. You can't sell the whole house, and the buyer only gets what you owned.

For example, three siblings inherit their mother's house in equal one-third shares. One sibling lives in it and won't sell. One wants out now. One lives out of state and stopped answering texts. The sibling who wants out can sell her one-third to a buyer of partial interests and get paid in weeks. That buyer now owns one-third and can negotiate with the other two, offer to buy them out, or file for partition. The sibling who sold is out of the fight.

This works because it changes who is at the table. When two heirs of four sell their shares, the buyer owns half, and the holdouts now deal with a business owner instead of a brother. Often that leads to a whole-house sale or a buyout within months. The trade-off is price. Selling your share alone pays less than your share of a clean full sale, because the buyer takes on the delay and the dispute.

What happens when one sibling lives in the inherited house and refuses to sell?

Living there doesn't give that sibling a bigger share or a veto. Every co-owner generally has the right to use the whole property, so the others usually can't just evict them, but they can push for a buyout, sell their own shares, or file for partition.

Rent is a common fight. In many states a co-owner who lives in the house doesn't automatically owe rent to the others unless they shut the others out. Courts often sort out who paid taxes, insurance and repairs, and sometimes the value of that sibling's use, when they divide partition proceeds. Ask a local attorney how your state handles it.

The occupying sibling also controls access in practice. They can refuse appraisers, agents and showings. Selling your share avoids that, because the buyer takes on the access problem instead of you.

What if two heirs want to keep the house but won't buy out the others?

Wanting to keep it isn't enough. If they won't pay fair value for the other shares, the heirs who want out can sell their own shares or ask a court for partition. Under the Uniform Partition of Heirs Property Act, in states that adopted it, the heirs who did not ask for a sale get a set window to buy out the heirs who did, at the appraised value. If they don't, the sale can go forward.

A written, dated buyout offer is the best first step. Put the price, the deadline and the terms on paper and send it to every heir. A clear deadline settles more of these than months of group texts.

Why do heirs disagree in the first place?

Most deadlocks fall into a few patterns. Knowing which one you're in tells you which option will work.

  • Different money situations. One heir needs cash now. Another sees the house as a long-term asset. Neither is wrong.
  • Emotional attachment. The heir who grew up there, or cared for the parent at the end, may see a sale as erasing them. This is the hardest kind to negotiate.
  • Someone lives there. A sale means that heir loses their housing, so expect resistance.
  • Arguments over value. Without an appraisal, each heir picks the number that supports their side.
  • Old money grudges. Loans that were never repaid, or one heir who handled the parent's finances, turn every decision into a fairness test.
  • A missing, estranged or incarcerated heir. Their share still counts and their signature is still needed for a normal sale. See selling a house when an owner is incarcerated.
Reality check: These standoffs rarely fix themselves. Taxes and insurance keep coming due, repairs get put off, and the house loses value while everyone waits.

Step by step: what to do when heirs can't agree

  1. Find out where the estate stands. Was probate opened? Is there an executor? If the estate is open, the executor may be able to sell. If it was never opened, someone has to open it or use your state's small estate or heirship process.
  2. Pull the deed and the estate file. Confirm who the owners are, each person's percentage, and how title is held.
  3. Get a title search. It shows the mortgage, unpaid taxes, judgments against any heir, and any estate recovery claim.
  4. Get one real number. An appraisal or a written cash offer ends most value arguments.
  5. Make a written buyout offer. Price, deadline, terms. Send it to every heir.
  6. If that fails, try a listing with everyone's signature. Give it a set time.
  7. If that fails, compare selling your share against partition. Cash in weeks at a discount, or full share value later, minus legal fees, after a court case.
  8. Close through a title company or attorney. Never sign over a deed or take payment outside a proper closing.

Special cases that change the answer

The house is still in probate

While the estate is open, the executor or administrator usually controls the sale, not the heirs one by one. In many states you can't sell your individual share until the property passes to you. Some buyers will sign a contract now that closes when the estate transfers the house.

There's still a mortgage

The mortgage sits on the whole house, not on each share. A buyer of your share accounts for your portion of the balance. If the house owes more than it's worth, your share may have no value to sell.

An heir is missing or won't respond

The missing heir still owns their share. A normal sale needs their signature. In a partition case, courts can serve a missing heir by publication in a newspaper and move forward if they don't respond. The rest of the heirs can still sell their own shares.

An heir is incarcerated

Incarcerated heirs keep their property rights. They can sign through a power of attorney or a facility notary, or refuse. See our guide on selling a house while incarcerated.

The parent was on Medicaid

Under federal rules, states must seek repayment from the estates of Medicaid enrollees age 55 or older for nursing facility and home and community-based care. States can't recover while the enrollee is survived by a spouse, a child under 21, or a blind or disabled child, and must have hardship waivers (Medicaid.gov, Estate Recovery). Find out about any claim before anyone sells or divides the money.

Taxes on the sale

Your tax basis in inherited property is generally its fair market value on the date of death, according to the IRS. You only have a taxable gain if you sell for more than that basis. If you sell soon after the death, the gain is often small. Check with a CPA.

Frequently asked questions

Do all heirs have to agree to sell property?

Not always. During probate, the executor can often sell without every heir's consent, within the will and state law. After the heirs own the house together, a normal sale needs every owner's signature, but any co-owner can sell their own share or ask a court to order a partition sale.

Can siblings force the sale of inherited property?

Yes. Any co-owner can usually file a partition lawsuit, and the court can order the house sold and the money split by ownership share. Other siblings can contest it and slow it down, but they generally can't stop it. States that adopted the Uniform Partition of Heirs Property Act add notice, an appraisal and a buyout right first.

How do I sell inherited property without co-owner agreement?

You have two routes. You can sell only your own share to a buyer who purchases partial interests, which needs no one else's signature. Or you can file a partition action and let the court order a sale of the whole property.

What happens when one sibling is living in an inherited property and refuses to sell?

Living there doesn't give that sibling a veto or a bigger share. The other heirs can offer a buyout, sell their own shares, or file for partition. In many states the sibling in the house doesn't automatically owe rent, but courts often account for taxes, repairs and use when they split partition proceeds.

What if two heirs want to keep the house and won't buy out the others?

Wanting to keep the house isn't enough. If they won't pay fair value for the other shares, the remaining heirs can sell their own shares or ask a court for partition. In states with the heirs property act, the heirs who want to keep it first get a set window to buy out the heirs asking for a sale.

How long does a partition lawsuit take?

Often 6 to 18 months, and longer if the other heirs fight it or appeal. Legal fees commonly run from several thousand dollars to tens of thousands, and they usually come out of the sale proceeds.

What if one heir is missing or won't respond?

The missing heir still owns their share, so a normal sale needs their signature. In a partition case the court can serve them by publication and move forward if they don't answer. Their share of the money is usually held for them. The other heirs can still sell their own shares.

Do I pay capital gains tax when I sell an inherited house?

Only on gain above your basis. The IRS says the basis of inherited property is generally its fair market value on the date of death. If you sell soon after the death, the taxable gain is often small. Check with a CPA for your situation.
