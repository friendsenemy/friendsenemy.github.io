Inherited a Columbus House? In Ohio, the Paperwork Decides Who Has to Sign the Sale

Ohio heirs usually face one quiet choice early on: let the executor sell the house straight out of the estate, or have the probate court hand it to the heirs with a certificate of transfer first. The second route can turn one signature into eight, because Ohio still recognizes dower and title companies expect married sellers' spouses to sign too. Here's how each route works in Franklin County, what the house costs you while you decide, and when listing beats a cash sale.

When a parent dies and leaves a house in Columbus, most families start by asking how long probate takes. In Ohio there's an earlier question that matters more for a sale: who will be on the deed when the house is sold? The answer can be one person, the executor, or it can be every heir plus the husband or wife of every married heir. Which one you get depends mostly on a filing choice that's often made in the first few months, sometimes without anyone explaining it to the family.

This guide starts with that choice, because it drives almost everything else: how long a sale takes, how many people have to cooperate, what happens if one of them stops answering the phone, and whether a buyer will close at all. After that we cover the shortcuts for small estates, the six-month creditor window, what Franklin County's property tax changes mean for an empty house, and an honest comparison of listing versus a cash offer.

✍️ In Ohio, the Paperwork Decides How Many People Sign the Deed

An Ohio house that was in the deceased owner's name alone, with no survivorship deed and no transfer-on-death designation affidavit on record, usually has to pass through the estate. Once a fiduciary is appointed by the Franklin County Probate Court (the Probate Division of the Court of Common Pleas, at 373 South High Street), the house can leave the estate in one of two ways:

  • 🏛️ The executor or administrator sells it. The buyer gets a fiduciary deed signed by the fiduciary alone. The heirs never hold title, and the money goes into the estate account to be divided later.
  • 📜 The court transfers it to the heirs. Under Ohio Revised Code 2113.61, the fiduciary applies for a certificate of transfer, and the house moves into the names of the people who inherit it under the will or Ohio's intestacy law. From that day on, the heirs are co-owners, and any sale needs all of them.

Plenty of families choose the second route by default. It feels simpler, it's what the court's form packet walks you through, and it lets the estate close sooner. It's often the right call when one heir is keeping the house or when everyone gets along. But if the plan is to sell to a third party, transferring first can multiply the signatures you need, and a single missing signature can stall a closing for months.

🏛️ Route One: The Executor Sells With a Fiduciary Deed

When the fiduciary sells, the buyer deals with one person. The fiduciary signs a fiduciary deed in the statutory form, the title company checks the probate file for the appointment and the authority to sell, and the sale closes. Brothers in Cleveland and cousins in Phoenix don't have to find a notary.

The catch is authority. A fiduciary can sell real estate without a separate court case when the will grants a power of sale, when the surviving spouse and all beneficiaries consent in writing under R.C. 2127.011 (which sets a minimum price of 80% of the inventory appraisal), or when the court orders a land sale under Chapter 2127. We walk through those three paths in detail in our guide to selling a probate house in Columbus. For this page, the point is simpler: if the executor has authority to sell, keeping the house in the estate until closing usually means fewer moving parts.

Two more advantages are easy to miss. First, the sale proceeds sit in an estate account, so debts, final bills and the funeral can be paid before anyone gets a share, which is how the law expects it to work. Second, there's one decision-maker. Heirs can object to a lowball price, and the executor has a fiduciary duty to get fair value, but no single heir can block a reasonable sale by refusing to sign.

📜 Route Two: A Certificate of Transfer Puts Every Heir on Title

The certificate of transfer is Ohio's way of moving real estate from an estate to the heirs without a deed. Under R.C. 2113.61, the fiduciary can apply any time after filing an inventory that lists the house and before the final account. The application has to state that the estate's known debts have been paid or secured, or that there are enough other assets to pay them. Once a complete application is filed, the statute directs the court to issue the certificate within five days.

The certificate names each heir or beneficiary, where they live and the share each one takes. It then goes on record, and the Franklin County Auditor moves the parcel into those names. If three siblings inherit equally, each now owns an undivided one-third interest as tenants in common.

That's a perfectly good result if one sibling is buying out the others, or if the family wants to rent the house out for a while. It's a harder result if the family plans to sell right away, because now:

  • ✍️ every heir named on the certificate signs the deed, in front of a notary, wherever they live;
  • 💍 any heir who is married is usually asked to have their spouse sign as well (more on that below);
  • 🧒 an heir who is a minor, or who has lost capacity, can't sign at all without a guardian and court approval;
  • ⚰️ if an heir dies before the sale closes, their share goes into their estate, and now you have a second probate case wrapped around the first.

None of that is unusual. Title companies in Columbus close sales from heirs every week. But each extra signer is another person who can be slow, unreachable, overseas, in a nursing home, or unhappy with the price.

💍 Dower: Why Your Brother's Wife May Have to Sign

Ohio is one of very few states that still recognizes dower. Under R.C. 2103.02, a spouse who hasn't released it or been barred from it has a dower interest in real estate the other spouse owns during the marriage: a potential life estate in one-third of that property. Dower ends with divorce, and for property the owner still holds at death the spouse can take their regular inheritance share instead. In everyday practice, though, the rule shows up at closing: when a married Ohio owner sells, the standard deed includes a release of dower signed by the owner's spouse, and title companies expect to see it.

Here's why that matters for an inherited house. Once a certificate of transfer puts your married brother on title, his wife's signature is normally part of the package when the house sells, even though she inherited nothing and may never have seen the house. Multiply that across a family of five siblings, three of them married, and you can need eight signatures from eight people in several states. If one marriage is in the middle of a divorce, that one signature can become the hardest one in the deal.

A fiduciary deed from the estate sidesteps all of that. The estate is the seller, and the heirs' spouses have nothing to release. This is the single most practical reason Columbus attorneys sometimes advise keeping the house in the estate until it's sold.

📉 When the Estate Is Small Enough to Skip Full Administration

Ohio offers release from administration under R.C. 2113.03 for smaller estates. The Franklin County Probate Court lists the limits as a gross probate estate under $35,000 in general, or under $100,000 when everything passes to a surviving spouse. Its filing cost is around $105 to $115, compared with the deposit for a full administration.

Few Columbus houses fit under $35,000, so release mostly helps when the surviving spouse is the only heir and the estate is modest. When a house does go through a release, the court can still order the real estate transferred, and the result looks a lot like a certificate of transfer: the heirs end up on title and sign the eventual sale.

Before you open anything, check whether the house needs probate at all. Search the Franklin County Recorder's records for:

  • 📄 A transfer-on-death designation affidavit. If one was recorded, the named beneficiary records an affidavit of confirmation with a death certificate and owns the house without probate.
  • 🔗 A survivorship deed. If your parent held the house with a spouse or someone else "for their joint lives, remainder to the survivor," the survivor records an affidavit and owns it outright.

Either of those can put the house in one person's name within weeks. Just remember that if that one person is married, their spouse's dower release will likely be part of the sale.

⏳ The Six-Month Creditor Window and Why Buyers Watch It

Creditors of an Ohio estate generally have six months from the date of death to present claims (R.C. 2117.06). That deadline shapes inherited-house sales in two ways.

First, the certificate of transfer application has to state that known debts are paid or secured, or that other assets will cover them. If the house is the only real asset and there are medical bills, credit cards or a reverse mortgage, the family may not be able to make that statement honestly, and selling from the estate is the cleaner path.

Second, title examiners look at timing. A sale by heirs inside that six-month window, while debts are unsettled, gets more questions than one after it. That doesn't mean you can't sell early; executors with a power of sale close inside six months all the time. It means the paperwork should match the facts, and a cash buyer shouldn't be pushing you to transfer title just to close faster.

⚠️ The most important warning on this page: don't let anyone, including a buyer, rush you into a certificate of transfer or an affidavit that misstates debts so a sale can close sooner. If the estate owes money and the house was the asset that should have paid it, heirs who sign those statements and then spend the proceeds can end up answering for it. Ask the estate's attorney which route is honest for your facts, then pick the buyer.

🧾 Franklin County Taxes After the Owner Dies

Ohio has no inheritance tax, and the Ohio estate tax was repealed for deaths on or after January 1, 2013, so most Columbus heirs owe the state nothing just for inheriting. The property tax bill is a different story.

Credits tied to the owner living there. Ohio's owner-occupancy credit, formerly called the 2.5% rollback, and the homestead exemption for seniors and disabled homeowners both depend on the owner occupying the home as their primary residence as of January 1 of the tax year. When the owner dies and nobody who qualifies lives there, expect those reductions to come off in a later tax year. Heirs who keep a vacant house for more than a year usually see the bill rise even if nothing else changes.

The 2026 value update. Franklin County re-values property every three years. After the 2023 reappraisal, which raised median residential values by about 41%, the 2026 triennial update projected an average residential increase in the high single digits, with new values used for taxes beginning with the bills paid in 2027. Because Ohio's tax reduction factors limit how much existing levies can collect, bills don't rise in step with values, but an inherited house held into 2027 will usually cost a bit more to carry than it does now.

The rate. Effective property tax rates in Columbus-area taxing districts typically run about 1.5% to 1.8% of market value each year, depending on school district and levies. On a $250,000 house, that's roughly $4,000 a year, or $330 to $375 a month.

❄️ Carrying a Vacant Columbus House Through Winter

Every month the heirs spend deciding has a cost, and in central Ohio the expensive months are December through March. A realistic monthly budget for an empty $250,000 house:

  • 🏛️ Property tax: about $330 to $375.
  • 🛡️ Vacant-home insurance: about $100 to $200. Most homeowner policies limit coverage once a house sits empty beyond a set period, often 30 to 60 days, so call the carrier.
  • 🔥 Gas and electric: $150 to $250 to keep the furnace running at a safe setting. A burst pipe in a Linden or Hilltop basement can cost far more than a winter of heating.
  • 🌿 Lawn, snow and upkeep: $100 to $200, more if a neighbor complains to City of Columbus code enforcement about grass or sidewalks.
  • 💧 Water and sewer: minimum charges continue even with the water off at the meter.

That's roughly $700 to $1,000 a month before any mortgage payment. If the heirs spend eight months collecting signatures from spouses in three states, the delay alone can cost $6,000 to $8,000. The route you pick for title is also a carrying-cost decision.

🔀 Your Options, Rated Honestly

🏷️ Executor lists it on the MLS

Best for a sound house

If the will grants a power of sale, the house is in decent shape and nobody needs money in the next 60 days, listing usually nets the most. Columbus has had steady buyer demand in most neighborhoods, and a clean, move-in-ready house will draw financed buyers who pay more than any cash buyer, including us.

🔑 One heir buys out the others

Good if someone wants it

Here a certificate of transfer to the heirs, or directly to the buying heir if the others assign their shares, can be the right tool. Get an appraisal, agree on a number, and let the buying heir finance or pay cash.

🏚️ Sell as-is for cash

Best when condition or people are the problem

A cash sale makes sense when the house needs a roof, a furnace or a foundation repair that nobody wants to fund, when heirs are scattered and the signature list is long, when a lender's appraisal would flag the house, or when the carrying costs are eating the equity.

🧰 Fix it up first

Only with money, time and agreement

Paint, flooring and a cleanout can add more than they cost, but only if one heir will manage it and the others agree in writing on how the repair money gets paid back. Without that agreement, repair projects are where many heir disputes start.

💵 A Worked Cash Offer on a Hilltop House

Cash buyers generally pay about 60% to 80% of a house's after-repair value, lower in that range when the house needs more work. Here's how that looks on a typical inherited house on the west side.

A 1950s brick ranch in Hilltop would sell for about $230,000 fully updated. It needs roughly $45,000 of work: a furnace, a roof, kitchen and bath updates and a basement that seeps every spring. Four siblings inherited it, two of them married and living out of state.

  • 📈 Cash range: 60% to 80% of $230,000 is $138,000 to $184,000. With $45,000 of work, an honest offer lands near the lower-middle, around $145,000 to $160,000.
  • 🏷️ Listing as-is: it might sell to a retail buyer or a rehabber for $165,000 to $180,000, but only after several months on the market, inspection renegotiation, and a financed buyer's appraisal and repair requests. Subtract 5% to 6% commission and closing costs and the net is around $155,000 to $170,000, minus three to six months of carrying costs ($2,500 to $5,000).
  • ⚖️ The difference: often $5,000 to $15,000 in favor of listing on paper, narrowing once carrying costs and a failed financed deal are counted.

On this house, a cash sale is a fair option, not a slam dunk. If the executor has a power of sale and one sibling lives nearby to manage a listing, listing may still win. If the family already took a certificate of transfer and now needs six owners and two spouses to sign, the faster, simpler closing is worth more.

🤝 When an Heir Holds Out After the Certificate Issues

Once a certificate of transfer makes the heirs co-owners, the probate court's role over the house is largely finished. If one heir refuses to sell, the others' remedy is usually a partition action under R.C. Chapter 5307 in the Franklin County Court of Common Pleas. The court appoints commissioners to value the property, and Ohio lets a party elect to take the whole property at its appraised value by paying the others their shares (R.C. 5307.09). If no one elects and the house can't be divided, it's sold.

A contested partition case commonly takes several months to more than a year and costs each side thousands in legal fees. That's why the holdout problem is so much easier to avoid than to fix: an executor with a power of sale doesn't need the reluctant heir's signature at all. If you're already past that point, read our guides to what to do when heirs can't agree and how partition lawsuits work. A co-owner can also sell their own share, which we explain in can one co-owner sell without the others.

📋 What To Do This Week

  1. Pull the deed and recorder index. Search the Franklin County Recorder for a transfer-on-death designation affidavit or survivorship deed. Either one may make probate unnecessary.
  2. Read the will for a power of sale. Look for language authorizing the executor to sell real estate. That one clause decides whether Route One is open.
  3. Make a signature list. Write down every heir, whether each is married, where they live, and whether anyone is a minor or can't sign. If the list is long, raise Route One with the attorney before anyone files for a certificate of transfer.
  4. Insure the vacancy. Call the homeowner's carrier, tell them the owner died and the house is empty, and get vacant-dwelling coverage if needed.
  5. Winterize or heat it. Either keep the furnace on at a safe setting or have the plumbing professionally winterized.
  6. Get two numbers. Ask a local agent for a written estimate of the as-is listing price, and get a written cash offer. Compare the net, after costs and time, before deciding.

✅ Bottom Line

In Columbus, selling an inherited house is less about how fast the probate court moves and more about whose names end up on the deed. A fiduciary deed from an executor with authority to sell needs one signature. A certificate of transfer turns the heirs into co-owners, and because Ohio still recognizes dower, it can also bring their spouses into the closing. Choose the route on purpose, before the paperwork chooses it for you.

If the house is in good shape and the family agrees, list it, and you'll likely net more than a cash offer. If the house needs real work, the signature list is long, or one heir is already digging in, ask us. We'll tell you which situation you're in before we talk about a price.

❓ Frequently Asked Questions

What is a certificate of transfer in Ohio?

It is a probate court document under Ohio Revised Code 2113.61 that moves real estate out of a deceased person's estate and into the names of the heirs or beneficiaries, without a deed. It lists each person's name, residence and share. The fiduciary can apply after filing an inventory that includes the property, and the statute directs the court to issue it within five days of a complete application.

Do the heirs' husbands and wives have to sign when we sell an inherited Ohio house?

Often, yes, if the heirs already hold title. Ohio still recognizes dower, a spouse's interest in real estate the other spouse owns during the marriage, and title companies normally expect a married seller's spouse to sign a release of dower in the deed. A sale by the executor with a fiduciary deed avoids this, because the estate, not the heirs, is the seller.

Is it better to sell from the estate or transfer the house to the heirs first?

If the plan is to sell to an outside buyer and the executor has authority to sell, keeping the house in the estate usually means one signature, one decision-maker and proceeds that can pay estate debts first. Transferring to the heirs first is often better when one heir is keeping the house, the family plans to rent it, or everyone is local and in agreement.

How long does it take to get a certificate of transfer in Franklin County?

The certificate cannot be applied for until an inventory listing the house has been filed, which comes after the fiduciary is appointed. Once a complete application is filed, Ohio law directs the court to issue the certificate within five days. In practice, most families reach that point a few months after opening the estate.

Can we skip a full estate if the Columbus house is the only asset?

Only if the estate is small enough. The Franklin County Probate Court lists release from administration for gross probate estates under $35,000, or under $100,000 when everything passes to a surviving spouse. Most Columbus houses are worth more than $35,000. Check first for a recorded transfer-on-death designation affidavit or survivorship deed, which can avoid probate entirely.

Will the 2026 Franklin County value update raise taxes on an inherited house?

Usually somewhat. The 2026 triennial update projected an average residential value increase in the high single digits, used for bills paid starting in 2027. Ohio's tax reduction factors keep bills from rising as fast as values, but holding a house into 2027 generally costs a little more each month.

Do we lose the owner-occupancy credit after our parent dies?

Usually, in a later tax year. Ohio's owner-occupancy credit and the homestead exemption depend on the owner living in the home as their primary residence as of January 1. When no qualifying owner lives there anymore, those reductions come off, so a vacant inherited house often gets a higher bill the longer it is held.

Does Ohio have an inheritance tax?

No. Ohio has no inheritance tax, and the Ohio estate tax was repealed for deaths on or after January 1, 2013. Heirs may still owe federal capital gains tax if they sell for more than the house was worth at the date of death, but the stepped-up basis usually keeps that small when the house is sold soon after the death.

What if one heir won't sign after the certificate of transfer is issued?

The others can file a partition action under Ohio Revised Code Chapter 5307 in the Court of Common Pleas. Commissioners value the property, a party may elect to take it at the appraised value by paying the others their shares, and otherwise the house is sold. A contested case can take months to over a year, which is why the holdout problem is easier to avoid by having the executor sell.

Should we list the inherited Columbus house or sell it for cash?

If the house is in good shape, the heirs agree and nobody needs money quickly, list it; you will likely net more. A cash sale makes more sense when the house needs major work, the list of required signers is long, an heir is holding out, or carrying costs of roughly $700 to $1,000 a month are eating into the equity.
