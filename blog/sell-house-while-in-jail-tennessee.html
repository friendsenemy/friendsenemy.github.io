Selling a House From a Tennessee Jail or Prison: The Sentence Takes the Executor Job, Not the Deed

Tennessee law strips anyone sentenced to the penitentiary of the right to serve as executor, administrator or other fiduciary, but it leaves his own property alone. Whether the house is his or an estate he was supposed to settle decides who signs, which court gets involved and how fast a sale can close. Here's how each path works in Tennessee, with real costs and an honest take on when listing beats a cash sale.

By Dustin Ray, Founder of Pages of Purpose LLC. Not legal advice. Laws differ by state, Tennessee statutes and TDOC procedures change, and estate questions turn on the exact wording of a will and a sentence. Before anyone signs a power of attorney or deed, or files in probate or chancery court, talk to an attorney licensed in Tennessee, where the property is.

Most calls we get about Tennessee houses and incarceration start with the wrong worry. Families assume prison strips a person of the right to own or sell property. It doesn't. What a Tennessee prison sentence does take away is quieter, and it catches people off guard: the right to act as someone else's executor, administrator, conservator or other fiduciary. That matters far more often than you'd think, because the house in question is frequently a parent's house, and the person now in prison is the one the will named to handle it.

So before anyone hires a notary or drafts a power of attorney, answer one question: is the house his, or is he the person who was supposed to settle an estate that owns it? The two answers lead down very different roads in Tennessee, and this guide takes them one at a time.

⚖️ A Tennessee Prison Sentence Ends the Executor's Job Automatically

Tennessee Code Annotated § 40-20-115, titled "Disqualification from fiduciary office," is short. A sentence of imprisonment in the penitentiary ends the inmate's right to serve as executor, administrator, guardian, fiduciary or conservator, and the statute says it works as a removal from that office. Its roots go back to the Code of 1858, and it is still on the books.

Three things follow for a family:

  • 🚫 Nobody has to file a motion to remove him. The statute does the removing. But the probate file won't update itself, so someone still has to tell the court and ask for a successor.
  • 🧾 Courts now ask up front. Since 2019, a petition to open an estate has had to disclose the petitioner's felony and misdemeanor convictions and any penitentiary sentence (§ 30-1-117), and the personal representative swears under § 30-1-111 that he isn't disqualified by one. A son serving TDOC time can't quietly qualify as executor of his mother's estate.
  • ⏸️ Anything he signed as executor after the sentence is suspect. A title company that learns the executor was in custody when he signed a listing agreement or deed will stop the closing until a properly appointed successor ratifies or re-signs.

The trigger is a sentence to the penitentiary. Someone held in the Davidson County or Shelby County jail awaiting trial hasn't been sentenced to anything, so the statute doesn't reach him yet. A short county-jail sentence on a misdemeanor is a different situation from a felony sentence to Department of Correction custody, and felony inmates sometimes serve TDOC sentences while housed in a county jail. What counts is the sentence, not the building, so ask the estate lawyer how it applies to your facts.

⚠️ The common mistake: a family keeps the incarcerated brother as executor "because Mom wanted him to," and he signs estate papers by mail. Months later a buyer's title examiner spots the sentence, and every signature he gave as executor has to be redone by a court-appointed replacement. Fix the appointment first. It's cheaper.

🏠 What the Sentence Leaves Alone: Owning, Inheriting and Signing for Himself

Tennessee has no civil-death rule, and we found no Tennessee statute that hands a prisoner's property to a court-appointed manager. A conviction doesn't forfeit his house or his inheritance. He keeps title, he can still inherit from a parent, and he can still sign a deed or a power of attorney for his own property. The fiduciary statute bars him from managing other people's assets; it says nothing about managing his own.

That split creates a workable plan in most estate cases. Say a mother in Clarksville left her house to her two sons, and the older one, named executor, is now serving a TDOC sentence. The court appoints the younger son (or another qualified person) as administrator with the will annexed. The older son, still an heir, signs a power of attorney so someone can sign the deed for his share. One brother runs the estate, and the other still has his say as an owner.

One wrinkle is worth asking a lawyer about. Because the statute uses the broad word "fiduciary," an inmate may also be unable to keep serving as someone else's agent under a power of attorney or as trustee of a family trust. If your plan depends on the person inside acting for anyone but himself, get an opinion before relying on it.

Which court handles the estate depends on the county. In Shelby County, it's the Shelby County Probate Court. In Davidson County, probate matters are heard in the Seventh Circuit Court, which replaced the old Probate Court in 1998, though everyone in Nashville still calls it probate court. In most of the state's other 93 counties, the chancery court handles wills and estates, and the clerk and master keeps the file.

Tennessee real estate generally passes straight to the heirs or devisees at death. A personal representative sells it only when the will grants a power of sale or the court authorizes it. So in many inherited-house cases the heirs sign the deed themselves, and the executor problem is about paying debts and closing the estate rather than the sale. 🖋️ Tennessee's Durable Power of Attorney, Read Closely

When the house belongs to the person inside, a power of attorney is almost always the tool. Tennessee hasn't adopted the newer Uniform Power of Attorney Act most states moved to. It still runs on its own Uniform Durable Power of Attorney Act, Title 34, Chapter 6, Part 1, and a few features of that act matter here.

  • 🔒 Durability takes specific words. Under § 34-6-102, the document must say it survives the principal's later disability or incapacity, or words showing the same intent. Leave that sentence out and it isn't durable. That matters less for a healthy inmate than for an older parent, but title underwriters check it.
  • 🏡 The real estate power has a statutory menu. § 34-6-109 lists powers a POA can grant, including the power to buy, sell, lease or otherwise deal with real property and to sign deeds, deeds of trust and closing statements. A POA that names the property by address and legal description and grants that power leaves the title company little to question.
  • 🧾 A form from the internet is the usual failure. Generic national forms tend to skip the durability clause, describe the property loosely or omit the power to sign the closing statement. Paying a Tennessee lawyer $150 to $500 to draft it is cheap insurance.

Tennessee's act doesn't spell out a notary or witness ceremony for a financial POA. The recording statutes do. A document has to be authenticated before the register of deeds will record it, and a deed signed by an agent is only as good as the recorded authority behind it. So in practice the POA must be notarized.

🗂️ What the Register of Deeds Will Need

Every Tennessee county has a register of deeds, and that office is where the POA and the deed end up. Four state rules shape what you hand over:

  • ✍️ Authentication. § 66-22-101 requires the maker's original signature, either acknowledged before an officer or proved by two subscribing witnesses. Acknowledgments can be taken by notaries, county clerks and their deputies, and chancery clerks and masters (§ 66-22-102). Title companies strongly prefer a notarial acknowledgment.
  • 📁 Recording the POA. § 66-24-101 allows powers of attorney authorizing conveyances to be registered. "Allows" sounds optional, but no Tennessee title company will insure a deed signed by an agent unless the POA is recorded with it, usually first, in the county where the house sits.
  • 💲 Oath of consideration and transfer tax. The deed carries a sworn statement of the consideration or value, whichever is greater, and the register collects the realty transfer tax of 37 cents per $100 of that figure (§ 67-4-409). On a $230,000 sale that's $851. Tennessee custom usually puts it on the buyer, but the contract decides.
  • 🏷️ Preparer and fees. The deed must state who prepared it. Recording runs $5 per page with a $10 minimum, plus small processing fees.

🏢 Getting a Signature Inside TDOC or a County Jail

The Tennessee Department of Correction runs state prisons such as Riverbend in Nashville, Turney Center, Bledsoe County Correctional Complex and West Tennessee State Penitentiary, and contracts with CoreCivic for others, including Trousdale Turner and Whiteville. We couldn't find a published statewide TDOC procedure for outside notaries, and in our experience each facility handles it through its own channels. What usually works:

  1. Start with the institutional counselor or case manager. Ask whether a staff notary is available and, if not, how an outside notary is approved as a professional visitor.
  2. Route it through a lawyer when you can. An attorney visit is the easiest way into a contact room, and a notary who comes with the attorney rides on that approval.
  3. For a county jail, call the sheriff's office. Metro Nashville's jails are run by the Davidson County Sheriff's Office, and the Shelby County jail at 201 Poplar by the Shelby County Sheriff. Both have a process for legal and professional visits; the front desk usually doesn't know it.
  4. Bring everything in one trip: the POA, any deed, and a short letter listing what each document does. Prison mail and visit approvals take weeks, so a second trip costs a month.

Identification is where many visits fail, because a booking officer takes the driver's license. Our guide to getting a notary into a jail covers ID workarounds in detail. For Tennessee, ask the notary ahead of time what evidence of identity they'll accept from someone holding only a TDOC ID card.

💍 Married Owners: Entireties, and a Survivorship Interest Creditors Can Reach

Tennessee still recognizes tenancy by the entirety for married couples, and a deed to a husband and wife creates one unless it says otherwise. The Tennessee Supreme Court restated the core rule in Bryant v. Bryant (2017): neither spouse owns a separate half, and one spouse can't sell or transfer any part of entireties real estate alone. If the incarcerated owner's spouse is on the deed, both signatures are needed, whether the inmate signs in person or through a POA.

Tennessee has its own quirk here, one that confuses families with restitution or court-cost judgments. A judgment against one spouse doesn't become a lien on the house itself. But Tennessee courts have let a creditor of one spouse reach that spouse's survivorship interest. In plain terms, the debt can't force the house to be sold out from under the other spouse, but it can attach to what the debtor-spouse would get if he outlived his wife. On a sale, the title company may ask that any such judgment be paid or released before closing. Get a title search early so the number isn't a surprise. Our spouse-in-jail guide covers the marital side more broadly.

🛡️ The $35,000 Homestead and Who It Protects

Tennessee's basic homestead exemption, § 26-2-301, shields up to $35,000 of equity in a principal residence from most creditors. When two joint owners both live there and both claim it in the same proceeding, the combined figure is capped at $52,500. Next to Florida's homestead, which has no dollar cap, that's modest.

For a family deciding whether to sell, the takeaway is practical. If the person inside owes restitution or other judgments, the homestead exemption won't hide much equity. And it protects a principal residence, which a house sitting empty for a multi-year sentence may no longer be. Don't assume equity is safe because the house "was his home." Ask the defense or estate lawyer what judgments exist before you plan how the proceeds will be split.

⏳ Foreclosure in Tennessee Moves in Weeks, Not Months

Tennessee deeds of trust almost always allow a non-judicial foreclosure: the trustee sells the house at auction without a lawsuit. After federal servicing rules give the borrower roughly 120 days of delinquency before the first notice, the state-law part goes fast:

  • 📰 Under § 35-5-101 as amended effective July 1, 2025, the notice of sale runs in a local newspaper at least twice, with the first publication at least 20 days before the sale, and is also posted online by a registered posting company.
  • 📬 The notice goes to the borrower by certified or registered mail on or before the first publication date, to the property address, which an empty house can't receive.
  • 🔁 A sale can be postponed without republishing as long as it happens within a year of the original date.

Tennessee law gives a borrower a two-year right of redemption after foreclosure unless the deed of trust waives it, and nearly every modern deed of trust does. For an inmate, that means the first notice he actually sees may arrive a few weeks before the auction, and there's no long cushion afterward. If the mortgage is behind, the POA is no longer a someday project; see how selling can stop a foreclosure.

🧭 Your Choices, Weighed Plainly

Keep the house and appoint a manager by POA

Best for short sentences with equity

If the sentence is under two years, the mortgage is current and a family member can live there or rent it out, keeping it may make the most sense. The POA lets someone pay bills, deal with the servicer and sign a lease. It only works if someone is actually doing that work every month.

List with a Tennessee agent

Usually the most money

A sound house in Nashville, Knoxville, Chattanooga or the Memphis suburbs will typically net more on the open market, even after a commission. With a recorded POA, the agent deals with the family member and the inmate never needs to sign again. Expect 60-120 days from listing to closing.

Sell as-is to a cash buyer

Best for distressed or deadline cases

This fits when the house needs major work, a foreclosure sale date is set, the heirs are split, or the estate needs a substitute administrator before anything can move. You trade price for certainty and speed: 2-4 weeks once authority is in place.

Wait until release

Rarely the right call

Waiting makes sense only if the house is paid off, insured, occupied and the sentence is short. Otherwise taxes, insurance and deterioration pile up, and a foreclosure or tax sale can overtake the plan.

💵 Running the Numbers on a Nashville House

Take a three-bedroom house in Madison, inside Metro Nashville, owned outright by a man now serving a four-year TDOC sentence. It's empty. Fixed up, it would sell for about $320,000. It needs a roof, HVAC and cosmetic work, roughly $40,000.

Holding it. Davidson County assesses homes at 25% of appraised value, and the 2025 General Services District rate is $2.782 per $100 of assessed value, a bit higher inside the Urban Services District. On a $260,000 appraisal that's about $1,810 a year, or $150 a month. Add a vacant-dwelling policy ($120-$200 a month), minimal utilities ($80-$120) and lawn care and upkeep ($100-$150). Call it $450-$620 a month before any mortgage, roughly $27,000 over a four-year sentence, while the roof gets worse.

Selling to a cash buyer. Investors typically pay about 60-80% of after-repair value, depending on condition and risk. Here that's $192,000 to $256,000; a realistic offer for this house would land around $215,000 to $235,000, with no repairs and no commission. The buyer usually pays the $0.37-per-$100 transfer tax.

Listing it. Sold as-is to a retail buyer, maybe $265,000-$280,000. Subtract a 5-6% commission, about $3,000 in seller closing costs and two or three months of carrying costs, and the net lands near $245,000-$260,000. That beats the cash offer by $15,000-$30,000 if the sale holds through inspection. With a recorded POA and a family member who can handle showings, listing is often the better move. Add $300-$800 for the POA and notary visit to either path, or $1,500-$4,000 in legal fees if an estate needs a substitute administrator first.

🏷️ When the Open Market Is the Better Call

We buy houses in these situations, and we'd still tell many Tennessee families to list. If the house is in decent shape, the POA is recorded, someone local can let in an inspector and appraiser, and no foreclosure date is set, a listing should net more. Middle Tennessee's market is deep enough that sound houses sell.

Cash makes sense when one of the hard parts is present: a set trustee's sale date, a house too rough for a mortgage lender, heirs who won't sign until someone buys out a share, or an estate stalled because the named executor was removed by his sentence. 📋 What To Do This Week

  1. Pull the deed from the county register of deeds (most have online search) and note whose names are on it and how.
  2. Find out whether he was named executor, trustee or agent for anyone. If so and he's been sentenced, call the estate's lawyer about a successor under § 40-20-115.
  3. Check the sentence status. Awaiting trial and serving a TDOC sentence lead to different answers on the fiduciary question.
  4. Call the mortgage servicer and the county trustee's office to learn whether a foreclosure notice or delinquent taxes exist.
  5. Have a Tennessee lawyer draft a durable POA with the property's legal description and the § 34-6-109 real estate powers, and ask your title company to approve it before the visit.
  6. Book the notary through the counselor, sheriff's office or attorney visit, then record the POA in the county where the house is.
  7. Get a listing estimate and a cash offer side by side and compare what you'd actually take home from each.

✅ Bottom Line

In Tennessee, prison doesn't take a person's house, but a penitentiary sentence does take away any executor or other fiduciary role he held. Sort out which situation you're in first. If he owns the house, a well-drafted durable POA, notarized inside and recorded with the register of deeds, lets the family sell it, and listing usually pays more. If he was supposed to settle a parent's estate, get a successor appointed by the probate or chancery court before anyone signs a contract. And if a trustee's sale notice has already run, act within days, not months.

❓ Frequently Asked Questions

Does going to prison remove someone as executor of an estate in Tennessee?

Yes. Under Tenn. Code Ann. § 40-20-115, a sentence of imprisonment in the penitentiary ends a person's right to serve as executor, administrator, guardian, conservator or other fiduciary and operates as a removal from that office. The probate or chancery court still has to appoint a successor before the estate can act.

Does the executor rule apply if he's only in county jail awaiting trial?

Generally no. The statute is triggered by a sentence to the penitentiary, not by an arrest or pretrial detention. A felony sentence served in a county jail can raise the question, so ask a Tennessee estate lawyer how it applies to the specific sentence.

Can a Tennessee inmate still inherit a parent's house?

Yes. Tennessee has no civil-death rule, and a conviction does not forfeit an inheritance. The inmate remains an heir or devisee and must sign, or give a power of attorney, for any deed transferring his share.

Who sells the house if the named executor is in a Tennessee prison?

The court appoints a successor, often an administrator with the will annexed. In many Tennessee estates the real estate passes directly to the heirs, so the heirs sign the deed together, with the incarcerated heir signing through a power of attorney.

Which Tennessee court handles the estate?

Shelby County has its own Probate Court. In Davidson County, probate matters go to the Seventh Circuit Court. In most other counties, the chancery court handles wills and estates and the clerk and master keeps the file.

Does a Tennessee power of attorney have to be recorded to sell a house?

State law allows a power of attorney for conveying real estate to be registered, and in practice title companies require it to be recorded with the register of deeds in the county where the house sits, along with or before the deed the agent signs.

What makes a Tennessee power of attorney durable?

Under the Uniform Durable Power of Attorney Act, Tenn. Code Ann. § 34-6-102, the document must say that it survives the principal's later disability or incapacity, or use words showing that intent. Without that language it is not durable.

How much is the Tennessee transfer tax on a house sale?

The realty transfer tax is 37 cents per $100 of the consideration or value, whichever is greater, under Tenn. Code Ann. § 67-4-409. On a $230,000 sale that is $851. By local custom the buyer usually pays it, but the contract controls.

How fast can a Tennessee foreclosure happen while the owner is locked up?

Once the lender starts, the trustee's sale notice must run in a local newspaper at least twice, the first time at least 20 days before the sale, and be posted online. The mailed notice goes to the property address, so an incarcerated owner may learn of it only weeks before the auction.

Can a creditor of an incarcerated spouse reach a house held by the entirety in Tennessee?

A judgment against one spouse does not become a lien on entireties property itself, and the house cannot be sold without both spouses. Tennessee courts have allowed a creditor to reach the debtor spouse's survivorship interest, so a title company may want such a judgment resolved at closing.
