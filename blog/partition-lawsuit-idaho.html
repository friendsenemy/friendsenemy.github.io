Partition Lawsuits in Idaho: Three Referees, Heirs Nobody Can Find, and a Cost Judgment on Every Share

Idaho's partition chapter is an 1881 machine the district courts still run. It can bind an heir nobody can locate, divide family land one branch at a time, park a missing owner's money with the county recorder, and even order a sale on credit. It also charges every owner's share for the work, with a lien the judgment can enforce against your other property. Here is how it actually unfolds — and the two cheaper routes most Idaho families miss. Or ask us a question, free.

A lot of Idaho land has been owned by the same family since before anyone alive remembers signing anything. A grandfather dies without a will in 1974. His six children inherit undivided shares. Two of them die; their shares split again. One moved to Oregon and stopped answering letters in 1998. Nobody ever opened a probate. The deed at the county recorder still carries the grandfather's name, and no title company in Idaho will insure a sale.

That is the situation Idaho's partition chapter — Title 6, Chapter 5 of the Idaho Code, lifted from an 1881 code of civil procedure and barely touched since — was actually built to solve. It is slow and it is expensive. But it can do three things almost nothing else can: sort out ownership one family branch at a time, bind an heir nobody can locate, and hand the court a way to hold a missing owner's money until that person turns up. If your problem is a tangled title rather than a stubborn sibling, that machinery is worth understanding before you decide what to do.

🧬 Idaho Can Partition Branch by Branch — and Bind Heirs No One Can Find

Most partition statutes assume you know who the owners are. Idaho's does not. Idaho Code § 6-502 requires the complaint to set out every person's interest "whether such persons be known or unknown," and expressly contemplates shares that are uncertain, contingent, or dependent on an executory devise — and says that if a party cannot be named, the complaint must say so.

From there, § 6-506 allows service by publication on an unknown or out-of-state party, with the published summons carrying a description of the property. Section 6-529 tells the court to protect the rights of unknown holders of a life estate "as far as may be, as if they were known and had appeared," and § 6-530 requires the court to value and protect any vested or contingent future interest.

The payoff is in § 6-515. Once the referees' report is confirmed, the judgment of partition is "effectual forever" and binding on everyone named, on all unknown persons who were served by publication, on anyone claiming through them — and it is not invalidated by the death of a party before final judgment. For a family whose chain of title has three dead owners and one unreachable cousin, that single sentence is the point of the whole exercise.

Then there is the tool Idaho families almost never hear about: partial partition under § 6-509. When a complete partition among everyone at once is "impracticable or highly inconvenient," the court can first determine the shares of the original cotenants — the six children, say — partition as if they were the only parties, and then deal separately with each branch's descendants, or leave a branch as tenants in common if that branch prefers. In practice that lets one cooperative branch take a parcel and walk away while the court keeps working on the branch that is still arguing.

⚠️ File the lis pendens the same day. Section 6-504 requires the plaintiff to record a copy of the complaint or a notice of pendency with the recorder in every county where the land sits "immediately after filing," and from that moment it is notice to everyone. Skip it and a co-owner can sell or mortgage an undivided share to a stranger who takes free of your case.

🏦 Where an Absent Owner's Money Goes

Idaho answers a question that stops most families cold: what happens to the share belonging to the cousin nobody can find? The money does not go to the other owners and it does not sit in a lawyer's trust account for a decade.

Under § 6-537, proceeds belonging to an unknown owner, or to a person outside Idaho with no legal representative here, must be invested in interest-bearing securities for that person's benefit. Section 6-538 says that investment is normally made in the name of the county recorder and that officer's successors, and § 6-540 makes the recorder collect the interest, deposit the securities with the county treasurer, and keep a public account book of every investment. If the known owners have agreed in writing on their shares, or the court has already adjudicated them, § 6-539 lets the referee take the securities in the owners' own names instead and hand them over on a receipt.

Shares belonging to a minor or a protected person go to that person's guardian under §§ 6-542 and 6-543. And § 6-523 lets the case stay open after the money is paid into court so the judge can decide a fight over who is actually entitled to a particular share — with further testimony, or a reference, or full pleadings if the dispute needs them.

👷 Referees, Surveyors and the Report That Decides Everything

Idaho does not use "commissioners," and the judge does not personally draw lines on a plat. Under § 6-512 the court appoints three referees unless the parties all consent to a single referee under § 6-546. The referees allot the portions "quality and quantity relatively considered," mark them with landmarks, and may hire a surveyor with assistants (§ 6-513). They then file a report describing each share with particularity (§ 6-514), and the court may confirm, change, modify, or set it aside — or appoint new referees and start that part over (§ 6-515).

Two practical consequences. First, the referees' report is where the case is really decided; by the time it reaches the judge, most of the fight is about whether to accept it. Second, the referees cost money. Section 6-517 requires the court to ascertain the referees' expenses, including the surveyor's, add whatever fee it allows the referees themselves, and apportion the total "among the different parties to the action equitably." Consenting to one referee under § 6-546 is the single easiest way to cut that bill, and it takes nothing but everyone's signature.

The court orders a sale instead of a division only where the property "is so situated that partition cannot be made without great prejudice to the owners," and § 6-541 lets the court order one owner to pay another cash compensation — owelty — when the parcels cannot come out exactly even, using "the ordinary principles of equity."

🔨 How an Idaho Partition Sale Runs — Including Sales on Credit

If the court does order a sale, the mechanics are old and specific, and two of them surprise people.

Public auction, with execution-style notice. Section 6-524 requires every referee sale to be at public auction to the highest bidder, on notice published the way notices are published for a sale of real property on execution, and the notice must state the terms and disclose any prior estate, charge or lien the buyer takes subject to. Plan on weeks of published notice, not days.

Distinct lots sell separately. Under § 6-531 the terms must be announced at the sale, and if the premises consist of distinct farms or lots, "they must be sold separately." A family holding a house in town and twenty acres outside it does not get one combined number.

The court can order a sale on credit. This is the provision Idaho lawyers outside partition practice rarely expect. Section 6-525 directs the court to fix, in the order of sale, the terms of credit allowed for the purchase money of any portion it directs to be sold on credit. Section 6-526 then lets the referees take mortgages and other security for that purchase money — in a known adult owner's own name, in a guardian's name for a minor's share, and in the clerk of the court's name for everything else. A court-ordered partition sale in Idaho can, in other words, be seller-financed, with the owners holding paper instead of cash.

Nobody running the sale can buy it. Section 6-532 bars a referee, anyone acting for a referee's benefit, and a minor's guardian (except for the minor's benefit) from having any interest in a purchase, and declares sales made in violation of the section void. After the sale the referees report the parcels, buyers, prices, terms and securities (§ 6-533); the court confirms and orders conveyances (§ 6-534); and an owner or lienholder who buys can simply receipt for their own share of the proceeds instead of writing a check for it (§ 6-535). The recorded conveyance bars all parties, all published unknowns, and anyone holding an unrecorded deed or lien at the start of the case (§ 6-536).

📒 The Cost Judgment Becomes a Lien on Your Share

Here is where Idaho gets expensive. Section 6-545 says the costs of partition — including reasonable counsel fees spent by the plaintiff or a defendant for the common benefit, referees' fees and other disbursements — are paid by the parties entitled to share in the land, in proportion to their interests, and may be specified in the judgment. When they are, they become a lien on the several shares, and the judgment "may be enforced by execution against such shares and against other property held by the respective parties."

Read that last clause twice. An Idaho cost judgment in a partition case is not limited to the land being divided. If your apportioned share of the costs exceeds what your interest is worth, the judgment can reach your other property. The counterweight is in the same section: when litigation arises between only some of the parties, the court may make those parties pay the expense of their own side fight.

Costs also outrank private liens. Under § 6-518, a mortgage or judgment lien against one owner's undivided interest attaches only to the share allotted to that owner after partition — but that share "must first be charged with its just proportion of the costs of the partition, in preference to such lien." On a sale, § 6-520 sets the waterfall: the property's just proportion of general costs, then the costs of the reference, then the liens in priority order, then the residue to the owners. Section 6-547 adds the cost of a title abstract — with interest — to the taxable costs when the court finds the abstract was necessary. Idaho is still an abstract state in its partition statute, and on an old family title that abstract is not cheap.

⚖️ No Heirs Property Act Here — and What Takes Its Place

More than twenty states have adopted some version of the Uniform Partition of Heirs Property Act, which gives family co-owners an appraisal, a right of first refusal to buy out the owner who wants to sell, and an open-market listing instead of a courthouse auction. Idaho has not. Neither Title 6, Chapter 5 nor the Idaho Uniform Probate Code contains a buyout-right procedure for inherited land. A cousin with a one-twelfth interest in Idaho can file, and the statute gives the rest of the family no statutory right to match a price.

What Idaho gives you instead is private ordering, and you have to use it before somebody files. Section 6-544 recognizes partition by agreement without an action, and even lets a guardian consent to it for a minor or protected person on a court order. A written agreement among the owners — appraisal, buyout price, deadline, who pays what — is enforceable, far cheaper than referees, and the only "heirs property" protection Idaho families actually have.

🏛️ The Probate Route Most Idaho Families Miss

If the owner died and the estate is still open, you may not need a civil partition at all. Idaho Code § 15-3-911 lets the personal representative or any one of the heirs or devisees petition the probate court, before the estate closes formally or informally, to partition undivided interests. After notice to the interested heirs, the court partitions "in the same manner as provided by the law for civil actions of partition," and may direct the personal representative to sell property that cannot be divided without prejudice and cannot conveniently be allotted to one party.

Same substantive rules, one case instead of two, and a judge who already has the file. If an Idaho estate is open and the heirs are stuck on a house, this is the first thing to ask an attorney about. ⌛ Idaho Timelines and What It Costs

Filing happens in the district court for the county where the land is. Expect a couple of hundred dollars in filing fees, service costs on top, and publication costs if anyone must be served by publication.

Realistic schedules, assuming nobody appeals: an uncontested case where all owners are known and everyone wants out runs roughly 8 to 14 months — pleadings, an initial decree of interests, referees appointed, a report, confirmation, then a published auction. Add unknown heirs, a publication period and a title abstract on an old chain and you are at 12 to 20 months. A genuine fight over who owns what, or an objection to the referees' report, pushes past two years.

Legal fees, Idaho market: $5,000 to $12,000 for an uncontested partition with cooperative owners; $20,000 to $50,000 and up once ownership itself is contested or a report is challenged. Referees and a surveyor add a few thousand on acreage. An abstract on a hundred-year-old family title can run $1,000 to $3,000. Those numbers come out of everyone's share under § 6-545, which is why the arithmetic below matters more than the law does.

💵 A Caldwell House With Six Owners, Four Ways

Say a 1,500-square-foot house in Caldwell, worth about $350,000 fixed up, needs $45,000 of roof, HVAC and flooring work. Six owners hold undivided shares; one of them cannot be found. As-is market value is roughly $280,000.

Carrying it is not free. Idaho's homeowner's exemption takes 50% of a home's value off the tax roll, capped at $125,000 — and the Idaho State Tax Commission is explicit that it lasts only until the home's ownership changes or it stops being the owner's primary residence. Once the house is vacant family property, that $125,000 goes back on the roll. In Canyon County, where consolidated levy rates in the cities run a little over 1% of taxable value, the swing is roughly $1,200 to $1,500 a year in extra tax — check the current levy with the county assessor. Add a vacant-dwelling insurance policy at $1,800 to $3,500 a year, utilities kept on to prevent frozen pipes through a Treasure Valley winter, lawn and snow, and you are spending $600 to $900 a month on a house nobody lives in.

🧭 Four Ways Out Without a Judgment

🤝 A written buyout before anyone files

Cheapest by far

One appraisal, an agreed price per share, a refinance or a family loan, and deeds. Section 6-544 recognizes partition by agreement, and a guardian can join for a minor on a court order. No referees, no abstract, no cost lien.

🏛️ A § 15-3-911 petition inside an open estate

Fastest court route

If probate is still open, the personal representative or any heir can ask the probate judge to partition or to sell. Same rules, one file, no second filing fee.

🏷️ List it with all signatures in hand

Highest price

Idaho's market rewards a house that shows well. If every owner will sign a listing agreement and someone can pay for repairs and carrying costs, this nets the most per share. It requires unanimity you may not have.

Most certain

We will say this plainly, because the rest of the page is only useful if we do. Do not sell to us if the house is structurally sound, the owners who have to sign will sign, and nobody needs money inside six months. Six co-owners who can agree on a listing price will out-earn our offer by $10,000 to $15,000 a share on the Caldwell numbers above, and that gap is real money.

Do not sell to us if a single owner can qualify for a refinance and wants to keep the place. A buyout at appraised value pays the departing owners more than we can, and it keeps the house in the family.

📋 What To Do in the Next Seven Days

  1. Pull the deed and the recorder's index for every county where the land sits. You are looking for whose name is on title, whether it says joint tenancy, and whether anyone has recorded a mortgage or judgment against a single share.
  2. Check for an open probate in the county where the deceased owner lived. If one is open, § 15-3-911 is probably your cheapest path and it closes when the estate closes.
  3. Write down every owner and branch — names, last known addresses, who has died and when. That list is what decides whether you need publication, and whether partial partition under § 6-509 can carve out the cooperative branch.
  4. Get the tax and insurance status in writing from the county treasurer and the insurer. Unpaid Idaho property taxes and a lapsed vacant-home policy are the two fastest ways for family land to become somebody else's.
  5. Put one written buyout proposal in front of everyone before anyone files. One number, one deadline, one appraiser. Most Idaho partition cases we see could have ended here.
  6. Price the alternative honestly. Ask a local agent what the house brings fixed up and as-is, and ask us what we would pay. Then compare both against your share of a two-year case.

✅ Bottom Line

Idaho's partition statute is an unusually powerful title-cleaning tool and an unusually blunt instrument for family disagreement. It can bind heirs nobody can find, divide a family's land one branch at a time, hold a missing owner's money with the county recorder, and even sell on court-approved credit. It will also charge every owner's share for the privilege, with a cost judgment that can reach property outside the case.

So the real decision in Idaho is not "partition or not." It is whether you can get a written agreement — a buyout, a listing, or a sale — signed before someone hands the file to three referees. If you can, do that. If you cannot, find out whether a probate is still open, because § 15-3-911 is a shorter road to the same place. And if neither is available because the title is broken and the owners are scattered, a cash sale of the whole house or of your own share is worth comparing against your share of a two-year case before you file.

❓ Frequently Asked Questions About Idaho Partition Cases

Can an Idaho court partition property when some of the heirs are unknown?

Yes. Idaho Code § 6-502 requires the complaint to state the interests of known and unknown persons, § 6-506 allows service by publication on unknown or out-of-state parties, and § 6-515 makes the confirmed judgment binding on unknown persons who were served by publication and on anyone claiming through them. Sections 6-529 and 6-530 require the court to protect unknown and contingent interests.

What is a partial partition under Idaho law?

Under § 6-509, when a complete partition among everyone at once would be impracticable or highly inconvenient, the court can first determine the shares of the original cotenants and partition as if they were the only parties, then deal separately with each branch's descendants — or leave a branch as tenants in common if that is what the branch wants. It is how Idaho handles land that has been inherited two or three times over.

How many referees does an Idaho partition case use?

Three, by default. Section 6-512 directs the court to appoint three referees when it orders a partition. Section 6-546 lets the court appoint a single referee instead if the parties consent, and that one referee has all the powers of three. Agreeing to one referee is the simplest way to cut the cost of the case, because § 6-517 apportions referee and surveyor expenses among all the parties.

Can an Idaho partition sale be made on credit instead of cash?

Yes. Section 6-525 requires the court to fix, in the order of sale, the terms of credit allowed for the purchase money of any portion it directs to be sold on credit, and § 6-526 lets the referees take mortgages or other security for that money — in an adult owner's own name, in a guardian's name for a minor, and in the court clerk's name for other shares. A court-ordered Idaho partition sale can be seller-financed.

Does an Idaho partition sale have to be at public auction?

Under § 6-524, referee sales of real property under the partition chapter must be at public auction to the highest bidder, with notice published the way notices are published for a sale of real property on execution, and the notice must disclose any prior estate, charge or lien. Section 6-531 adds that terms must be announced at the sale and that distinct farms or lots must be sold separately.

Can a referee buy the property at an Idaho partition sale?

No. Section 6-532 bars a referee, or anyone acting for a referee's benefit, from having any interest in a purchase, and bars a minor's guardian from buying except for the minor's benefit. Sales made contrary to that section are void. An owner or a lienholder entitled to proceeds can buy, and § 6-535 lets the referees take that buyer's receipt for the part of the price that belongs to them.

Where does a missing Idaho owner's share of the sale money go?

Section 6-537 requires proceeds belonging to an unknown owner, or to someone outside Idaho with no legal representative here, to be invested in interest-bearing securities for that person's benefit. Section 6-538 puts the investment in the county recorder's name, and § 6-540 makes the recorder collect the interest, deposit securities with the county treasurer, and keep a public account of it.

Does Idaho have a Uniform Partition of Heirs Property Act?

No. Idaho has not adopted the Uniform Partition of Heirs Property Act, so inherited family land in Idaho gets no statutory appraisal-and-buyout procedure and no required open-market listing. What Idaho does recognize is partition by agreement without a lawsuit under § 6-544, which is why a written family buyout agreement matters more here than in states that adopted the act.

Can we partition an Idaho house while the estate is still in probate?

Often, yes, and it is usually cheaper. Idaho Code § 15-3-911 lets the personal representative or any one of the heirs or devisees petition the probate court before the estate closes to partition undivided interests. The court partitions in the same manner as a civil partition action and can direct the personal representative to sell property that cannot be divided without prejudice to the owners.

Why does a title abstract come up in an Idaho partition case?

Because Idaho still taxes it as a cost. Section 6-547 provides that if the court finds an abstract of title was necessary and the plaintiff — or a defendant, where the plaintiff did not get one — procured it, the cost of the abstract with interest must be allowed and taxed. On an old family chain of title an abstract can run $1,000 to $3,000, and under § 6-545 it comes out of everyone's share.
