Partition Lawsuits in Hawaii: The Court Can Award the Ground You've Lived On, Clear the Title, and Split the Lawyers' Bills

Hawaii's partition statute gives a circuit court unusually broad tools. A judge can set apart the part of the land an owner has occupied or improved, settle disputed and unknown ownership in the same case, and divide attorney's fees among the owners based on who benefited. Since 2017, inherited family land has its own procedure run by a special master. For a family that has shared land for generations, those tools can mean keeping the homestead. For a family that just wants out, they mean more steps before the check. Here's how it works. Or ask us a question, free.

On the mainland, a partition lawsuit over a single house almost always ends in a sale. Hawaii's law is built for something different. Much of the co-owned property here is family land that has passed through several generations, sometimes with two or three houses on one lot, sometimes with ancestral interests nobody has formally traced. The statute gives the court tools to sort that out, and the most important of them is the power to give a particular owner a particular piece.

🌿 A Hawaii Court Can Award the Ground You've Lived On

Under HRS § 668-7, a circuit court in a partition case can do much more than order a sale. Among other powers, it can:

  • 🏡 Set apart a particular portion to a particular owner who, "by prior occupation or improvement or otherwise," is equitably entitled to it, and require that owner to pay for any value beyond their proportionate share.
  • ⚖️ Balance things out with money. The court can sell other portions and use the proceeds to equalize the division.
  • 🧩 Divide part and sell the rest, or sell the whole, when partition in kind would be impracticable or "greatly prejudicial" to the owners.
  • 🎲 Divide by agreement or by drawing lots.

Hawaii courts have used these powers for a long time. The case notes to § 668-7 cite decisions going back to 1897 that consider improvements a co-owner made, and a 1977 Hawaii Supreme Court decision upholding a partition in kind. A 2005 decision held that a court isn't required to divide strictly by each owner's percentage just because a party asks; it can consolidate and re-subdivide the land in the way that's equitable.

What that means in practice: if your cousin built the back house twenty years ago and has lived in it ever since, a Hawaii judge can consider giving your cousin that part of the land, with a payment to the rest of you if it's worth more than their share. The owner who wants a sale doesn't automatically get one.

⚠️ County rules still decide whether land can be split. Under § 668-7(b), any subdivision plan in a partition case must be approved by the county planning department before the court approves it. Minimum lot sizes and zoning on Oahu, Maui, Kauai or the Big Island often decide whether a division is possible at all. If the county won't allow two lots, the "back house" solution may not exist.

📜 Title Comes First: Disputed Claims, Unknown Heirs and Old Interests

A Hawaii partition case doubles as a title proceeding. Section 668-7 lets the court determine "any and all questions of conflicting or controverted titles or claims," remove clouds on title, and vest title by judgment without needing deeds from minors or unknown or absent owners. Section 668-8 provides for a jury on title disputes when demanded.

Other sections handle the problems that are common with old family land:

  • 🔍 Unknown and absent owners. Section 668-9 addresses owners who can't be identified or found, so the case can proceed.
  • 📦 Unproved shares. Under § 668-10, if someone has color of title to a share but hasn't proved legal title, and nobody contests it, the court can set aside land or sale proceeds for whoever turns out to own that share and partition the rest.
  • 🧱 Paramount claims. Under § 668-8.5, a person who didn't appear and claims title superior to the co-owners' title isn't bound by a partition judgment unless the case is combined with a quiet title action under chapter 669.

That last rule is why many Hawaii partition cases are filed together with a quiet title claim. Combined quiet title and partition actions have been used to consolidate land with many small, inherited interests, and they've drawn public criticism when they pushed out family owners who held tiny fractions. If you've been served with one of these, don't ignore it. A small inherited interest can still be worth real money, and the heirs property act may give your family rights that didn't exist before 2017.

👪 Since 2017, Family Land Goes Through a Special Master

Hawaii adopted the Uniform Partition of Heirs Property Act as HRS chapter 668A, and it applies to partition actions filed on or after January 1, 2017. In every partition case, the court must decide whether the property is heirs property. If it is, chapter 668A governs unless all co-owners agree otherwise in a record.

Hawaii's version runs much of the process through a special master. After the court determines the property's value, the special master notifies the parties that any owner who didn't request a sale may buy out the owners who did. Under § 668A-8:

  1. 🗓️ Owners have 45 days after that notice to elect to buy all the interests of the owners who asked for a sale.
  2. 💲 The price for each interest is the court's value for the whole property times that owner's fraction.
  3. 🏦 The court sets a payment deadline at least 60 days after the notice of who's buying.
  4. 🔁 If some buyers don't pay, the ones who did get 20 days to take the remaining interest.
  5. 🧾 The owner who requested partition by sale is liable for reasonable court fees.

If no buyout happens, the act prefers dividing the land over selling it. Section 668A-10 tells the court to weigh, among other factors, how long the family has owned or possessed the property, any owner's "sentimental attachment" including ancestral or unique value, the lawful use an owner is making of it, and who has paid taxes, insurance and upkeep. No one factor decides it. A sale, if ordered, is generally an open-market sale under § 668A-11 rather than an auction.

For a Hawaii family with deep roots on the land, these factors carry real weight. For the family side of these disputes, see our guide on what to do when heirs can't agree.

🔨 Sold Outside the Heirs Act: An Auction, Then Bidding Can Reopen in Court

When property that isn't heirs property is sold in a chapter 668 case, § 668-14 requires a public auction after newspaper notice at least once a week for four successive weeks, with the first notice at least 30 days before the sale. Every sale must be reported by the commissioners and approved and confirmed by the court.

The confirmation step has a Hawaii twist. The Hawaii Supreme Court held in 2004 that the circuit court has equitable discretion to reopen bidding after the auction but before confirmation, and can refuse to confirm the high bid without finding the price was so low it amounted to fraud. In practice, that means the auction price isn't always the final price. A higher bidder can show up at the confirmation hearing. If you're an owner, that's a protection against a bargain-basement sale. If you're hoping to buy the property yourself at auction, plan for competition at confirmation.

💸 Who Pays: Shared Costs and Apportioned Attorney's Fees

Hawaii's cost rule is friendlier to the owner who files than many states'. Under HRS § 668-17:

  • 💵 The plaintiff pays the costs of the proceeding up front, but eventually all owners pay them in proportion to their interests.
  • ⚔️ Costs caused by a contest over a particular owner's share are charged against that share and paid according to how that fight comes out.
  • 👩‍⚖️ The judge may also allow attorney's fees for any party's lawyer and apportion them among the owners as is equitable, based on the services performed and the benefit each owner received. The Hawaii Supreme Court has applied this to fees in a quiet title action that was needed for the partition.
  • 📮 If more than ten defendants are named, the plaintiff's cost burden is capped at what it would be with ten.

So the owner who does the work of clearing title and getting the property sold can often recover part of that cost from the others' shares, while an owner who starts a losing fight over their own share bears the cost of it.

🧮 Credit for Living There, and the Rent Offset

An owner who has been paying taxes, insurance and repairs will ask for credit. Hawaii's Intermediate Court of Appeals held in 2010 that, in a partition case without ouster or an agreement, a court has discretion to allow an apportioned rental offset against those contributions, to the extent the reasonable rental value of the occupying owner's use exceeded their share of ownership. In other words, the cousin who lived in the house and paid the bills may get credit for the bills, but the court can weigh it against the value of having lived there.

💵 A Waianae Lot With Two Houses, Three Ways

Four cousins inherited their grandmother's lot in Waianae on Oahu, a quarter each. There's a main house and a small back house where one cousin has lived for fifteen years, paying the property tax and fixing the roof. Fixed up, the property might sell for about $760,000; as it stands, about $650,000.

  • 🧾 Holding costs. At Honolulu's fiscal 2027 Residential rate of $3.50 per $1,000, $650,000 of value carries about $2,275 a year in property tax before any homeowner exemption, around $190 a month. Hurricane and dwelling coverage on an older, partly vacant property can run $150 to $300 a month, plus utilities and upkeep on the main house.
  • 🌿 Division under § 668-7: if Honolulu's zoning allows the lot to be split, the court could award the back-house portion to the cousin who lives there, with an equalizing payment if it's worth more than a quarter, and sell the main-house portion. It's the slowest path, but it's the one that keeps a home in the family.
  • 👪 Heirs property buyout: if the court values the whole at $650,000, the cousin who lives there could buy the other three quarters for about $487,500 within the statute's windows, if a lender will finance it.
  • 💵 Cash sale before filing: at 60 to 80 percent of the $760,000 repaired value, $456,000 to $608,000. For this property we'd likely be around $500,000 to $540,000. Each cousin's quarter would be roughly $125,000 to $135,000 before credits for the one who paid the taxes and roof, with no special master, no subdivision application and no year of carrying costs.

The honest takeaway: if the cousin in the back house can finance a buyout or the lot can legally be split, those outcomes beat our offer and keep the family on the land. A cash sale makes sense if nobody can finance a buyout, the county won't allow a split, and the cousins would rather have a certain number now than a court-supervised sale a year or two from now.

🧭 Paths Out of a Hawaii Co-Ownership

🌿 Division that keeps a home in the family

Uniquely possible in Hawaii

Section 668-7 lets the court award the portion an owner occupied or improved. Check county zoning before you count on it.

🤝 Buyout, inside or outside the heirs act

Fairest when someone can pay

An agreed appraisal and a refinance avoids the special master's timeline. Inside the act, the 45-day election and payment deadlines are strict.

🏷️ Open-market listing

Best price for a sound home

Hawaii's tight inventory rewards a well-presented listing. If all owners sign, it usually beats an auction and a cash offer.

💵 Cash sale of the whole or one share

Fastest and most certain

Worth the discount when title is messy, the house needs work, or carrying costs and fees would eat the difference.

📋 Steps to Take This Week

  1. 📄 Get the deed and title history, and find out whether the land is in Land Court or the Regular System.
  2. 👪 Map every owner and how they inherited, including anyone who has died since, and note owners you can't locate.
  3. 🗺️ Ask the county planning department what minimum lot size and zoning apply before you assume the land can be divided.
  4. 🧾 Gather records of taxes, insurance, repairs and improvements each owner paid for.
  5. 🏡 Write down who has lived on the property and for how long; occupation and improvements matter under § 668-7.
  6. ✉️ Propose a written plan to the others: a buyout at an appraised value, a division if zoning allows, or a sale.

✅ Bottom Line

Hawaii partition law is built for family land. Judges can award the piece an owner has lived on or improved, settle old and disputed titles, spread attorney's fees by benefit, and reopen bidding before confirming a sale, and since 2017 inherited property gets a special master, a buyout window and a preference for division. Know which of those tools fit your land, check the county's zoning, and put a written proposal in front of your relatives before the case gets expensive.

❓ Frequently Asked Questions

Can a Hawaii court give me the part of the land I live on in a partition?

It can. HRS 668-7 lets the court set apart a portion to an owner who is equitably entitled to it by prior occupation or improvement, and require payment for any value beyond that owner's share.

Does the county have to approve a division of land in a Hawaii partition?

Yes. Under HRS 668-7(b), a subdivision plan in a partition case must be approved by the county planning department before the court approves it, so zoning and minimum lot sizes can rule out a division.

Who pays attorney's fees in a Hawaii partition case?

Under HRS 668-17, costs are eventually shared by all owners in proportion to their interests, and the judge may allow attorney's fees for any party and apportion them based on services performed and benefit received.

When does Hawaii's heirs property act apply?

Chapter 668A applies to partition actions filed on or after January 1, 2017. The court must decide whether the property is heirs property, and if it is, the act governs unless all co-owners agree otherwise in a record.

How long do family members have to buy out a co-owner under Hawaii's heirs property act?

Owners who didn't request a sale have 45 days after the special master's notice to elect to buy, and the court sets a payment deadline at least 60 days after the notice of who is buying.

Who pays court fees when an owner asks for partition by sale of heirs property in Hawaii?

Under HRS 668A-8(i), the co-owner who requested partition by sale is liable for reasonable court fees.

How is a Hawaii partition sale conducted?

Outside the heirs property act, by public auction after newspaper notice once a week for four weeks, the first at least 30 days before the sale, with the sale subject to court confirmation. Heirs property sales are generally open-market sales.

Can bidding reopen after a Hawaii partition auction?

Yes. The Hawaii Supreme Court held in 2004 that the circuit court may reopen bidding after the auction but before confirmation, and may decline to confirm the high bid without a finding of fraud.

What if some Hawaii heirs are unknown or can't be found?

The partition statute lets the court proceed against unknown and absent owners, vest title by judgment without their deeds, and set aside land or proceeds for unproved shares under HRS 668-10.

Is a cash sale better than a Hawaii partition lawsuit?

Not when an owner can finance a buyout or the land can legally be divided, because those keep more value in the family. A cash sale makes sense when title is messy, repairs are heavy, or no buyout or division is realistic.
