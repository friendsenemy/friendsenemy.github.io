Partition Lawsuits in Kansas: Any Owner Can Elect to Take the Property at the Commissioners' Value Before It Goes to the Sheriff

Kansas handles partition with one compact statute, K.S.A. 60-1003, that has barely changed since 1964. Its most important feature is easy to miss: when the property cannot be divided, any party may elect to take it at the value fixed by three court-appointed commissioners. Only if nobody elects — or two owners elect against each other — does the sheriff sell it, and even then not for less than two-thirds of that value. Here is how to use that election, and how to avoid being on the wrong end of it. Or ask us a question, free.

Most partition statutes give co-owners two outcomes: split the land or sell it to the public. Kansas adds a third that sits between them, and it changes the strategy of the whole case. Subsection (c)(4) of K.S.A. 60-1003 says that where the property is not subject to partition in kind, "any one or more of the parties may elect within a time so fixed by the judge to take the property or any separate tract at the appraised value."

Read that slowly. A sibling who wants to keep the family house does not have to outbid investors on the courthouse steps. She can take it at the number the commissioners set — as long as nobody else elects against her. That one provision is why a Kansas partition is usually won or lost at the appraisal, not at the sale.

🙋 Electing to Take the Property: The Kansas Buyout Built Into Every Case

The election is available to "any one or more of the parties," not just heirs and not just the defendants. It applies to the property as a whole or to "any separate tract," which matters for families who own a house in town and a quarter-section in the county. The judge fixes the deadline; there is no statutory number of days, so read the order carefully and calendar it.

Three things to understand before you rely on it:

  • 💵 You pay the other owners their share of the appraised value. Electing on a $180,000 appraisal when you own half means coming up with roughly $90,000, plus your share of the costs. Line up financing before the deadline; a lender will want a copy of the commissioners' report and the court's order.
  • ⚔️ Two elections cancel each other. The statute sends the property to the sheriff if "two or more elect to so take, in opposition to each other." If your brother also wants the house, the election stops being a shortcut and you will compete at the sale instead.
  • 📉 A low appraisal helps whoever elects. If you do not want the property, the commissioners' number is your floor, not just theirs. Do not leave valuation to the other side.
⚠️ The key warning: in Kansas, the commissioners' appraisal is the price at which a co-owner can take your share. If you skip the commissioners' process and fail to file exceptions to a low report, you may find your interest bought at that number with no auction at all.

👥 Three Commissioners Do the Work, Not the Judge

After the judge decides each party's interest and orders partition, 60-1003(c)(2) requires the appointment of three commissioners. Their first job is to divide the property among the owners according to their interests. If that "cannot be made without manifest injury, or is for any reason impracticable," they appraise it instead, valuing each tract separately, and report to the court.

Commissioners are usually local people with real estate knowledge — appraisers, brokers, sometimes farmers for rural ground. Give them what they need: repair bids, rent history, photos, and comparable sales. That last item is harder in Kansas than almost anywhere, because sale prices filed with deeds are confidential under K.S.A. 79-1437f. An agent with MLS access or a licensed appraiser is often the only way to put real comparables in front of the commissioners.

Any party may file exceptions to the report. Under 60-1003(c)(3) the judge then holds a hearing and may approve, disapprove, modify the report, award specific portions to specific parties, or direct further proceedings. If nobody objects to a report dividing the land in kind, the judge enters judgment on it. Exceptions are your one formal chance to fix a bad number before it becomes the election price.

🔨 The Sheriff's Sale and Its Two-Thirds Floor

When nobody elects, or competing parties elect, the judge orders the sheriff to sell the property "in the manner provided for sale of property on execution." That means a public sale after published notice, run by the county sheriff, followed by the court's confirmation. The protective rule is in the same sentence of the statute: "No sale shall be made at less than two-thirds of the valuation placed upon the property by the commissioners."

The floor protects owners from a give-away, but notice what it is tied to: the commissioners' valuation again. On a $240,000 appraisal the floor is $160,000. Sheriff's sales attract investors who bid with repairs, holding time and risk in mind, so bids frequently cluster near the floor rather than near market value. If the sale fails to reach the floor, it cannot be made at that price, and the parties are back before the judge.

🛑 Kansas Judges Can Refuse Partition Outright

Subsection (d) gives the court full power to make any order needed for "a just and equitable partition," and it ends with a clause many states lack: the court "may refuse partition if the same would result in extraordinary hardship or oppression." The bar is high — the Kansas Supreme Court has said a cotenant is not required to furnish another with a home because of their financial circumstances — but in a case involving, say, an elderly co-owner, a property bound up with another person's business, or an unusual family arrangement, the clause gives a defendant something to argue.

🛢️ Mineral Interests and Farm Ground

The same statute reaches "an estate or interest created by an oil, gas or mineral lease or an oil or gas royalty." Kansas courts treat oil and gas lease interests like real estate for partition purposes, though an overriding royalty with no possessory interest has been held not subject to partition. Where a family owns a farmhouse and severed minerals, ask early whether the minerals are part of the case and whether they should be valued as a separate tract — the election works tract by tract, so one sibling may take the house while the minerals are sold or divided.

Farm ground is also where partition in kind is realistic. A 320-acre farm with two roads can sometimes be split; a three-bedroom house on a city lot cannot.

🧾 What the Answer Must Say

Under 60-1003(b) each defendant's answer states the nature and extent of their interest and may deny the interests claimed by anyone else. One Kansas-specific trap: any claim of adverse possession must be affirmatively pleaded, and the defendant carries the burden of proving it. A co-owner who has lived in the house for decades and believes they now own it all must say so in the answer, not later.

Credits for money spent belong in the case too. Kansas courts have required accountings in partition cases and have allowed a cotenant reimbursement for improvements measured by how much the improvements increased the property's value — not simply what they cost.

💲 Who Pays: Costs and Fees Divided by Ownership Share

Subsection (c)(5) tells the court to tax "the costs, attorney fees and expenses," including the cost of bringing an abstract of title up to date or obtaining title insurance, and to apportion them among the parties according to their interests. The Kansas Supreme Court has treated the attorney fee award as discretionary, with factors for the trial court to weigh. In practice that means a co-owner with a one-quarter share should expect to bear about a quarter of the shared costs and any fees the court awards, usually taken from the sale proceeds.

⌛ How Long a Kansas Partition Takes, and What It Costs to Carry

  • 🗓️ Uncontested: service and answers take one to two months; commissioners and their report another two to four; an election or a sheriff's sale and confirmation another two to four. Six to twelve months is common.
  • ⚔️ Contested: disputes over shares, adverse possession or exceptions to the report push cases past a year, and appeals add more.
  • 💵 Shared costs: filing and service fees in the low hundreds; commissioners' fees and an abstract or title report often $1,000–$3,000 combined; awarded attorney fees from a few thousand dollars upward.
  • 🏚️ Carrying the property: Kansas property tax runs roughly 1.3–1.6% of market value a year, so a $180,000 Topeka house costs about $200–$240 a month in tax, plus $100–$200 for insurance, $120–$250 for utilities and yard care. Over a year that is $5,000–$8,000 the owners split one way or another.

💵 A Topeka House, Three Ways Out

Two brothers and a sister inherit their mother's house in Topeka in equal thirds. The commissioners appraise it at $165,000 as-is; fully repaired it would be worth about $215,000. The sister lives nearby and wants to keep it. The brothers want cash.

  • 🙋 Sister elects: she owes each brother one-third of $165,000 — $55,000 each, $110,000 total — plus her share of costs. If neither brother elects against her, she keeps the house.
  • 🔨 Nobody elects: the sheriff sells; the floor is $110,000. If the winning bid is $130,000, each third is about $43,000 before roughly $3,000–$4,000 of shared costs per person.
  • 💵 A cash offer to all three: 60–80% of $215,000 is $129,000–$172,000; a realistic offer on this house might be $140,000–$150,000, closing in weeks, with no commissioners or sheriff.

If the sister can finance her election, that is probably the best outcome for the family and better for the brothers than a sheriff's sale. Our offer only beats it if nobody wants the house and the alternative is a sheriff's auction.

🗂️ Your Options in a Kansas Partition

📐 Win the appraisal

Everything depends on it

Feed the commissioners real comparables and repair bids, and file exceptions if the report is wrong. Their number becomes both the election price and the sale floor.

🙋 Elect to take the property

Best way to keep it

Meet the judge's deadline with financing in place. Talk to the other owners first so you are not met with a competing election.

🤝 Settle before the report

Cheapest

Agree on a buyout or a listing and file a stipulation. You skip commissioners' fees and the sheriff entirely.

🚫 Don't Sue a Co-Owner Who Will Sign

If the other owners will sign a listing, a partition case only adds commissioners, a sheriff and attorney fees that come out of everyone's share. Filing makes sense when someone refuses to sell, cannot be found, or is living in the property without paying. And if the property is in good condition with cooperative owners, list it — a cash sale to us would leave money on the table.

✅ Bottom Line

A Kansas partition revolves around one number. Three commissioners set it; any owner can take the property at it; the sheriff cannot sell below two-thirds of it. If you want the house, plan to elect and have financing ready. If you want out, make sure the commissioners' number is honest, because it may be the price your share is bought at. And if everyone can still agree, a stipulated buyout or listing beats the whole process.

❓ Kansas Partition: Ten Common Questions

Can I keep the house in a Kansas partition case by buying out the other owners?

Yes, through the election in K.S.A. 60-1003(c)(4). Where the property cannot be divided, any party may elect within the time the judge sets to take the property, or any separate tract, at the commissioners' appraised value. If two or more parties elect against each other, the property goes to a sheriff's sale instead.

Who decides what the property is worth in a Kansas partition?

Three commissioners appointed by the judge. They first try to divide the property; if that would cause manifest injury or is impracticable, they appraise it, valuing each tract separately, and report to the court. Any party may file exceptions, and the judge may approve, disapprove or modify the report.

What is the minimum price at a Kansas partition sheriff's sale?

No sale may be made for less than two-thirds of the commissioners' valuation. The sale is conducted by the sheriff in the manner provided for sales on execution and must be confirmed by the court.

Can a Kansas judge refuse to order partition?

Yes. K.S.A. 60-1003(d) allows the court to refuse partition if it would result in extraordinary hardship or oppression. Courts apply that sparingly; financial hardship alone usually is not enough, since one co-owner is not required to provide another with a home.

Who pays attorney fees in a Kansas partition lawsuit?

The court taxes costs, attorney fees and expenses, including bringing an abstract of title up to date or obtaining title insurance, and apportions them among the parties according to their interests. Kansas courts treat the amount of any attorney fee award as a matter for the trial court's discretion.

Can oil and gas or mineral interests be partitioned in Kansas?

Yes. K.S.A. 60-1003 expressly covers interests created by an oil, gas or mineral lease and oil or gas royalties, and Kansas courts treat lease interests like real estate for partition. An overriding royalty without a possessory interest has been held not subject to partition.

I have lived in the property for years. Can I claim I own it all in a Kansas partition?

Only if you plead it. Under K.S.A. 60-1003(b), a claim of adverse possession must be affirmatively pleaded in the answer, and the defendant claiming it carries the burden of proof. Possession by one co-owner is generally presumed to be on behalf of all, so these claims are hard to win.

Will I be reimbursed for improvements I paid for on a co-owned Kansas property?

Possibly. Kansas courts have required accountings in partition cases and have allowed reimbursement for improvements based on how much they increased the property's value, rather than simply their cost. Bring receipts and evidence of the value added.

How long does a partition lawsuit take in Kansas?

An uncontested case commonly runs six to twelve months from filing to an election or confirmed sale. Exceptions to the commissioners' report, disputes about ownership shares, adverse possession claims or competing elections can push it well past a year.

Is it better to sell my share to a cash buyer than go through a Kansas partition?

Not always. If a family member can elect at the commissioners' value, or the owners can agree on a listing, those usually leave you more. A cash sale of your share or the whole property makes sense when nobody wants to elect, the property needs significant repairs, or the alternative is a sheriff's sale near the two-thirds floor.
