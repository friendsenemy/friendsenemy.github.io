In Alabama, the Words on Your Deed and a 10-Day Deadline Decide Who Keeps a Co-Owned House

Alabama recognizes two kinds of survivorship deeds, and only one of them can be broken by a single owner. And when one owner files for a sale, the other has a statutory right to buy them out at an appraised price. Here is how those rules play out, what the money looks like, and when listing beats a cash sale.

Most co-owners who search this question want to know one thing: can I force a sale? In Alabama the better first question is a different one. What exactly does your deed say? Alabama treats survivorship language more strictly than most states, recognizes two kinds of survivorship that behave in opposite ways, and gives any co-owner a statutory right to buy out the person who files for a sale. Those three rules decide more Alabama co-ownership fights than any argument about fairness.

This guide is for living co-owners: unmarried partners who bought together, siblings or friends who pooled money, ex-spouses still on the same deed, and small investors. If you inherited your share and the family can't agree, read our guide to heirs who can't agree as well, because a different Alabama statute can apply to family-inherited land.

📜 Read the Deed First: Alabama Has Two Kinds of "Survivorship"

Pull the recorded deed from the probate office in the county where the house sits. In Alabama the probate judge's office is also the recording office, so the deed is there even if no one ever died. Then look at the words after the grantees' names. Three patterns show up again and again:

  1. "As tenants in common," or nothing at all. Each owner holds an undivided share. When an owner dies, that share goes to their heirs or the people named in their will, not to the other co-owner.
  2. "As joint tenants with right of survivorship." When one owner dies, the survivor takes the whole. But since the Alabama Supreme Court decided Nunn v. Keith in 1972, this is a common-law joint tenancy. Either owner can break it alone by deeding their share to someone else, even to a relative or a straw party. The ownership then becomes a tenancy in common and the survivorship right is gone.
  3. "For and during their joint lives, and upon the death of either, to the survivor." Language like this can be read as a tenancy in common for life with cross-contingent remainders. The Alabama Supreme Court explained in Durant v. Hamrick (1981) that the survivor's right under that kind of deed is not destructible by the act of the other owner. Neither of you can cut the other out by a side deed.

There is also a timing wrinkle. In Bernhard v. Bernhard (1965), the court had held that both joint tenants had to agree to sever. Nunn overruled it, but later decisions kept the old rule for survivorship deeds signed in the window between those two cases. If your deed dates from the late 1960s or early 1970s, have a lawyer check which rule governs it.

And one more rule from the statute itself. Ala. Code §35-4-7 says that when a joint tenant dies, their share does not pass to the other owner unless the deed states survivorship or uses words that show that intent. A deed that says only "joint tenants" does not give the survivor the house in Alabama. Plenty of couples who bought together believe otherwise.

⚠️ The quiet severance. If you hold an ordinary Alabama survivorship deed with a partner you no longer trust, your partner may be able to sign a deed to a third party and record it without telling you. Your survivorship protection disappears the moment it is recorded. If that worries you, talk to a lawyer now about what you can do, and check the probate records every few months.

✂️ What One Owner Can Do Without the Other

A co-owner in Alabama can sell, give away, or mortgage their own undivided share. They cannot sign away the whole house, and a buyer of their share gets only what they had: a fractional interest, with the same right to live there and the same right to ask for a partition.

That matters in three practical ways:

  • Your share is sellable even if your co-owner won't cooperate. There is a market for undivided interests, though it pays less than a share of a full sale because the buyer inherits the dispute.
  • A lender who takes a mortgage on one owner's share can foreclose on that share, which can leave you co-owning with a bank or an investor.
  • A judgment against your co-owner can attach to their share. Alabama's homestead exemption from creditors under Ala. Code §6-10-2 is modest and indexed to inflation, nothing like the large protections some states offer, so a co-owner's debts can become your problem faster than you'd expect.

🏛️ Asking a Court to Sell: Circuit Court or Probate Court

When co-owners can't agree, any one of them can ask a court to divide the property or to sell it and divide the money. Alabama's partition chapter, Title 35, Chapter 6, has a separate article for partition in circuit court (starting at §35-6-20) and another for partition in probate court. Circuit court is where most contested co-owner cases end up, especially when there are disputes over who paid what, whether a deed is valid, or how the proceeds should be split. A local real estate litigator will tell you which court your county's judges expect to see.

The law prefers dividing land in kind. For a single house on a city lot, that's rarely realistic, so the person asking for a sale usually shows that the property can't be fairly split and that a sale is the only way to divide its value. On acreage, a house plus a few lots, or a duplex, expect the other side to argue for a physical division instead.

Realistic timelines: an uncontested sale for division, where everyone just wants the court's help to sell, can wrap up in four to eight months. A contested case with credits, title disputes, or appeals commonly runs a year or more.

🔑 The 10-Day Rule: Your Co-Owner Can Buy You Out at Appraisal

This is the Alabama rule that surprises people who file first. Article 4A of the partition chapter (Ala. Code §§35-6-100 to 35-6-104) says that when someone petitions for a sale for division, the court shall provide for the other co-owners to buy the petitioner's interest. The process runs like this:

  1. Notice. A co-owner who wants to buy must tell the court no later than 10 days before the date set for trial. They can do it even if a default has been entered against them.
  2. Price. If the parties can't agree, the court appoints one or more real estate appraisers or commissioners, who report in writing within 30 days of appointment (§35-6-101).
  3. Payment. The buying co-owner then has 30 days to pay the appraised value into court. Once the court approves, the clerk executes the deed to the buyer (§35-6-102).
  4. If they don't pay, the court goes back to its usual practice and the case proceeds toward a sale (§35-6-103).
  5. The appraisal bill is taxed as court costs to the co-owner seeking to buy, not to you (§35-6-104).

What this means for strategy depends on which side you are on.

If you want out: filing for a sale may end with your co-owner buying your share at an appraiser's number, not with a listing on the open market. Appraisals of a dated house in an Alabama market can come in well below what an agent might have marketed it for. Before you file, get your own appraisal or broker opinion so you know whether that outcome is acceptable.

If you want to keep the house: the statute gives you a path, but only if you can actually fund it. Thirty days is not long to close a loan. Talk to a lender before the 10-day notice deadline, not after the appraisers report.

🧾 Who Paid What: Rent, Taxes, Mortgage and Repairs

Money arguments between Alabama co-owners usually fall into four buckets:

  • Living in the house. As a general rule, a co-owner in possession doesn't owe the others rent simply for living there. That changes if they've excluded the other owner (changed the locks, refused entry) or agreed to pay. Rent from a tenant is different: it belongs to all owners in proportion to their shares.
  • Taxes, insurance and the mortgage. An owner who paid more than their share of these can usually ask for credit when the proceeds are divided.
  • Improvements. A new roof that kept the house sellable is easier to get credit for than a remodeled kitchen nobody else agreed to.
  • Lawyers. Ala. Code §34-3-60 lets the court tax a reasonable attorney's fee as costs in a sale for division or a partition in kind. In Holman v. Bane (1997), the Alabama Supreme Court repeated that those fees are for work benefiting the common property, not for the controversy over each owner's rights. The fight over credits is on your own dime.

Keep a simple spreadsheet now: every tax bill, insurance premium, mortgage payment and repair, with the date and who paid it. That document is worth more in a partition accounting than any lawyer's letter.

🏷️ The Tax-Class Trap When Someone Moves Out

Alabama's property taxes are among the lowest in the country, but the way they're calculated creates a co-ownership trap. The Alabama Department of Revenue assesses single-family owner-occupied homes as Class III, at 10% of market value. Most other property, including a house no owner lives in as a residence, is Class II, at 20%.

So when the co-owner who lived in the house moves out, the assessed value can double. On a house worth $220,000 in a jurisdiction with about 60 total mills, that's roughly $1,320 a year as Class III and roughly $2,640 as Class II. Neither is huge by national standards, but the difference often arrives as a back bill after the county notices the change, and the co-owners then argue about who owes it. Millage varies widely by city and school district, so check your county revenue commissioner's numbers before relying on this example.

💍 Married, Unmarried, Divorced: No Entireties Shield in Alabama

Some states let married couples hold a home as tenants by the entirety, which blocks one spouse from selling or encumbering the house alone and protects it from one spouse's creditors. Alabama does not use that form of ownership. Married couples here usually take title as joint tenants with right of survivorship, so the rules in the first section apply to them too.

For divorcing or divorced owners, the divorce judgment usually controls: it may award the house to one spouse, order a sale, or set a deadline for a refinance. If the judgment is silent and both names stay on the deed, you're simply co-owners, and partition is available. Our guide to selling during a divorce covers the timing questions.

Unmarried partners have no divorce court to sort things out. Their only tools are the deed, any written agreement between them, and a partition case.

🧭 Your Alabama Exits, Ranked

🤝 Agree and list it

Best price

If both owners will sign a listing agreement and the house shows reasonably well, list it. Alabama's deed tax is low, at 50 cents per $500 of value under Ala. Code §40-22-1, so closing costs mostly come down to commission and repairs. This is the right answer more often than people in a fight want to admit.

💵 One owner buys the other out by agreement

Fastest if financed

Agree on a price, have the buying owner refinance, and record a deed. Base the price on an appraisal both sides pick, and account for unequal payments so nobody feels cheated later.

⚖️ File for a sale for division

Leverage, with a catch

Filing ends the stalemate, but remember the Article 4A buyout right. Your co-owner may end up owning the house at an appraised price rather than the house going to market.

📄 Sell your share alone

Exit without a lawsuit

You can sell your undivided interest to an investor who takes over the dispute. You'll get less than a share of a full-market sale, but you can be done in weeks, and you stop paying taxes and insurance on a house you can't use.

🏚️ Sell the whole house as-is for cash

When the house needs work

If both owners will sign but the house needs repairs nobody wants to pay for, a cash buyer closes without inspections or appraisal contingencies. You give up price for speed and certainty.

💵 Running the Numbers on a Huntsville-Area House

Take a three-bedroom house in Madison County that would sell for $260,000 fully updated. It needs a roof, HVAC, and cosmetic work, about $35,000 in all. Two siblings own it 50/50; one moved out last year.

  • Listing after repairs: $260,000 minus 6% commission ($15,600), minus $35,000 repairs, minus about four months of carrying costs, roughly $206,000 to split, if both siblings fund the repairs and agree on every step.
  • Cash sale as-is: a cash offer of 60–80% of repaired value would run $156,000 to $208,000. At 70%, that's $182,000, closing in about three weeks with no repairs.
  • Selling one half alone: an investor buying a disputed 50% share might pay 25–40% of the full value for that half, because they inherit the partition case.

Monthly carrying cost while you argue: property tax of roughly $110–$220 a month depending on the class and local millage, a vacant-dwelling insurance policy at roughly $100–$175 a month, utilities kept on for showings at $120–$200, and lawn and upkeep at $75–$150. Call it $400–$750 a month, before any mortgage payment. A partition case that takes a year can eat $5,000 to $9,000 that way, plus lawyers.

✅ Bottom Line

In Alabama, the deed's wording decides whether your co-owner can cut off survivorship on their own, and the Article 4A buyout statute decides whether filing for a sale actually gets you a sale. Read the deed, track the money, and know your appraised number before you file. If you both agree, list it. If you don't, you have options: a negotiated buyout, a partition case, or selling your share and walking away.

❓ Frequently Asked Questions

Can one co-owner sell an Alabama house without the other?

Not the whole house. One co-owner can sell, give away or mortgage only their own undivided share. A buyer of that share steps into the seller's shoes as a co-owner. To sell the entire property without everyone's signature, someone has to file a partition case and get a court-ordered sale.

Does a survivorship deed in Alabama stop my co-owner from selling their share?

Usually not. Since Nunn v. Keith (1972), a standard joint tenancy with right of survivorship in Alabama can be broken by one owner deeding their share to someone else, which turns the ownership into a tenancy in common. Deeds signed between the 1965 Bernhard decision and Nunn, and deeds written as a tenancy in common with cross-contingent remainders, can carry a survivorship right that one owner cannot destroy alone. Have an Alabama real estate lawyer read the exact words.

What happens if a deed just says "joint tenants" in Alabama?

Ala. Code 35-4-7 says a joint tenant's share does not pass to the other owners at death unless the deed states a right of survivorship or uses words showing that intent. Without that language, each owner's share passes through their own estate, the same as a tenancy in common.

Can my co-owner buy me out if I file for a partition sale in Alabama?

Yes. Under Ala. Code 35-6-100, when someone petitions for a sale for division, any other co-owner can ask to buy the petitioner's interest. They must notify the court at least 10 days before the trial date. If the parties don't agree on price, court-appointed appraisers report within 30 days, and the buyer then has 30 days to pay the appraised value into court.

Who pays the appraisers in an Alabama partition buyout?

Ala. Code 35-6-104 taxes the appraisers' costs as court costs to the co-owners who are seeking to buy or are buying the interest, not to the person being bought out.

Which court handles a partition case in Alabama?

Alabama's partition chapter has separate articles for partition in circuit court and partition in probate court. Many co-owner cases with title questions, money claims or disputes over credits are filed in circuit court; a lawyer will tell you which court fits your county and your facts.

Who pays the lawyers in an Alabama partition case?

Ala. Code 34-3-60 lets the court set a reasonable attorney's fee and tax it as costs in a sale for division or a partition in kind. The Alabama Supreme Court has said those fees cover work that benefits the common property, not the fight over who is entitled to what. Expect to pay your own lawyer for the contested parts.

Does the co-owner living in the house owe the other owner rent in Alabama?

Generally, a co-owner in possession does not owe rent to the others just for living there, unless they have shut the others out (ouster) or agreed to pay. Credits for taxes, insurance, mortgage payments and repairs are usually sorted out in the partition accounting.

Will property taxes go up if the co-owner who lives in the house moves out?

They can. Alabama assesses single-family owner-occupied homes at 10% of value (Class III) and most other property, including a house nobody lives in as a home, at 20% (Class II). If the occupying owner moves out, the assessment can double. Tell the revenue commissioner's office about the change rather than waiting for a back bill.

Should Alabama co-owners list the house or sell it for cash?

If the house is in decent shape and everyone will sign a listing agreement, list it; you will almost always net more. A cash sale makes sense when one owner refuses to cooperate, the house needs major work, an owner can't be found, or a partition case is coming and you want a fixed number instead of an appraisal fight.
