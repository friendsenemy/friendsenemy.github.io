Wyoming Houses After a Death, a Divorce or a Missed Payment

Wyoming gives families a shortcut that most states do not: a summary procedure that can move real estate without a full probate. Under Wyoming Statutes section 2-1-205, when a person's entire estate, including personal property, is worth no more than $400,000 after liens and encumbrances, the people entitled to it can apply for a decree of summary distribution at least 30 days after the death. For many Wyoming homes, that limit is high enough to cover the house.

How the Summary Decree Works

The application is filed with the district court in the county where the case belongs. Notice of the application must be published once a week for two consecutive weeks in a newspaper of general circulation in that county. If no one objects and the court is satisfied, it issues a decree that serves as evidence of who now owns the property, and that decree can be recorded with the county clerk so the heirs can sell.

Wyoming also has a separate affidavit, section 2-1-201, with the same $400,000 ceiling, used to collect money and personal property and to transfer vehicle titles through the county clerk. For the house itself, families and title companies usually rely on the summary decree or a regular probate.

Checklist before you apply

  • Add up everything the person owned in their own name, minus mortgages and liens, to see whether you are under $400,000.
  • Find any will and the death certificate.
  • List every heir with an address, since everyone with an interest needs to be accounted for.
  • Search the county clerk's records for a transfer on death deed.

Transfer on Death Deeds: Recorded Before Death or Not at All

Wyoming allows transfer on death deeds under section 2-18-103. The rule families most often miss is subsection (e): the deed is valid only if it was signed and recorded in the office of the county clerk where the property sits before the owner died. An unrecorded deed found among papers does not transfer the house. The beneficiary also takes the house subject to any existing mortgage or lien.

Spouses: Tenancy by the Entirety and Divorce

Wyoming recognizes tenancy by the entirety. Under section 34-1-140, a deed that names the owners as "husband and wife," "spouses" or similar language creates a tenancy by the entirety unless the deed says otherwise. When one spouse dies, the survivor generally owns the whole house. During a divorce, Wyoming courts divide property in a way they find just and equitable, and the decree should spell out who signs the deed.

Heirs Who Disagree

If several heirs end up owning a Wyoming house and one wants out, any co-owner can ask the district court for partition, and when the house cannot be split the court can order a sale. Wyoming is not among the states that have adopted the Uniform Partition of Heirs Property Act, so the general partition rules apply. Ranch families with land in several names often find that a share sale or buyout is quicker than a lawsuit. Our page on selling when heirs disagree explains the options.

Foreclosure by Power of Sale, Then Three Months

Most Wyoming mortgages contain a power of sale, so lenders can foreclose by advertisement instead of a lawsuit. Section 34-4-103 requires the lender to send written notice of intent to foreclose at least 10 days before it starts publishing the notice of sale. After the sale, section 1-18-103 gives the owner a right to redeem within three months for most property, or twelve months for agricultural real estate.

That three-month redemption window is short. An owner with equity usually does better by selling before the auction, when the full price goes toward the payoff and the rest stays with the family.

Back Taxes: Certificates and a Four-Year Window

Wyoming counties sell tax certificates on delinquent property rather than the property itself. Under section 39-13-108, the certificate holder can apply for a tax deed no sooner than four years and no later than six years from the original tax sale, and the owner can redeem before a deed issues. Delinquent taxes carry interest of 18 percent a year under the same section, so an inherited house that sits unpaid for several years becomes expensive to recover.

When the Owner Is in Jail or Prison

For a deed, Wyoming title companies generally require a power of attorney that has been notarized so it can be recorded with the county clerk. Each jail and the state prisons have their own procedures for notary access and legal mail, so call before sending papers. Our page on selling when an owner is incarcerated explains how the agent signs at closing.

Listing Versus a Direct Sale in Wyoming

If the summary decree or probate is done, the heirs agree, the house is in good shape and there is no notice of sale or tax deed application pending, a Wyoming listing agent will usually net more money than a direct buyer. A cash or partnership offer is the better fit when time or paperwork is the obstacle: an heir who will not sign, a house far from where the family lives, serious repairs, or a three-month redemption clock already running.

Quick answers for Wyoming

Can a Wyoming house pass without full probate?

Often, yes. Under section 2-1-205, if the entire estate is $400,000 or less after liens, heirs can apply to the district court for a summary distribution decree at least 30 days after death, with notice published two consecutive weeks.

Does a Wyoming transfer on death deed have to be recorded?

Yes. Section 2-18-103(e) says it is valid only if signed and recorded with the county clerk where the property is located before the owner's death.

How long is the redemption period after a Wyoming foreclosure sale?

Three months from the sale for most property and twelve months for agricultural real estate under section 1-18-103.

When can a Wyoming tax certificate holder get a tax deed?

No sooner than four years and no later than six years after the original tax sale under section 39-13-108; the owner can redeem before the deed issues.
