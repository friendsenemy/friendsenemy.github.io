Selling a New Jersey House After a Death, Split or Tax Sale

New Jersey is one of the few states that still has an inheritance tax, and it reaches the family house. A home left to a child or spouse is exempt. A home left to a brother or sister is taxed at 11 percent above the first $25,000, and a home left to a niece, nephew, cousin or friend is taxed at 15 percent and up. Until that tax is settled, it is a lien on the decedent's property for up to 15 years, which is why title companies ask for a state tax waiver before they will insure a sale.

Inheritance Tax, Waivers and Who Inherits

The tax depends on the beneficiary's relationship, not on the estate's size. Under N.J.S.A. 54:34-2, transfers to a spouse or domestic partner, children, parents and grandparents carry no tax. Siblings and sons- or daughters-in-law pay 11 percent on amounts over $25,000, rising in steps to 16 percent. Everyone else pays 15 percent on the first $700,000 and 16 percent above that.

N.J.S.A. 54:35-5 makes the tax a lien on all of the decedent's property for 15 years from death unless it is paid or bonded. In practice, the seller's attorney or the estate's tax preparer files with the Division of Taxation to get the waiver, and a sale may wait on it. If an aunt left her house to three nieces, expect the tax to come out of the sale proceeds and plan the price around it.

The County Surrogate and the Small-Estate Affidavits

Each county has a Surrogate's office that handles uncontested wills and appointments. Contested matters go to the Probate Part of the Superior Court, Chancery Division.

When there is no will, New Jersey has two affidavit shortcuts that, unlike many states, count real estate. A surviving spouse or domestic partner can use one when the total real and personal assets do not exceed $50,000 (N.J.S.A. 3B:10-3). Another heir can use one when the total does not exceed $20,000, with written consent from the other heirs (N.J.S.A. 3B:10-4). Most New Jersey houses are worth far more than those limits, so most families will need letters of administration or a probated will before selling.

A Sibling Who Will Not Sell

New Jersey enacted the Uniform Partition of Heirs Property Act in 2025. The act adds protections when inherited property is held by relatives, including a court-ordered appraisal and a chance for the family members who did not ask for a sale to buy out the ones who did. A partition suit is still a lawsuit, with its own fees and delays, and both sides usually come out ahead if they can agree first.

Tax Sale Certificates and the Choice You Must Make in Time

New Jersey sells tax sale certificates on delinquent property. A private certificate holder must wait two years from the sale before filing to foreclose the owner's right of redemption; a town that bought its own certificate can file after six months (N.J.S.A. 54:5-86). Until final judgment, the owner can redeem by paying the certificate amount, interest and costs.

The rules on surplus equity were amended in 2024. Under N.J.S.A. 54:5-87, the owner or heirs may ask, before final judgment, for a sheriff's sale or internet auction so that surplus can be recovered. If nobody makes that request, the holder can take title without a sale and the owner has no claim to the equity. Missing that step can cost a family everything above the tax debt. A negotiated sale before judgment is usually the better outcome.

Foreclosure Goes Through the Superior Court

Mortgage foreclosure in New Jersey is judicial. Under the Fair Foreclosure Act, N.J.S.A. 2A:50-56, the lender must send a notice of intention to foreclose at least 30 days, and no more than 180 days, before filing. The court process that follows often takes many months, which gives an owner with equity time to sell. See selling before the sheriff's sale for what to line up.

Spouses, Divorce and Entireties Property

Married couples who take title together in New Jersey generally hold it as tenants by the entirety, and N.J.S.A. 46:3-17.4 says neither spouse may sever or transfer their interest alone; both must consent in writing. In a divorce, the court divides marital property by equitable distribution. Until the judgment is entered, plan on both signatures for any sale.

If One Owner Is in Jail

New Jersey requires a power of attorney to be in writing, signed and acknowledged (N.J.S.A. 46:2B-8.9). For an incarcerated owner, the acknowledgment has to be arranged through the facility, and the title company should approve the form before it is signed.

When Listing Is the Smarter Move

If the house shows well, the heirs agree, the waiver is in hand and nobody is facing a tax foreclosure deadline, list it with a New Jersey agent. An updated house with owners who agree will usually net more on the open market than with any investor. We will say so.

Quick answers for New Jersey

Do I pay New Jersey inheritance tax on a house left to me by my parent?

No. Transfers to a spouse or domestic partner, children, parents and grandparents are exempt, while siblings pay 11 percent above $25,000 and most other beneficiaries pay 15 or 16 percent.

How long is New Jersey inheritance tax a lien on the house?

Under N.J.S.A. 54:35-5 the tax is a lien on the decedent's property for 15 years from death unless it is paid or bonded, which is why title companies ask for a tax waiver.

When can a New Jersey tax sale certificate holder foreclose?

A private holder must wait two years from the certificate sale, and a town that holds its own certificate can file after six months.

Can one spouse sell a New Jersey house owned as tenants by the entirety?

No. N.J.S.A. 46:3-17.4 says neither spouse may sever or transfer an entireties interest alone; both must consent in writing.
