Selling a Hard-to-Sell Connecticut House: Probate Districts, Heirs and Law Days

A Connecticut house in one person's name almost always goes through Probate Court

Connecticut gives heirs fewer shortcuts for real estate than most states. The small estate procedure covers up to $40,000 of the deceased person's solely owned personal property, and it is not available at all if the person owned any Connecticut real estate in their name alone. Connecticut also has no transfer-on-death or beneficiary deed for land. So unless the house was held jointly with survivorship or in a trust, an inherited Connecticut house goes through the Probate Court for the district where the person lived, and a court-appointed fiduciary signs the deed.

Even the small estate route runs through the court. The affidavit is filed with the Probate Court, and the court issues a decree that a bank or other holder relies on to release the money. So whether the estate is tiny or large, the local probate district is where things start.

What to do this week

  • Find the original will, if there is one, and the death certificate.
  • Look up the deed in the town clerk's land records to see how title was held.
  • Call the probate district's clerk and ask which forms start an estate.
  • Keep paying insurance and property taxes on the house while the estate is open.

Heirs who disagree: a 45-day buyout window

Connecticut adopted the Uniform Partition of Heirs Property Act, and it changes what happens when relatives co-own a house and one asks for a sale. Property qualifies as heirs' property when it is held as tenants in common with no written agreement among all owners about partition, at least one owner got title from a relative, and relatives hold a meaningful share (the statute uses 20 percent tests).

In a qualifying case, after the court sets the value, any co-owner other than the one who asked for a sale can elect, within 45 days of the court's notice, to buy that person's interest. The price is the court's value for the whole property times the seller's fractional share. The court then sets a payment date at least 60 days later. Partition cases are filed in Superior Court and take time. If one heir wants out now, read what to do when heirs disagree and our partition lawsuit explainer.

Strict foreclosure and the law day

All Connecticut foreclosures go through court. Many end in strict foreclosure: no auction at all. The court sets a law day, the deadline to redeem by paying the debt. Once every law day passes, the lender files a certificate of foreclosure in the land records and owns the house. Any equity you had is gone. You can ask the court for a foreclosure by sale instead, which can return a surplus if the sale brings more than the debts.

Questions to ask the moment you are served

  • Is the lender asking for strict foreclosure or foreclosure by sale?
  • Has a law day been set, and for which defendants?
  • Is your case eligible for mediation, and have you returned the forms?
  • How much equity is in the house compared with the debt?

Lenders must give notice of the state's foreclosure mediation program with the complaint. The program covers cases with return dates through June 30, 2029. Mediation buys time, and that time can be used to sell. See selling before foreclosure.

Tax sales: six months to redeem at 18 percent

Connecticut property taxes are collected by each town or city, and the municipal tax collector can sell a property at a tax sale. The owner then has six months to redeem by repaying the buyer the purchase price plus 18 percent a year interest. For abandoned property the redemption window can be cut to 60 days. Towns can also foreclose a tax lien in court instead. An empty inherited house can lose ground fast under those rules.

Owner in prison: two witnesses plus a notary

Under Connecticut's Uniform Power of Attorney Act (Conn. Gen. Stat. § 1-350d), a power of attorney must be dated, signed and witnessed by two witnesses. A signature acknowledged before a notary public or a commissioner of the Superior Court is presumed genuine, and a title company will want that. Inside a correctional facility, that means arranging two witnesses as well as the notary, so ask the facility first. Our guide to selling a house while incarcerated has the general steps.

Divorce and the marital home

Connecticut does not use community property. At divorce, Conn. Gen. Stat. § 46b-81 lets the Superior Court assign to either spouse all or any part of the other spouse's estate, after weighing statutory factors. Until a judgment or written agreement, plan on both spouses signing any sale.

When listing with a Connecticut agent nets more

If the house is in good repair, all owners agree, and no law day or tax sale is pending, a local Connecticut agent will usually get you more than a cash or partnership sale. Quick answers for Connecticut

Can a Connecticut small estate affidavit transfer a house?

No. Connecticut's small estate procedure covers up to $40,000 of solely owned personal property and is not available if the deceased person owned any Connecticut real estate in their name alone.

Does Connecticut allow transfer-on-death deeds?

No. Connecticut has no transfer-on-death or beneficiary deed for real estate, so a house usually passes through the Probate Court unless it was held jointly with survivorship or in a trust.

What is a law day in a Connecticut foreclosure?

In a strict foreclosure the court sets a law day, the deadline to redeem by paying the debt. Once all law days pass, the lender files a certificate of foreclosure and takes title without an auction.

How long do I have to redeem after a Connecticut tax sale?

Generally six months, by repaying the purchase price plus 18 percent a year interest; for abandoned property the window can be 60 days.
