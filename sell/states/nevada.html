Selling a Nevada Home Held as Community Property: Divorce, Death or Debt

Nevada is one of the few community property states, and that single fact drives most complicated home sales here. Property either spouse acquires during the marriage is presumed to belong to both of them equally. In a divorce, the court is told to divide it equally. At a death, how the deed was worded decides whether the house passes straight to the surviving spouse or goes through District Court.

Community property and the family home in Nevada

Under NRS 123.220, all property acquired after marriage by either spouse is community property unless a written agreement, a court decree or another statute says otherwise. NRS 123.225 says each spouse's interest is "present, existing and equal." One spouse can also give the other written power of attorney to sell or encumber community property (NRS 123.230), which helps when one spouse is deployed, ill or incarcerated.

Survivorship has to be written on the deed

Nevada lets married couples hold a house as community property with right of survivorship, so it passes to the survivor without probate. But NRS 111.064 is clear that survivorship does not arise automatically. The deed must expressly say the couple takes title as community property with a right of survivorship. A deed that just says "community property" may leave the deceased spouse's half to pass under the will or the law of intestacy.

Dividing the house in a Nevada divorce

NRS 125.150 tells the court to make, to the extent practicable, an equal disposition of the community property. A judge can order an unequal split only after finding a compelling reason and putting the reasons in writing. That is a much firmer 50/50 starting point than in equitable distribution states.

In practice, one spouse buys the other out, or the house is sold and the equity split. If you need one buyer who can pay both of you at closing, even while you are not on speaking terms, see our page on selling a house during a divorce and our longer guide, can I sell my house before, during or after divorce. When a Nevada owner dies

Nevada estates are settled in the District Court. The state offers several shortcuts based on size:

  • Small estate affidavit (NRS 146.080): up to $25,000, or $150,000 if the claimant is the surviving spouse, starting 40 days after death. It cannot be used if the decedent left any Nevada real property.
  • Set aside without administration (NRS 146.070): a court order for estates of $150,000 or less.
  • Summary administration (NRS 145.040): a faster probate when the gross estate, after encumbrances, is $500,000 or less.

Nevada also allows a deed upon death, a transfer-on-death deed added to the statutes in 2011. Under NRS 111.681 it is valid only if recorded with the county recorder before the owner dies. After the death, the beneficiary must publish and mail a notice to creditors, and the estate's creditors can still reach the property if the probate estate is not enough to pay them (NRS 111.689).

Heirs or co-owners who disagree

Nevada was the first state to adopt the Uniform Partition of Heirs Property Act, in 2011 (NRS 39.600 to 39.705). When inherited family property is the subject of a partition case, the court sets its value and lets the relatives who did not seek a sale buy out the one who did. If you would rather sell your share than litigate, read selling your share of a co-owned house.

Nevada foreclosure and the mediation program

Nevada home foreclosures usually run outside court under a deed of trust. Under NRS 107.080, the trustee records a notice of default, the owner has 35 days to cure, and at least three months must pass after the notice of default is recorded before the sale can go forward. The notice of sale is then posted for 20 days in a row and published once a week for three weeks.

For owner-occupied homes, the trustee must include information about Nevada's Foreclosure Mediation Program, run with Home Means Nevada, Inc. The homeowner can petition the district court to take part, and the sale cannot happen until a certificate shows mediation was completed or not required. That gives an owner time to sell before the auction.

Unpaid property taxes: the treasurer as trustee

Nevada does not sell tax liens to investors. Under NRS 361.570, the county treasurer holds a delinquent property in trust for two years after the first Monday in June of the year the certificate is dated, or one year if the county found it abandoned. Delinquent taxes accrue interest at 10% a year. If nobody redeems, the treasurer takes a deed and can later sell. From any excess proceeds, the county keeps the first $300 and 10% of the next $10,000; former owners must claim the rest within one year after the treasurer's deed is recorded (NRS 361.610). Selling before the deed keeps all of the equity.

Quick answers for Nevada

Is a Nevada house split 50/50 in a divorce?

Generally yes. NRS 125.150 tells the court to make an equal disposition of community property to the extent practicable, and an unequal split requires a compelling reason stated in writing.

Does community property in Nevada automatically pass to the surviving spouse?

Not always. Under NRS 111.064, survivorship applies only if the deed expressly says the couple holds title as community property with a right of survivorship.

Can a Nevada small estate affidavit be used for a house?

No. The NRS 146.080 affidavit, up to $25,000 or $150,000 for a surviving spouse, cannot be used if the decedent left any Nevada real property.

How long does a Nevada owner have to pay delinquent property taxes?

The county treasurer holds the property in trust for two years after the first Monday in June of the certificate year, or one year if it was found abandoned, before taking a deed.
