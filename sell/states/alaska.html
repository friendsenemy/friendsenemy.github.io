Selling a Troubled Alaska House: Borough Tax Foreclosure, Estates and Divorce

Unpaid property tax: the city or borough takes the house, with no auction

In most states, a house with years of unpaid taxes ends up at a public tax sale. In Alaska, the city or borough that levies the tax goes to court with a foreclosure petition, and the property transfers directly to the municipality. Nobody bids on it.

The owner is not out the door right away. The municipality must hold the property for at least one year, called the redemption period. To redeem, you pay the overdue taxes, penalties, interest and costs, plus newer taxes and assessments. The right to redeem runs out 30 days after the municipality first publishes its notice that redemption is expiring. You can generally stay in the home during the redemption period, but damaging the property can end that right.

Even after title passes, a former owner may be able to buy the property back for up to ten years, as long as the municipality has not sold it or dedicated it to a public use by ordinance. The repurchase price includes the foreclosure judgment, interest of up to 15 percent a year, back taxes and foreclosure costs. Because the tax lien comes first, a mortgage on the house is generally wiped out in the process, which is one more reason lenders and heirs pay attention to these cases.

Why heirs miss these deadlines

Alaska houses are often inherited by children who live Outside. Notices go to the old address, the house sits empty through a winter, and the first letter anyone opens is the expiring-redemption notice. If you inherited an Alaska property, call the city or borough finance office now and ask whether it is on a foreclosure list.

Mortgage default: a trustee sale on a 90-day clock

Most Alaska home loans are deeds of trust, and most foreclosures happen outside court. The trustee records a notice of default at least 30 days after the default and at least 90 days before the sale, then mails it by certified mail within ten days of recording. You can reinstate the loan any time before the sale, although a trustee can refuse if you already reinstated after two or more earlier notices.

After a nonjudicial sale there is no right to redeem unless the deed of trust itself provides one, and the lender cannot pursue you for a deficiency. Ninety days is enough time to sell if you start right away.

Estates handled through the Alaska Court System

Alaska's affidavit procedure for collecting a small estate does not reach a house. It works only when personal property other than vehicles is worth $50,000 or less, vehicles total $100,000 or less, at least 30 days have passed since the death, and there is no real estate unless it passes outside probate. A house in the deceased owner's name alone usually means an informal probate case and an appointed personal representative.

A smaller estate can use summary administration, where the personal representative does not have to give notice to creditors and can transfer property right away, then files a sworn closing statement. It is available only when the estate's value, after liens and debts, does not exceed the family allowances plus probate, funeral and last-illness costs.

Transfer-on-death deeds and the one-year wait

Alaska does allow transfer-on-death deeds. The owner signs the court system's form before a notary and records it in the recording district where the land sits before death. The property then passes outside probate. One catch: creditors have one year after the death to make claims against the property, and the court system itself warns that some buyers and title companies are cautious during that year.

Married owners: entireties title and opt-in community property

When a married couple takes title to Alaska real estate, they hold it as tenants by the entirety unless the deed says otherwise (AS 34.15.110). In a divorce, Alaska courts divide property equitably, but couples can opt into community property with a written community property agreement signed before or during the marriage. Either way, both spouses usually sign a sale until a court order or settlement says otherwise. See our page on selling a house during a divorce.

Relatives who share title

Alaska has not adopted the Uniform Partition of Heirs Property Act, so co-owners who cannot agree are left with an ordinary partition lawsuit. That costs time and legal fees on both sides. When one owner simply wants out, a buyout of one co-owner's share is often the faster route.

An owner in custody

An owner who is incarcerated still owns the house and can sign a deed or a power of attorney. The document has to be acknowledged before a notary so it can be recorded, and each facility handles notary visits its own way. Our guide on selling a house while in jail explains the usual steps.

When an Alaska agent is the better call

If the house is in good shape, the owners agree, and no redemption or trustee deadline is close, a local Alaska listing agent will usually net you more than a cash or partnership sale. Quick answers for Alaska

What happens to an Alaska house with unpaid property taxes?

The city or borough files a foreclosure petition in court and the property transfers to the municipality rather than being auctioned. The owner has a redemption period of at least one year, and the right to redeem ends 30 days after the first publication of the notice that redemption is expiring.

Can a former owner buy back a house after Alaska tax foreclosure?

Often yes, for up to ten years, as long as the municipality has not sold it or dedicated it to a public use. The price includes the foreclosure judgment, interest of up to 15 percent a year, back taxes and costs.

Does Alaska allow transfer-on-death deeds?

Yes. The owner signs the deed before a notary and records it in the recording district before death, but creditors have one year after the death to make claims, so some buyers and title companies are cautious during that year.

How long does an Alaska trustee foreclosure take?

The notice of default must be recorded at least 90 days before the sale, and you can reinstate the loan any time before the sale. There is no right to redeem after a nonjudicial sale unless the deed of trust provides one.
