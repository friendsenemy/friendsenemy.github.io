Selling a Complicated House in Alabama: Redemption Rights, Heirs and Probate

Alabama foreclosure does not end on auction day

In most states the foreclosure auction is the end of the road. Alabama is different. After a mortgage foreclosure sale, the former owner keeps a statutory right to redeem the house by paying back what the buyer paid plus allowed charges. A 2015 law (Act 2015-79, amending Ala. Code § 6-5-248) set that window at 180 days for a home that carried a homestead exemption in the tax year of the sale, and one year for other residential and commercial property. The change took effect January 1, 2016.

That right is worth something. A family that lost a house at the courthouse may still hold an interest a buyer will pay for, and the heirs of a former owner may hold it too. The same law requires the lender to send the borrower a notice of the right to redeem by certified mail at least 30 days before the foreclosure date.

If the sale has not happened yet, you have more room. Most Alabama mortgages are foreclosed outside court under a power of sale, so things move quickly once the newspaper notice runs. Selling before the sale date usually keeps more of your equity than redeeming afterward. Our general guide to selling before a foreclosure auction covers the timing.

When the owner has died: the county Probate Court

Alabama estates are handled by the Probate Court in the county where the person lived, and that county's probate judge appoints the personal representative. If the house was titled only in the deceased owner's name, a buyer's title company will normally want someone appointed before a deed is signed.

The state's summary procedure for small estates will not move the house. It covers personal property only, and an estate does not qualify if the person owned real estate that did not pass automatically at death. A 2025 bill, HB 164, raised the small-estate ceiling from an inflation-adjusted $25,000 to a figure tied to the combined homestead, exempt property and family allowances. Real estate is still left out.

Alabama also has no transfer-on-death deed for land. Families who planned ahead usually used a survivorship deed, a life estate deed or a living trust. If none of those shows up in the chain of title, expect an estate case.

Documents to pull together first

  • The death certificate and the original will, if there is one
  • A copy of the current deed and any mortgage statements
  • The most recent property tax bill from the county
  • Names and mailing addresses for every heir, including anyone who has lost touch with the family

Family land and Alabama's 2014 heirs' property law

A lot of Alabama land has passed down without a will for two or three generations. Each heir then owns an undivided share, and any co-owner can ask a court to divide or sell the whole property. Since 2014 Alabama has had the Uniform Partition of Heirs Property Act (Ala. Code Title 35, Chapter 6A). When land qualifies as heirs' property, the court sets a value, gives the other relatives a chance to buy out the person who asked for a sale, and looks at whether the land can be split before ordering it sold.

That protects families, but it takes months. When one heir wants out and the rest want to keep the land, buying that single share is often quicker than a lawsuit. Pages of Purpose can make an offer on one heir's share, or on the whole property if everyone agrees. See what to do when heirs disagree about selling a house.

Divorce: a fair split, not an automatic half

Alabama is not a community property state. Courts divide marital property under Ala. Code § 30-2-51 in a way that is fair and just, which is not always equal. Until the judge rules or the two of you sign a settlement, a house titled in both names needs both signatures to sell. A sale during the case goes smoothest when the agreement or a court order says how the money will be divided.

An owner in jail or prison

Being locked up does not take away ownership. The owner can still sign a deed or give someone a power of attorney. Under Alabama's Uniform Power of Attorney Act, the principal signs the document, and a signature acknowledged before a notary is presumed genuine (Ala. Code § 26-1A-105). A title company will expect that notarized acknowledgment so the power of attorney can be recorded with the deed. Each jail and prison has its own way of arranging a notary, so call the facility's records or legal office before mailing anything. Our guide on getting a notary inside a jail or prison explains the steps.

Back taxes: a three-year lien clock

Alabama property taxes are due October 1 and become delinquent on January 1. If they stay unpaid, the tax collector gets a probate court order and sells a tax lien. The high bidder receives a certificate, not the house. When a private party holds the certificate, the owner generally has three years from the sale to redeem by paying the amount bid plus interest and costs, and after three years the holder can seek a tax deed. When the state holds the lien, the owner can redeem until the state passes title to someone else.

Three years sounds like a long time, but interest and costs keep adding up, and an heir who lives in another state may never see the notices. If an inherited house might have back taxes, check with the county early.

Quick answers for Alabama

Can I get my Alabama house back after a foreclosure sale?

Often, yes. Alabama gives the former owner a statutory right to redeem: 180 days for a home with a homestead exemption in the tax year of the sale, and one year for other residential and commercial property, under the 2015 amendment to Ala. Code 6-5-248.

Can an Alabama small estate procedure transfer a house?

No. Alabama's summary procedure for small estates covers personal property only, and an estate does not qualify if the person owned real estate that did not pass automatically at death, so a house usually needs a case in the county Probate Court.

Does Alabama allow transfer-on-death deeds?

No. Alabama has no transfer-on-death deed for real estate, so families use survivorship deeds, life estate deeds or living trusts to avoid probate.

How long do I have to redeem an Alabama tax lien?

When a private buyer holds the tax lien certificate, the owner generally has three years from the sale to redeem by paying the amount bid plus interest and costs. After that the certificate holder can seek a tax deed.
