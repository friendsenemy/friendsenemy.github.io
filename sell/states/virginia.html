Virginia Houses Owned by Several People: Partition, Heirs and Next Steps

In Virginia, a house that belongs to several people can be forced to a decision by any one of them. Virginia Code section 8.01-81 says tenants in common, joint tenants and coparceners can compel partition, and since 2020 Virginia has also had the Uniform Partition of Heirs Property Act, which adds protections for families who inherited land together. If you share a Virginia house with siblings, cousins or an ex-partner, the question is rarely whether it can be sold, but on what terms.

Partition in Virginia: What Each Co-Owner Can Do

A partition suit is filed in the circuit court where the property sits. The court can divide land into parts or, for a single house that cannot be split, order a sale and divide the money according to each person's share after costs. Virginia's heirs property act, adopted in 2020, adds an appraisal, a right for the other owners to buy out the share of the person asking for the sale, and a preference for selling on the open market instead of at auction.

Most of the value in partition comes from the threat of it. Once everyone understands that a court can order a sale, the holdout often agrees to a buyout or a joint sale. Some co-owners prefer to sell their share outright and let the buyer deal with the others. Our page on selling your share when a co-owner won't sell explains that option, and the partition lawsuit explainer covers costs and timing.

Before you file anything

  • Get the current deed from the circuit court clerk's land records and confirm how title is held.
  • Write down each owner's share and whether anyone has paid more than their part of taxes or the mortgage.
  • Get a value opinion, so buyout talks start from a number.

When a Parent Dies: The Clerk, the Will and the Affidavit

Wills in Virginia are probated with the clerk of the circuit court in the locality where the person lived, and the clerk also qualifies executors and administrators. For very small estates, Virginia's small asset affidavit (Code section 64.2-601) lets a designated successor collect assets when the decedent's entire personal probate estate is $75,000 or less and at least 60 days have passed. The limit refers to personal property; it is not a way to sign a deed to a house.

Whether the executor can sell the house without every heir signing depends heavily on the will, so read it for a power of sale. If there is no power of sale, or no will, a title company will usually want every heir on the deed, or a court order.

Virginia has allowed transfer on death deeds since 2013, under the Real Property Transfer on Death Act (Code section 64.2-621 and following). If a parent recorded one before death, the named beneficiary may be able to sell without the estate being involved.

Spouses, Divorce and Tenancy by the Entirety

Virginia is an equitable distribution state, so a divorce court divides marital property fairly rather than automatically in half. Virginia also recognizes tenancy by the entirety for married couples, which generally means neither spouse can sell or encumber the house alone. If a divorce is pending, the sale usually needs both signatures or a court order.

An Owner in a Virginia Jail or Prison

Under Code section 64.2-1603, a power of attorney must be signed by the principal, and the signature is presumed genuine if acknowledged before a notary public. To be recorded with a deed, it must also meet Virginia's recording requirements. Because each facility handles notary visits and legal mail differently, call the jail or the Department of Corrections facility first and ask how a notary visit is arranged. Our page on selling when the owner is in jail or prison explains how a buyer works with the person holding the power of attorney.

Two Virginia Deadlines Worth Writing Down

Trustee's sale notice

Most Virginia home loans are deeds of trust, which a trustee can foreclose without a lawsuit. Code section 55.1-321 requires written notice of the sale to the owner; for owner-occupied residential property, it must be mailed at least 60 days before the sale (14 days for other deeds of trust). That 60-day notice is often the last practical moment to sell with time to close.

Back taxes and a judicial sale

Virginia localities collect delinquent real estate taxes by judicial sale rather than by selling liens to investors. Under Code section 58.1-3965, a locality may sell the property once taxes remain delinquent on December 31 following the second anniversary of the due date, or after one year for condemned, nuisance, derelict or blighted properties. Owners and heirs can usually pay the taxes before the sale.

When a Virginia Agent Will Get You More

If the co-owners agree, the house is ready to show and there is no trustee's sale or tax suit coming, list it with a Virginia agent. You will usually net more than a cash buyer pays. Direct sales make sense when the owners cannot agree, someone cannot sign, the house needs major work or a deadline is close.

Quick answers for Virginia

Can one co-owner force the sale of a house in Virginia?

Yes. Under Virginia Code section 8.01-81, co-owners can compel partition, and for a house that cannot be divided the circuit court can order a sale and split the proceeds.

Has Virginia adopted the Uniform Partition of Heirs Property Act?

Yes, in 2020. It adds an appraisal, a buyout right for the other owners and a preference for an open-market sale over an auction.

What is Virginia's small estate limit?

Virginia's small asset affidavit applies when the decedent's entire personal probate estate is $75,000 or less and at least 60 days have passed. It covers personal property, not a deed to a house.

How much notice does a Virginia homeowner get before a trustee's sale?

For owner-occupied residential property, Virginia Code section 55.1-321 requires written notice to the owner at least 60 days before the sale.
