Selling a South Carolina Family House Held as Heirs' Property

Across South Carolina, many family homes and lots have passed down for generations without a will or a probate, so the title is split among dozens of cousins. Lawyers call this heirs' property, and South Carolina has its own law for it. The Clementa C. Pinckney Uniform Partition of Heirs' Property Act, Sections 15-61-310 to 15-61-420 of the South Carolina Code, took effect on January 1, 2017. It applies to partition cases filed on or after that date, and it changes what happens when one relative goes to court to force a sale.

If your family is in that position, or if you simply inherited a house and need to sell, this page explains the South Carolina rules in plain English. How the Heirs' Property Act Protects Family Land

Under the act, the judge first holds a hearing to decide whether the property counts as heirs' property. Section 15-61-320 sets the test, which looks at whether there is no written agreement among the co-owners and how much of the property is held by relatives. If the property qualifies, several protections kick in:

  • A real appraisal. Under § 15-61-360, the court sets fair market value, and co-owners get notice and 30 days to object.
  • A chance to buy out the seller. Under § 15-61-370, relatives who did not ask for a sale can buy the shares of those who did, at the appraised value times their fraction. They tell the court at least 10 days before trial and pay within the time the court sets, at least 60 days after notice.
  • A preference for dividing the land. Under § 15-61-380, the court divides the property unless that would cause manifest prejudice to the co-owners as a group.
  • An open-market sale if it must be sold. Under § 15-61-400, the default is a listing with a licensed broker, who cannot sell below the court's value.

When the case was served by publication, the person who filed must also post a sign on the property within 10 days of the court's ruling. For a family, the practical lesson is that nobody can quietly force a fire-sale auction of an heirs' property house anymore.

Probate in the County Probate Court

South Carolina estates are opened in the probate court of the county where the person lived. A personal representative appointed by that court can deal with the house. If the estate was never opened, the heirs under the state's intestacy rules own it together, and a buyer will usually need all of them to sign or a probate to be opened first.

South Carolina law allows transfer-on-death titling for things like vehicles, mobile homes and boats, and payable-on-death bank accounts, but its probate code does not provide a transfer-on-death deed for a house. So the deed, the will or a probate usually decides who can sell.

Divorce: Equitable Apportionment

South Carolina is not a community property state. Under § 20-3-620, the court makes a final equitable apportionment of marital property, weighing 15 factors including the length of the marriage, marital fault and each spouse's contributions. Under § 20-3-630, marital property is generally what was acquired during the marriage, regardless of whose name is on the title. A sale during a divorce should follow a court order or a signed agreement.

Foreclosure and the 30-Day Bidding Window

Foreclosure in South Carolina goes through the courts, and the sale is usually conducted by the county Master-in-Equity, or by a referee appointed by the judge in counties without a master. One South Carolina rule stands out. Under § 15-39-720, when the lender asks for a deficiency judgment, bidding does not close on sale day. It stays open until the 30th day after the sale, and anyone can enter a higher bid with the required deposit during that time.

If the complaint says no deficiency judgment is demanded, the sale is final that day instead, under § 15-39-760. Either way, selling before the master's sale is how a family keeps its equity. Before that point, you can still sell, pay off the lender at closing and walk away with the difference.

Back Taxes: A 12-Month Redemption

When property taxes go unpaid, the county's delinquent tax collector can sell the property at public auction. The owner then has 12 months from the sale to redeem under § 12-51-90. Redemption costs the taxes and costs plus interest on the bid: 3 percent in the first three months, 6 percent in months four to six, 9 percent in months seven to nine and 12 percent in the last three months, capped at the opening bid amount.

If the property sells for more than the taxes owed, the extra money, often called overage, belongs to the owner of record just before the redemption period ended, under § 12-51-130. If no one claims it within five years, it goes to the county. If a family house went to tax sale, ask the county whether an overage is being held.

When the Owner or an Heir Is in Prison

An incarcerated owner can sign a power of attorney so a relative can sell for them. South Carolina's rules are stricter than many states. Under § 62-8-105, a power of attorney must be signed and witnessed with the same formality as a South Carolina will, and acknowledged or proved. Under § 62-8-109, once the principal becomes incapacitated, the agent cannot use it until it is recorded like a deed in the county where the principal lives. Ask the facility early how residents get a notary and witnesses.

Quick answers for South Carolina

What is South Carolina's heirs' property law?

The Clementa C. Pinckney Uniform Partition of Heirs' Property Act, Sections 15-61-310 to 15-61-420, took effect January 1, 2017. It requires an appraisal, gives relatives a chance to buy out co-owners who want a sale, and prefers an open-market broker sale over an auction.

Does a South Carolina foreclosure sale end on auction day?

Not always. Under Section 15-39-720, when the lender seeks a deficiency judgment, bidding stays open until the 30th day after the sale and anyone can enter a higher bid.

How long do I have to redeem after a South Carolina tax sale?

The owner has 12 months from the tax sale to redeem under Section 12-51-90. Interest on the bid runs 3, 6, 9 or 12 percent depending on the quarter of the redemption year, capped at the opening bid amount.

How must a South Carolina power of attorney be signed?

It must be signed and witnessed with the same formality as a South Carolina will and acknowledged or proved. After the principal becomes incapacitated, the agent cannot use it until it is recorded like a deed.
