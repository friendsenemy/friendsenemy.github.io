Selling a Tennessee House Before a Tax Sale, Trustee Sale or Family Split

In Tennessee, how long you have to get a house back after a tax sale depends on how far behind the taxes were. Under T.C.A. § 67-5-2701, the usual redemption period is one year from the court order confirming the sale. But if the taxes were delinquent more than five years, it drops to 180 days, and at eight years or more it drops to 90 days. For a house the court finds vacant or abandoned, it can be as short as 30 days. Redeeming also costs interest at 12 percent a year on the full purchase price.

Many inherited Tennessee houses sit empty with taxes piling up while the family sorts things out, which is exactly the situation that shortens those windows. The Sliding Redemption Scale After a Tax Sale

Tennessee collects delinquent property taxes through a court lawsuit that ends in a sale. After the sale, the period to redeem is set by the length of the delinquency:

SituationTime to redeem after the order confirming the sale
Taxes delinquent five years or lessOne year
More than five but less than eight years180 days
Eight years or more90 days
Property shown to be vacant or abandoned30 days

No matter which row applies, the right to redeem cannot be used more than one year after the confirming order. The 12 percent interest runs on the purchase price from the day the buyer paid the clerk until the motion to redeem is filed. For a house worth far more than the taxes owed, selling before the sale is almost always better than redeeming after it.

Tennessee Estates and Who Signs the Deed

Under T.C.A. § 16-16-201, the chancery court has exclusive jurisdiction over probate in most Tennessee counties. Some large counties have their own probate courts: Shelby County has the Shelby County Probate Court, and Davidson County estates go to the Seventh Circuit Court, Probate Division.

Small estates do not cover the house

Tennessee's small estate procedure applies when probate property is worth $50,000 or less, and the statute defines property as personal property only, so real estate is left out. Tennessee's code also has no transfer-on-death deed. That leaves two common ways a house gets sold: every heir signs the deed, or a personal representative with authority from a will or the court signs. Tennessee's Heirs' Property Buyout

When relatives own a house together and one wants to force a sale, Tennessee's Uniform Partition of Heirs Property Act applies. It is Part 3 of the state's partition chapter, T.C.A. §§ 29-27-301 to 29-27-313. The court determines the value, then gives the relatives who did not ask for a sale the right to buy out those who did.

Under § 29-27-307, a relative who wants to buy has 45 days after the notice, or a period the court sets, to file an election. The price is the court's value of the whole property times the share being bought. The court then sets a payment date at least 60 days after notice. If the buyout does not happen, the act prefers an open-market sale over an auction.

Many families settle before any of that. Often one heir sells their share, or the heirs sell together and split the money. Our page on selling when heirs cannot agree explains a share purchase.

Married Owners and Divorce

Tennessee recognizes tenancy by the entirety for married couples, so when one spouse dies the survivor owns the house and can sell with a death certificate. In a divorce, T.C.A. § 36-4-121 directs the court to divide marital property equitably, without regard to marital fault. Tennessee is not a community property state, so the split is not automatically equal.

Trustee Sales Without a Lawsuit

Most Tennessee home loans use a deed of trust, and foreclosure happens outside court. Under T.C.A. § 35-5-101, the notice of sale is published in a newspaper in the county, with the first publication at least 20 days before the sale. A copy must be mailed to the borrower by registered or certified mail no later than the first publication.

That can leave only a few weeks from the first notice to the sale. Tennessee law, under § 66-8-101, does give a two-year right to redeem after a foreclosure sale, but it is lost if the deed of trust expressly waives it, so check your loan papers. If a notice has been published, start working on a sale right away.

An Owner or Heir in Jail or Prison

An owner in custody can still sell through a power of attorney naming a family member. For a deed signed by an agent to be accepted and recorded with the county register of deeds, plan on the power of attorney being signed before a notary. Facilities handle notary access differently, so contact the facility early. If an heir is in custody, they still need to sign or be represented for the deed to be clean.

Quick answers for Tennessee

How long do I have to redeem a Tennessee house after a tax sale?

Usually one year from the order confirming the sale, but 180 days if taxes were delinquent more than five years, 90 days at eight years or more, and 30 days for property found vacant or abandoned. Redemption carries 12 percent annual interest on the purchase price.

Which court handles probate in Tennessee?

The chancery court handles probate in most Tennessee counties under T.C.A. 16-16-201. Shelby County has its own probate court, and Davidson County estates go to the Seventh Circuit Court, Probate Division.

Does Tennessee have an heirs' property partition law?

Yes. Tennessee's Uniform Partition of Heirs Property Act, T.C.A. 29-27-301 to 29-27-313, lets relatives who did not ask for a sale buy out those who did at the court-determined value.

How much notice is required before a Tennessee foreclosure sale?

The first newspaper publication must be at least 20 days before the sale, and a copy must be mailed to the borrower by registered or certified mail no later than the first publication.
