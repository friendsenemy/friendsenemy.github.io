Selling an Inherited or Stuck House in Vermont, Town by Town

Vermont's small estate process will not move a house. Under 14 V.S.A. section 1901, a small estate is one worth no more than $45,000 that consists entirely of personal property, with a narrow exception for a time-share. Once the person who died owned a home in their own name, the estate goes through the Probate Division of the Superior Court, and a sale usually needs that court's permission.

The House Is Not a Small Estate in Vermont

Families often assume a modest farmhouse or camp qualifies for the short route because its value is low. It does not, because the $45,000 small estate rule is limited to personal property. The usual path is a regular estate: someone files the will (if there is one), asks to be appointed executor or administrator, and gives notice to the heirs.

License to sell

Vermont's statutes give the Probate Division of the Superior Court power to order the sale of real estate in an estate, and to grant a license to sell when a sale is necessary or beneficial, for example to pay debts or to divide the estate fairly. The executor asks for the license, the court reviews it and interested people get notice. A buyer's title company will want to see that license in the file before closing.

What to gather first

  • The original will and the death certificate.
  • The last tax bill from the town where the house sits; Vermont taxes are billed by towns and cities.
  • Names and addresses of every heir, including children of a child who died earlier.
  • Any deed the person signed in later life, especially an enhanced life estate deed.

Enhanced Life Estate Deeds Instead of TOD Deeds

Many states use transfer on death deeds. Vermont's version is the enhanced life estate deed, often called a Lady Bird deed, governed by the Enhanced Life Estate Deed Act passed in the 2019–2020 legislative session (27 V.S.A. chapter 6). If a parent signed and recorded one, the house may pass to the named people at death without the Probate Division. Look in the town land records, which are kept by the town clerk, for a deed in the parent's name dated after the original purchase.

Back Taxes: The Town Collector and a One-Year Redemption

Vermont tax sales are local. The town's collector of delinquent taxes can sell land for unpaid taxes, and under 32 V.S.A. section 5260 the owner, a mortgage holder or a lien holder can redeem within one year from the day of sale by paying the sale price plus interest at one percent per month. If nobody redeems in that year, the buyer can get a deed.

That one-year window is shorter than many families expect, and it often runs while an estate is still open. If an inherited Vermont house has unpaid taxes, call the town clerk or collector and ask for the payoff and whether a sale has been scheduled.

Foreclosure Through the Court, With Room for a Sale

Vermont mortgage foreclosures go through the Superior Court. The court may order a judicial sale, or in some cases a strict foreclosure that transfers the house to the lender without a sale; by statute, strict foreclosure requires a finding that there is no substantial value above what is owed. For an owner-occupied home sold by judicial sale, the redemption period is six months from the decree unless the court orders a shorter time. Notice of the sale must be published once a week for three weeks, the first at least 21 days before the sale.

Those months between the decree and the sale are when an owner with equity can still sell and pay off the loan. Our page on selling before a foreclosure auction explains how that payoff works.

Heirs Who Cannot Agree

When several heirs end up owning a Vermont house together, any one of them can ask the court for partition. Vermont is not among the states that have adopted the Uniform Partition of Heirs Property Act, so the general partition rules apply, and the court can divide the land or order a sale. Partition is slow and costs every side money, which is why many families settle once someone puts an actual price on one heir's share. Our page on buying houses when heirs disagree explains how a share purchase can end a standoff, and what happens to a house when someone dies without a will covers who the heirs are.

An Owner Who Is Incarcerated

Vermont adopted the Uniform Power of Attorney Act in 2023 (14 V.S.A. chapter 127). A power of attorney is signed by the owner, and the signature is presumed genuine if the owner acknowledges it before a notary public. For a deed, title companies expect that acknowledgment, so plan for a notary. Ask the facility how notary visits and legal mail are arranged before you send anything.

Divorce in Vermont

Vermont family courts divide property equitably rather than by community property rules. If the house is being sold during the case, the final order should say who signs the deed and how the proceeds are split.

Listing Is Better When Nothing Is Stuck

If the estate is open, the license to sell is in hand, the house is in decent shape and the heirs agree, a Vermont listing agent will usually get you more money than we would pay. Do that. A cash or partnership sale is the better fit when the estate has stalled, a tax sale has already happened, the house needs a new roof or septic, or an heir cannot be found.

Quick answers for Vermont

Can a Vermont small estate include a house?

No. Under 14 V.S.A. section 1901, a small estate must be worth $45,000 or less and consist entirely of personal property, so an estate that includes a house goes through regular administration in the Probate Division.

Does Vermont allow transfer on death deeds?

Vermont uses the enhanced life estate deed, sometimes called a Lady Bird deed, under 27 V.S.A. chapter 6, which can pass a house at death without the Probate Division if it was signed and recorded.

How long do I have to redeem a Vermont house sold at a tax sale?

One year from the day of sale under 32 V.S.A. section 5260, by paying the sale price plus interest at one percent per month to the collector or town clerk.

What is the redemption period in a Vermont home foreclosure?

For an owner-occupied home in a judicial sale foreclosure, six months from the decree unless the court orders a shorter time.
