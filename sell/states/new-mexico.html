New Mexico Community Property: Selling When One Spouse Can't Sign

In New Mexico, a married couple's house is usually community property, and state law says both spouses must join in any sale, deed or mortgage of community real estate (NMSA 40-3-13). That one rule is behind most of the stuck sales we see here. When a husband is in prison, a wife has moved out of state and stopped answering, or one spouse has died, the house cannot simply be sold by the person who still lives in it.

Both Signatures, or a Recorded Power of Attorney

The same statute offers the main way around the problem. A spouse can act alone if the other spouse has signed a valid power of attorney and it has been recorded. A non-joining spouse can also ratify a deed in writing afterward. There are narrow exceptions, such as purchase-money mortgages and a deed from one spouse directly to the other.

For a spouse who is incarcerated, the practical steps are getting a power of attorney signed in front of a notary inside the facility, recording it with the county clerk, and only then signing the sale documents. Prison notary access varies by facility, so call ahead. Our page for houses where an owner is in jail or prison walks through how we coordinate it, and getting a notary inside a jail or prison covers the paperwork.

Divorce Under Community Property Rules

Because New Mexico is a community property state, the starting point in a divorce is that property acquired during the marriage belongs to both spouses. The joinder rule still applies while the case is pending, so a sale needs both signatures or a court order. Many couples agree to sell and split the proceeds rather than fight over who keeps the house.

When a Spouse Dies: The $500,000 Homestead Affidavit

New Mexico has a shortcut many families never hear about. Under NMSA 45-3-1205, a surviving spouse can take title to the couple's home by affidavit, without probate, when all of these are true:

  • The home was community property and the couple was married at death.
  • The spouse died without a will, or the will left the home to the survivor.
  • At least six months have passed since the death.
  • The home's full assessed value does not exceed $500,000.
  • No probate case is pending and the listed debts and expenses have been paid.

The affidavit is recorded with the county clerk where the home is located. Once it is recorded, the surviving spouse can sell alone. The limit was raised from $100,000 to $500,000 in 2011.

Probate Court, District Court and Small Estates

New Mexico splits estate work between two courts. Under NMSA 45-1-302, the county probate court and the district court both handle informal probate of a will and appointment of a personal representative, while formal proceedings, including contested ones, belong only to the district court.

For estates with no house involved, a small-estate affidavit can collect personal property 30 days after death when the estate is worth $50,000 or less (NMSA 45-3-1201). That route does not transfer real estate, so a house left to children normally needs a personal representative appointed first.

New Mexico also allows transfer-on-death deeds under its Uniform Real Property Transfer on Death Act. Check the county clerk's records for one before opening probate.

Heirs Property Protections Since 2017

New Mexico adopted the Uniform Partition of Heirs Property Act in 2017. When relatives inherit a house together and one of them files to force a sale, the act gives the others an appraisal and a chance to buy out that relative before a sale is ordered. This keeps family land from being sold cheaply at a courthouse auction, but it also means a co-heir cannot quickly force a sale. Negotiated buyouts often work better. Read whether all heirs must agree to sell for the general picture.

Foreclosure and the Nine-Month Redemption

New Mexico foreclosures go through the district court. After a foreclosure sale, NMSA 39-5-18 gives the former owner nine months to redeem. The New Mexico Supreme Court has recognized that a mortgage can shorten that period to as little as one month by written agreement, so read the mortgage. If you have equity, selling before the court sale beats trying to redeem after it.

Delinquent Property Taxes

In New Mexico, delinquent property is sold by the state Taxation and Revenue Department. Under NMSA 7-38-65, the department may sell a property any time after three years from the first date it appeared on the delinquency list. An heir who inherits a house with several years of unpaid taxes may have far less time than they think.

First Steps for a New Mexico Family

  1. Find out how title is held: both spouses, one spouse, or a trust.
  2. If a spouse is unavailable, plan for a recorded power of attorney or ratification.
  3. If a spouse has died, count six months and check whether the $500,000 homestead affidavit fits.
  4. Ask the county treasurer how many years of taxes are unpaid.

Quick answers for New Mexico

Do both spouses have to sign to sell a house in New Mexico?

For community real property, yes. NMSA 40-3-13 requires both spouses to join, unless one acts under a recorded power of attorney or the other later ratifies in writing.

Can a surviving spouse in New Mexico get the house without probate?

Often. Six months after the death, a surviving spouse can record an affidavit for a community-property home with a full assessed value of $500,000 or less if the conditions in NMSA 45-3-1205 are met.

How long can an owner redeem after a New Mexico foreclosure sale?

Nine months under NMSA 39-5-18, although a mortgage can shorten that to as little as one month by written agreement.

When can New Mexico sell a house for unpaid property taxes?

The Taxation and Revenue Department may sell the property any time after three years from the first date it appeared on the delinquency list.
