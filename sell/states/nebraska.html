Nebraska Inherited House? Affidavit, County Court Probate or a Quick Sale

Nebraska gives heirs a shortcut most states do not: a small estate affidavit made specifically for real estate. If the person who died owned Nebraska real property with a total assessed value of $100,000 or less, the heirs may be able to transfer the house by recording an affidavit with the county register of deeds, with no probate case at all. Many modest Nebraska homes fall under that line, so check it before you hire anyone.

The real estate affidavit at the register of deeds

Under Nebraska Revised Statutes section 30-24,129, the affidavit works when:

  • The decedent's interest in all Nebraska real property is $100,000 or less, measured by the assessment rolls for the year of death, minus any real estate taxes and interest owed at death.
  • At least 30 days have passed since the death, shown by an attached certified copy of the death certificate.
  • Every person claiming as a successor signs it (or someone legally acting for them).

It is filed with the register of deeds in a county where the property is located, and recorded in any other Nebraska county where the decedent owned land. The section was most recently amended in 2024 (LB 1195). Once it is recorded, the heirs who signed can sell together.

The catch is that last bullet. If one sibling will not sign, or one heir has died and left children of their own, the affidavit stalls and the family is back to County Court.

Personal property has its own affidavit

A separate affidavit under section 30-24,125 covers bank accounts, vehicles and other personal property when their total value is $100,000 or less, again 30 days after death.

When the estate goes to County Court

In Nebraska, the County Court, not a district or probate court, has exclusive original jurisdiction over decedents' estates (section 24-517). Larger estates, estates with disputes, and houses above the affidavit limit go there for informal or formal probate. Once a personal representative is appointed, they can sell the house and distribute what is left. See selling an inherited or probate home for how a sale fits into that process.

Some owners avoided all of this with a transfer-on-death deed, which Nebraska has allowed since 2012 (LB 536). Section 76-3409 requires it to be signed by the owner, or by someone in the owner's presence. Look for one in the register of deeds index before filing anything.

An owner who is incarcerated or cannot travel

Nebraska is strict about powers of attorney. Under section 30-4005, a power of attorney under the Nebraska Uniform Power of Attorney Act is not valid unless it is acknowledged before a notary public. A handwritten authorization mailed out of a jail or prison will not let a relative sell the house; the notary step has to happen. Families should ask the facility how inmates get documents notarized and allow time for it. Our page on selling a house when the owner is in jail or prison walks through the process, and our guide to getting a notary in jail covers the practical details.

Co-owners, partition and divorce

When co-owners cannot agree, any of them can ask the district court for a partition, which ends in a division or a court-ordered sale. Nebraska has not adopted the Uniform Partition of Heirs Property Act, so the extra buyout protections found in many states do not apply here. That makes a negotiated sale or a buyout of one owner's share more attractive than a lawsuit for most families. Our partition lawsuit explainer covers the general process and costs.

In a divorce, Nebraska courts divide the marital estate equitably under section 42-365. There is no fixed formula; court decisions describe a general range of one-third to one-half of the marital estate to each spouse, guided by fairness and the facts of the case. For estate, divorce or criminal-case questions, speak with an attorney licensed in Nebraska; Pages of Purpose LLC is not a law firm.

Trust deed foreclosure in Nebraska

Most Nebraska home loans are trust deeds, foreclosed outside court. The trustee first records a notice of default with the register of deeds. At least one month later (two months for some farm property), the trustee can give notice of sale, which must be published once a week for five consecutive weeks, with the last publication 10 to 30 days before the sale (sections 76-1006 and 76-1007). From the notice of default to the sale is roughly two to three months at minimum, a short window to sell and keep the equity.

Tax certificates and the three-year mark

Nebraska counties sell tax sale certificates on delinquent property. Delinquent taxes carry interest at 14% a year (section 45-104.01). The owner can redeem until the certificate holder applies for a tax deed, and under section 77-1837 that application comes within nine months after three years from the date of sale. A treasurer's tax deed is only available for lower-value parcels; above that threshold, the holder must foreclose the lien in court. Either way, selling before the three-year mark lets the payoff come out of the sale.

Quick answers for Nebraska

Can heirs transfer a Nebraska house without probate?

Often, yes. If the decedent's Nebraska real property has a total assessed value of $100,000 or less, the heirs can record an affidavit under section 30-24,129 with the register of deeds 30 days after the death, signed by every successor.

Which court handles probate in Nebraska?

The County Court has exclusive original jurisdiction over decedents' estates in Nebraska under section 24-517.

Does a Nebraska power of attorney have to be notarized?

Yes. Under section 30-4005, a power of attorney under the Nebraska Uniform Power of Attorney Act is not valid unless it is acknowledged before a notary public.

How long until a Nebraska tax certificate holder can get a deed?

Under section 77-1837, the holder applies within nine months after three years from the date of sale, and the owner can redeem until that application is filed.
