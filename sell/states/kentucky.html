Kentucky Houses Behind on Taxes, Stuck With Heirs or Facing a Commissioner Sale

In Kentucky, unpaid property tax does not just sit on the county's books. The county clerk can sell the bill as a certificate of delinquency, often to a private third-party purchaser. Under KRS 134.125 that certificate earns 12% simple interest a year, and under KRS 134.546 a lawsuit to collect on it can be filed once a year has passed since the taxes became delinquent. For a family dealing with a parent's house, or an owner who has fallen behind, that one-year mark is the clock to watch.

Kentucky certificates of delinquency: what the buyer can do

A third-party purchaser who buys the certificate steps into the county's lien. Kentucky law lets that purchaser add certain fees, including attorney's fees, within limits the statutes set, and requires notices to the owner. The collection suit can be brought any time from one year after delinquency up to eleven years after. If it goes to judgment, the court can order the property sold.

What that means in plain terms:

  • The amount owed keeps growing at 12% a year, plus allowed fees, until it is paid.
  • If more than one year goes unpaid, there can be more than one certificate on the same house.
  • Selling the house pays every certificate off at closing, from the sale money, so the family keeps what is left.

Get a written payoff for every certificate from the county clerk before you agree to any price. Those payoffs, plus any mortgage, are what a buyer has to clear.

Kentucky estates start in District Court

Kentucky handles probate in District Court in the county where the person lived. Two shortcuts matter for small estates:

  • Spouse's exemption. A surviving spouse is entitled to a $30,000 exemption (KRS 391.030). Under KRS 395.455 the court can dispense with administration when that exemption equals or exceeds the probate assets.
  • Agreement to dispense with administration. Under KRS 395.470, when there are no debts and everyone entitled to the personal estate agrees in writing, the family can file the agreement in District Court, advertise for creditors and post a bond.

Both procedures are written around personal property and the spouse's exemption. Whether a title company will insure a house sale off one of them is a question for a Kentucky probate attorney. Most inherited houses in Kentucky are sold through an appointed executor or administrator. Our page on selling an inherited or probate house explains how that sale works.

When heirs or co-owners pull in different directions

Kentucky gives co-owners two court paths. Under KRS 381.135, an owner can ask the circuit court to divide land, and three commissioners allot each person's share. Under KRS 389A.030, the circuit court can order the property sold instead. For a sale, the law presumes the property cannot be divided unless someone raises the issue, and the court must order division only if it can be done without materially impairing anyone's interest. A master commissioner usually conducts the public sale.

Kentucky has not adopted the Uniform Partition of Heirs Property Act. A 2026 Senate bill to adopt it never moved past committee referral, and a draft for the 2027 session is in the works. For now, a single heir can push a sale, which is why many families settle with a buyout instead. See options when heirs disagree.

Married owners: tenancy by the entirety

Kentucky recognizes tenancy by the entirety for married couples. The partition sale statute specifically excludes tenants by the entirety in a home they occupy as their principal residence, so one spouse cannot use partition to force the sale of the couple's home.

Divorce in Kentucky: "just proportions"

Under KRS 403.190, the court divides marital property in just proportions, without regard to misconduct. It considers each spouse's contribution, including homemaking, the length of the marriage and each spouse's economic circumstances. Property received by gift or inheritance stays separate, unless the couple's efforts increased its value. A house inherited from one spouse's parents is often not fully marital.

Owner in jail or prison in Kentucky

A power of attorney is how most incarcerated owners sell. Under KRS 457.050, the owner signs, or someone signs in the owner's conscious presence, and a signature acknowledged before a notary is presumed genuine. Title companies and county clerks expect that acknowledgment for a deed. Ask the facility how inmates get legal documents notarized before you mail anything. Our guide on getting a notary in jail covers what to ask.

Foreclosure: the master commissioner sale and the two-thirds rule

Kentucky foreclosures go through circuit court and usually end in a master commissioner's sale. Before the sale, the property is appraised. Under KRS 426.530, if the house sells for less than two-thirds of its appraised value, the owner may redeem it within six months of the sale. Redeeming takes the full price plus costs, so selling before the commissioner's sale is usually the way to protect any equity.

When a Kentucky listing beats a direct sale

If the taxes are current or can be paid at closing, the house needs little work, every owner is on board, and there is no court date coming, list with a Kentucky real estate agent. You will very likely net more on the open market. A cash or partnership sale earns its place when certificates are stacking up, heirs are split, or repairs would scare off buyers who need a loan.

Quick answers for Kentucky

What interest do Kentucky tax certificates of delinquency earn?

Under KRS 134.125, a certificate of delinquency bears 12% simple interest per year, and a collection suit can be filed once a year has passed since the taxes became delinquent.

Which court handles probate in Kentucky?

Kentucky estates are handled in District Court in the county where the person lived, and a surviving spouse is entitled to a $30,000 exemption.

Has Kentucky adopted the Uniform Partition of Heirs Property Act?

No. A 2026 Senate bill to adopt it did not move past committee referral, so partition is governed by KRS 381.135 and KRS 389A.030.

Is there a right of redemption after a Kentucky foreclosure sale?

Yes, if the house sells for less than two-thirds of its appraised value, the owner may redeem within six months of the sale under KRS 426.530.
