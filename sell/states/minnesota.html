Minnesota House Behind on Taxes, Stuck in Probate, or Owned by Feuding Heirs

The most important property-tax case in the country in recent years came out of Minnesota. In Tyler v. Hennepin County (2023), the U.S. Supreme Court held that a county could not sell a tax-delinquent home and keep the surplus beyond what was owed. For Minnesota families sitting on a house with unpaid taxes, the lesson is simple: the equity is yours, but the surest way to keep it is to sell before the county ends up with the deed.

Tax forfeiture in Minnesota after the Tyler decision

Minnesota does not sell tax liens to investors the way many states do. Unpaid property taxes go to a tax judgment, the land is sold to the State of Minnesota, and the owner gets a period of redemption. Under Minnesota Statutes section 281.17, that period is generally three years. It can be as short as one year for non-homestead property in a "targeted community." If the taxes are not paid by the end of the period, the property forfeits to the state and the county manages it.

Ms. Tyler's case changed the end of that story. In response, the Legislature passed a law that took effect May 19, 2024, setting aside $109 million for settlement payments tied to past forfeitures and requiring counties to share sale proceeds from older forfeited parcels. The practical point for a homeowner today: do not count on a surplus check. Selling during the redemption period lets you pay the county directly at closing and walk away with the rest.

Signs your redemption window is closing

  • You have received a notice of expiration of redemption from the county.
  • Two or more years of taxes are unpaid on a house nobody lives in.
  • The owner died and nobody has opened probate, so nobody has authority to pay or sell.

Inherited Minnesota homes go through District Court

Minnesota has no separate probate court. Estates are handled by the District Court in the county where the person lived, either informally through the probate registrar or formally before a judge.

The affidavit shortcut is narrow. Under section 524.3-1201, an affidavit for collecting personal property works only when the whole probate estate is $75,000 or less and 30 days have passed since death. It is written for bank accounts, vehicles and similar personal property, so it is not the tool for transferring a house. Minnesota also has a summary procedure for small estates (section 524.3-1203), which lets the court close an estate without further notice when its gross probate value, not counting the exempt homestead and exempt property, is $150,000 or less.

A transfer-on-death deed can avoid all of this. Minnesota has allowed them for years under section 507.071, and the deed works only if it was recorded in the county before the owner died. Before you open probate, have the county recorder's index checked for one.

Heirs and co-owners who will not agree

When siblings inherit a Minnesota house together and one refuses to sell, the remedy is a partition action in District Court. Minnesota has not adopted the Uniform Partition of Heirs Property Act, so there is no built-in buyout step for heirs. The court can divide the land or, for a single-family house, usually order it sold, with attorney fees and court costs coming out of everyone's share.

A quicker route is often for the heir who wants out to sell their share, or for all of them to sell to one buyer who sorts out the title. See selling your share when a co-owner won't sell.

Mortgage foreclosure: six months, then six more

Most Minnesota home foreclosures are "foreclosure by advertisement," handled outside court and ending in a sheriff's sale. According to Lutheran Social Service of Minnesota's financial counselors, the process typically runs about six months up to the sheriff's sale, followed by a six-month redemption period. Owners can ask to postpone the sale (the request must be made at least 15 days before it); that pushes the sale back five months but shrinks the redemption period to five weeks.

You are allowed to sell the house at any point before the sheriff's sale and through the end of the redemption period. That year-long runway is one of the longest in the country and gives a family real time to close a sale and keep the equity.

Divorce, and owners who cannot sign in person

Minnesota courts make a "just and equitable division" of marital property (section 518.58). The law presumes each spouse made a substantial contribution to what was built during the marriage, but it does not require a 50/50 split. While the case is open, both spouses normally have to sign any sale.

If an owner is in jail, in a nursing home, or out of state, a Minnesota statutory short form power of attorney (section 523.23) only works if the principal's signature is acknowledged before a notary. Plan the notary visit first, then the closing. Our divorce sale page explains how one buyer can pay both spouses at closing. Is listing with an agent the better move?

Often, yes. Quick answers for Minnesota

How long do I have to redeem a Minnesota house sold for back taxes?

Under Minnesota Statutes section 281.17 the redemption period is generally three years, and it can be one year for non-homestead property in a targeted community.

Can a Minnesota small estate affidavit transfer a house?

No. The affidavit under section 524.3-1201 is for collecting personal property when the probate estate is $75,000 or less, so a house usually needs probate, summary proceedings or a recorded transfer-on-death deed.

How long is the redemption period after a Minnesota sheriff's sale?

It is usually six months, or five weeks if the owner postponed the sale. The house can be sold at any point before the sale and through the end of the redemption period.

Did Tyler v. Hennepin County change Minnesota tax forfeiture?

Yes. In 2023 the U.S. Supreme Court held that a county could not keep the surplus from selling a tax-forfeited home, and a Minnesota law effective May 19, 2024 set aside $109 million for related settlements.
