West Virginia Homes With Back Taxes, Open Estates or Several Heirs

In West Virginia, a house with unpaid property taxes ends up in the hands of the State Auditor, not just the county sheriff. Each year the sheriff publishes a list of delinquent lands, and tracts still unpaid are certified to the Auditor, who sells the tax liens at public auction. The owner or anyone entitled to pay the taxes can still redeem until a tax deed is issued, but the cost grows with every step, so this is the first thing to check on an inherited or vacant West Virginia house.

How a West Virginia Tax Lien Moves From Sheriff to Auditor

The statute sets out the order of events. By September 10 the sheriff prepares a second list of real estate still delinquent on September 1, with a notice that unpaid tax liens will be certified to the Auditor on October 31. Before certification, you can redeem by paying the sheriff. After certification, you pay the Auditor.

Once a lien is sold, the buyer has to ask the Auditor to send a notice to redeem to the owner and others with an interest. That notice gives a date after which a deed can be issued. Under West Virginia Code section 11A-3-56, redeeming means paying the taxes and charges due at the sale with interest at one percent per month, plus later taxes the buyer paid, notice costs and certain title-examination expenses (capped at $500 before interest). If the house is your primary residence, the notice states that you may ask the Auditor to let you redeem in up to three installments before the deed issues.

Steps for a family facing a notice to redeem

  • Read the redemption deadline printed on the notice and put it on your calendar.
  • Call the Auditor's office for the exact payoff; do not guess from the original tax bill.
  • If no one in the family can pay, decide quickly whether to sell before the deed is issued, while the equity is still yours.

Estates Run Through the County Commission

West Virginia handles probate administration through the county commission and the clerk of the county commission, not a separate probate court. After the personal representative files an appraisement, the estate is normally referred to a fiduciary commissioner to handle claims. Code section 44-2-1 creates a shortcut: if the appraisement shows assets of $200,000 or less (not counting real estate specifically left to someone in a will and nonprobate assets), or there is a single competent beneficiary, the clerk records the appraisement and the estate can avoid that referral unless a creditor's claim is disputed.

Some counties have adopted an optional fiduciary supervisor system, which changes who reviews the paperwork. Ask the clerk of the county commission which system your county uses.

West Virginia also adopted the Uniform Real Property Transfer on Death Act (Code chapter 36, article 12) in 2014. A recorded transfer on death deed can move the house to the named beneficiary without the estate. Our page on inherited and probate houses explains where a buyer fits in.

Heirs in Different States

Many West Virginia houses belong to heirs who moved away years ago. When one of them wants to sell and another refuses, a co-owner can ask the circuit court for partition, and the court can order a sale when the house cannot be divided fairly. West Virginia has not adopted the Uniform Partition of Heirs Property Act, so the general partition statutes govern. Families often find a share purchase or a buyout cheaper than litigation. Our page on selling when heirs disagree walks through those choices, and do all heirs have to agree to sell answers the most common question.

Divorce: Equal Division by Default

West Virginia Code section 48-7-101 says that on divorce the court divides marital property equally between the parties, unless another part of the statute or the parties' agreement calls for something else. For a jointly owned house, that usually means a sale or a buyout at a value both sides accept.

When the Owner Is Incarcerated

West Virginia's power of attorney statute, Code section 39B-1-105, says a power of attorney must be signed by the principal and must be acknowledged before a notary public or other person authorized to take acknowledgments. Without that notarization it is not valid for a deed. Ask the regional jail or prison how notary visits are scheduled before mailing anything.

Trustee's Sales on Deeds of Trust

West Virginia home loans are usually secured by deeds of trust, which a trustee can foreclose without a lawsuit. Code section 38-1-4 requires the trustee to publish the notice of sale as a Class II legal advertisement in the county where the property sits and to send a copy to the borrower by certified mail. When that notice arrives, the sale is close, and selling or refinancing before the auction is the main way to keep the equity.

A Local Listing Can Be the Better Answer

If taxes are current, the estate is settled or the owners agree, and the house is in shape to show, a West Virginia listing agent will usually net you more than any direct buyer. A cash or partnership offer is a better match when there is a tax lien already sold, an heir who will not answer, a house that needs a new roof or foundation work, or a trustee's sale on the calendar.

Quick answers for West Virginia

Who sells delinquent property tax liens in West Virginia?

The State Auditor. The sheriff certifies unpaid tracts to the Auditor on October 31, and the Auditor sells the tax liens at public auction.

Can I still redeem a West Virginia house after the tax lien is sold?

Yes, until a tax deed is issued, by paying the Auditor the taxes and charges with one percent monthly interest plus allowed costs. A primary residence may be redeemed in up to three installments on request.

Which office handles estates in West Virginia?

The county commission and the clerk of the county commission. If the appraisement shows $200,000 or less, or there is a single competent beneficiary, the estate can usually avoid referral to a fiduciary commissioner.

Does a power of attorney need a notary in West Virginia?

Yes. Code section 39B-1-105 requires the power of attorney to be acknowledged before a notary public or other person authorized to take acknowledgments.
