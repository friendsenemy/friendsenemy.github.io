Selling Mississippi Heirs Property When the House Belongs to the Whole Family

In Mississippi, a house that has passed down for two or three generations without a probate is common, and so are deeds still in a great-grandparent's name. That is heirs property: dozens of cousins may each own a small fraction, and any one of them can stall a sale. The good news is that Mississippi changed its partition law in 2020 to protect families in exactly this spot, and the Chancery Court is the single place most of these problems get solved.

Heirs property and Mississippi's 2020 partition law

Mississippi enacted the Uniform Partition of Heirs Property Act in 2020 (Senate Bill 2553). When a co-owner asks a court to partition land that qualifies as heirs property, the act gives the other relatives protections they did not have before: a court-determined value, the right to buy out the co-owner who wants a sale, and a preference for open-market sales over courthouse auctions when a sale is ordered.

Before that law, an outside buyer could pick up one cousin's small share and force the whole family's land to auction. Today that tactic is much harder, which is good for families. It also means a full partition case takes time.

What we look for first

  • The last recorded deed and every death since then.
  • Whether any estate was ever opened in Chancery Court for those owners.
  • Which heirs are living, which have died, and who their own heirs are.
  • Unpaid taxes or a tax sale on the parcel.

With that map, we can often buy the house from every living heir in one closing, or buy the shares of the heirs who want out. Read more about selling an inherited house when heirs disagree.

Chancery Court handles estates, land and divorce

Mississippi keeps the old Chancery Court system. Each county's Chancery Court and chancery clerk handle wills and estate administration, and also land disputes and divorce. For a family with a stuck house, that means one courthouse office holds most of the records you need.

The small estate shortcut is narrow. Under Mississippi Code section 91-7-322, a small estate affidavit can be used when the entire probate estate, less liens, is $75,000 or less and at least 30 days have passed since the death. The section is written for collecting debts owed to the person who died and for delivering personal property such as bank funds, stock and belongings. It does not set out a way to transfer a house, so real estate usually needs an estate opened in Chancery Court, or a court determination of heirs, before a buyer's title company will insure it.

Transfer-on-death deeds since July 1, 2020

The Mississippi Real Property Transfer-On-Death Act (Title 91, Chapter 27) took effect July 1, 2020. An owner can record a deed that passes the house to a named beneficiary at death without probate. If your parent signed and recorded one, the property may already belong to the beneficiary, subject to the act's rules. If the deed was signed but never recorded, talk to a Mississippi attorney before relying on it.

Back taxes: the two-year tax sale clock

Mississippi sells land for unpaid taxes at a county tax sale. After the sale, the owner has two years from the date of sale to redeem under section 27-45-3. Redeeming costs the taxes, plus 5% damages on the taxes, plus interest of 1.5% per month or any part of a month, plus costs. Once the two years run out, the tax buyer can move toward full title.

Heirs property is especially exposed here, because nobody feels responsible for the tax bill. If a family house has been sold for taxes, do the math early. Selling within the two years lets the redemption be paid from the sale proceeds at closing.

Foreclosure without a courtroom

Mississippi mortgages are usually deeds of trust with a power of sale, so foreclosure normally happens without a lawsuit. Under section 89-1-55, the trustee must advertise the sale for three consecutive weeks in a newspaper published in the county and post a notice at the county courthouse for the same three weeks. The sale is then held by public outcry. Those three weeks of notice are often the last practical window to sell and protect the equity.

Owners in jail and spouses in a divorce

If an owner or heir is incarcerated, the sale usually runs through a power of attorney or a signed deed. Either one must be notarized for the chancery clerk to record it, so the family needs to arrange a notary through the jail or prison before the closing date. Our page on selling when an owner is in jail or prison covers the steps.

Mississippi is not a community property state. In a divorce, the chancellor divides the marital property, and until there is an order or agreement both spouses generally need to sign a sale of a jointly owned house. For estate, divorce or criminal-case questions, please speak with an attorney licensed in Mississippi; Pages of Purpose LLC is not a law firm.

When a Mississippi listing agent is the smarter choice

If the title is already clean, the heirs all agree, the house shows well and no tax or foreclosure date is close, list it with a local agent. You will very likely net more than a cash or partnership sale. Quick answers for Mississippi

Has Mississippi adopted the Uniform Partition of Heirs Property Act?

Yes. Mississippi enacted it in 2020 through Senate Bill 2553, giving relatives a court-determined value and a chance to buy out a co-owner who wants a sale.

Which court handles estates in Mississippi?

Each county's Chancery Court handles wills and estate administration, and it also hears land disputes and divorce.

How long do I have to redeem Mississippi land sold for taxes?

Two years from the date of the tax sale, paying the taxes plus 5% damages, interest of 1.5% per month and costs.

Can a Mississippi small estate affidavit transfer a house?

The affidavit under section 91-7-322 applies to estates of $75,000 or less and is written for collecting debts and personal property, so a house usually needs an estate or heirship proceeding in Chancery Court.
