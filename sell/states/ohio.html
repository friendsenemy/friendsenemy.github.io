Selling an Ohio House When a Spouse, Estate or Lender Is Involved

Ohio is the rare state that still recognizes dower. Under Ohio Revised Code 2103.02, a spouse who has not given it up holds a right to a life estate in one-third of the real property the other spouse owned during the marriage. That right can survive a sale the other spouse made alone. It is why an Ohio title company asks for the spouse's signature on a deed even when only one name is on the title.

Dower: The Signature Buyers Look For

Dower ends at the owning spouse's death in most cases, but the statute keeps it alive for land the deceased spouse conveyed during the marriage without the other spouse giving it up, and for land that was mortgaged or sold involuntarily. It also ends when an absolute divorce is granted. A surviving spouse may take the share given by Ohio's inheritance law instead of dower.

In practice, if you are selling a house titled only in your name, expect the buyer to ask your spouse to sign and release dower. If you are separated and the spouse will not cooperate, the sale may have to wait for the divorce decree. Our page on selling a house during a divorce covers how we handle that timing, and the Ohio co-owned house guide covers partition and dower together.

Equal Division in Ohio Divorces

Under R.C. 3105.171(C)(1), Ohio courts divide marital property equally unless an equal division would be inequitable. When the house is the biggest marital asset, the decree often orders it sold and the proceeds split.

Probate Court: Release From Administration and Certificates of Transfer

Each Ohio county has a probate court. Ohio's simplified procedure, release from administration, is available when the estate's assets are worth $35,000 or less, or $100,000 or less when everything passes to a surviving spouse by will or by law (R.C. 2113.03).

When heirs are keeping the house rather than selling, the probate court issues a certificate of transfer to move the title from the estate to them (R.C. 2113.61). When the estate is selling, things depend on the will. If the will gives the executor a power of sale, the executor can sell under it; if not, the executor or administrator must use the statutory land-sale proceedings in R.C. Chapter 2127. That difference can add months.

Transfer on Death Designation Affidavits

Ohio owners can name beneficiaries for real estate with a transfer on death designation affidavit under R.C. 5302.22. It must be recorded with the county recorder before the owner dies. If one was recorded, the house may pass to the named beneficiary outside probate. Check the recorder's index first.

Sheriff Sales and Redemption Until Confirmation

Ohio foreclosures go through the court of common pleas and end in a sheriff's sale. Under R.C. 2329.33, the owner can still redeem by paying the judgment, costs and interest at any time before the court confirms the sale. Once the sale is confirmed, that right ends. If the house has equity, selling before the sale or before confirmation can protect it. See selling before a foreclosure sale.

Delinquent Taxes and Tax Certificates

An Ohio county treasurer may sell tax certificates on delinquent parcels, transferring the county's lien to an investor at public auction (R.C. 5721.30 to 5721.43). Ask the treasurer whether a certificate has been sold on your parcel and who holds it. Interest keeps adding to the amount owed, so an heir who inherits a parcel with a certificate against it should get a written payoff figure early and factor it into any price the family discusses.

Partition Among Heirs and Co-Owners

An Ohio partition case goes to the court of common pleas, which appoints a commissioner, and up to three on request, to divide the property (R.C. 5307.04). If the commissioners report that the house cannot be divided without manifest injury to its value, a party may elect to take the whole property by paying the others their share of the appraised value (R.C. 5307.09). Families often settle around that appraised number.

Owner in Jail: Record the Power of Attorney First

An Ohio power of attorney must be signed by the principal, and a signature acknowledged before a notary is presumed genuine (R.C. 1337.25). For a deed, R.C. 1337.04 requires the power of attorney to be recorded with the county recorder before the deed signed under it is recorded. Quick answers for Ohio

What is dower in Ohio?

Under R.C. 2103.02, a spouse who has not given it up holds a right to a life estate in one-third of the real property the other spouse owned during the marriage, which ends with an absolute divorce.

What is the limit for release from administration in Ohio?

$35,000 of estate assets, or $100,000 when everything passes to a surviving spouse by will or by law, under R.C. 2113.03.

Can I still save my Ohio house after the sheriff's sale?

Under R.C. 2329.33 the owner can redeem by paying the judgment, costs and interest any time before the court confirms the sale.

Does an Ohio power of attorney have to be recorded to sell a house?

Yes. R.C. 1337.04 requires a power of attorney used for a deed to be recorded with the county recorder before the deed signed under it is recorded.
