Selling an Inherited or Disputed House in Delaware

In Delaware, one court sits over both estates and partition fights

Delaware does something almost no other state does. The Court of Chancery oversees estates, through the Register of Wills in New Castle, Kent and Sussex counties, and it also hears partition petitions when co-owners cannot agree. Delaware law says the Register of Wills in each county acts as a clerk of the Court of Chancery. So if you inherited a house with a brother who will not sign, both the estate and the dispute end up in front of the same court system.

That matters for a family under stress, because the first question is rarely "what is the house worth?" It is "who has the legal right to sign the deed?" In Delaware, that answer usually runs through the Register of Wills.

Why the small estate shortcut rarely helps with a house

Delaware has a small estate affidavit, but it is narrow. The estate's property generally cannot exceed $30,000, you must wait at least 30 days after the death, and the affidavit states that the person who died did not own Delaware real estate alone or as a tenant in common. In plain terms: if Mom owned the house in her own name, the affidavit will not move it.

The exception is a house held jointly with right of survivorship. That passes to the surviving joint owner without probate. Check the last recorded deed with the county Recorder of Deeds before you assume anything.

For most inherited Delaware homes, someone has to open an estate with the Register of Wills and be appointed personal representative. That person, not the heirs as a group, usually signs the sale contract.

New: transfer on death deeds took effect in December 2025

Delaware adopted the Uniform Real Property Transfer on Death Act, effective December 5, 2025. An owner can now sign a TOD deed naming who gets the house, but it must be signed, witnessed, notarized and recorded with the Recorder of Deeds before the owner dies. A deed found in a drawer after the funeral does not count. Because the law is so new, most estates we see in 2026 still go through probate.

When heirs or co-owners in Delaware disagree

Any co-owner can file a partition petition in the Court of Chancery in the county where the land sits. The court can divide the land or order it sold. A single-family house almost never gets split, so in practice a contested partition usually ends in a court-ordered sale, with legal fees taken off the top before anyone is paid.

Many families settle before that point. One common path: the heir who wants out sells their share, and the remaining owners decide whether to buy it, sell together, or keep going. Closing costs that surprise Delaware sellers: the realty transfer tax

Delaware's realty transfer tax is one of the highest in the country. According to the Delaware Division of Revenue, the state rate is 2.5% where a local transfer tax also applies (3% where none does), and the tax is typically split equally between buyer and seller. Put a local tax on top and the combined figure can reach about 4% of the price. On an estate sale, that comes out of the heirs' proceeds, so build it into any math you do.

An owner who is incarcerated

A person in a Delaware jail or prison still owns their real estate and can sell it, but they need a valid power of attorney or must sign the deed themselves. Delaware's power of attorney statute requires the document to be in writing, dated, and signed in front of a notarial officer and one adult witness. The witness cannot be a relative of the person signing or someone who inherits from them. Getting a notary into a facility takes planning, so ask the facility how it handles legal paperwork. Our guide on getting a notary inside a jail or prison covers the general steps.

Divorce, foreclosure and back taxes in Delaware

Divorce

Delaware's Family Court divides marital property by equitable distribution, which means a fair split based on the facts, not automatically 50/50. Delaware also recognizes tenancy by the entirety for married owners, so a house titled to both spouses usually needs both signatures to sell.

Foreclosure

Mortgage foreclosure in Delaware goes through the Superior Court, and the sheriff's sale is not final until the court confirms it. For owner-occupied homes of one to four units, Delaware law blocks judgment and sale until the lender files an affidavit that the owner had a chance to apply for loss mitigation programs. That built-in court time is often enough to sell on your own terms.

Back taxes

Delaware counties collect unpaid property taxes through monition sales run by the sheriff. Kent County's published procedures give the owner 60 days from the confirmation date, not the sale date, to redeem, and the winning bidder is repaid the bid plus 15% of the price. Missing that window means losing the house and its equity.

Quick answers for Delaware

Can a Delaware small estate affidavit transfer a house?

Usually not. Delaware's small estate affidavit is limited to estates of $30,000 or less and states that the person who died did not own Delaware real estate alone or as a tenant in common, so most inherited houses need an estate opened with the Register of Wills.

Does Delaware allow transfer on death deeds?

Yes. Delaware's Uniform Real Property Transfer on Death Act took effect December 5, 2025, and the deed must be signed, witnessed, notarized and recorded with the county Recorder of Deeds before the owner dies.

Which Delaware court handles partition when co-owners disagree?

A partition petition is filed in the Court of Chancery in the county where the land is located. The court can divide the land or order it sold, and a house is usually sold.

How long do I have to redeem a Delaware house after a monition tax sale?

Kent County's published procedures allow 60 days from the confirmation date, not the sale date. The winning bidder is repaid the bid plus 15% of the price.
