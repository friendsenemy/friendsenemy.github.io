Selling a Complicated Arkansas House: Small Estates, Heirs and State Land Sales

In Arkansas, a small estate affidavit can include the house

Many states keep real estate out of their small-estate shortcuts. Arkansas does not. Under Ark. Code § 28-41-101, heirs can use a small estate affidavit when everything the deceased person owned, minus what was owed against it, is worth $100,000 or less. The homestead and the statutory allowances for a surviving spouse or minor children are left out of that count, but the affidavit must still describe and value any real property, including the homestead.

The steps, in plain terms:

  1. Wait at least 45 days after the death, and make sure nobody has asked the court to appoint a personal representative.
  2. File the affidavit with the probate clerk of the circuit court in the right county. The statute sets a $25 filing fee and $5 per certified copy; no court order is needed.
  3. If the estate includes real property, publish notice of the death and the affidavit within 30 days after filing. Creditors then have three months from the first publication to present claims.

Before you rely on the affidavit to sell, ask the buyer's title company what it will want to see. Ask specifically whether it wants the three-month claims period to run first.

Beneficiary deeds can skip the court entirely

Arkansas allows beneficiary deeds under Ark. Code § 18-12-608. The owner names who receives the property at death, and nothing passes until then. The deed is valid only if it was recorded with the county recorder before the owner died. If there are two, the one signed last controls, regardless of which was recorded first. A will cannot undo a beneficiary deed.

When a full estate is needed

Arkansas probate is handled by the circuit court. If the estate is over $100,000, the heirs disagree, or a will names an executor who needs authority to sell, a personal representative is appointed and signs the deed. Our overview of what happens to a house when there is no will explains who inherits when nobody planned ahead.

Heirs' property: Arkansas adopted the partition act in 2015

Land that has passed down for generations without a will is common in Arkansas. Each heir owns a fractional share, and any one of them can file for partition. Arkansas adopted the Uniform Partition of Heirs Property Act in 2015. In a qualifying case the court sets a value, offers the other relatives a chance to buy out the heir who wants a sale, and looks at dividing the land before selling it. If you are the heir who wants out, or one of several who cannot agree, read what to do when heirs disagree and whether every heir has to agree to a sale.

Back taxes go to the Commissioner of State Lands

Arkansas handles delinquent property in two stages. Taxes are due October 15. If they go unpaid, the county collector holds the property for a year and then certifies it to the Commissioner of State Lands, no later than July 1 of the following year. Once certified, title goes to the state.

The owner still gets a chance. The Commissioner must send notice of the right to redeem, and the sale cannot be set earlier than one year after certification. You can redeem by paying the taxes, penalties, interest and costs before 4:00 p.m. on the last business day before the sale. After a sale, the window to challenge it is short, generally 90 days after the property is conveyed.

If an inherited house shows up on the state's list, that one-year notice period is the time to sell or pay, not the time to wait.

Foreclosure: the statutory sale and no redemption afterward

Arkansas lenders can foreclose in court or use the Statutory Foreclosure Act, and most home foreclosures use the out-of-court route. The notice of default and intention to sell must be recorded at least 60 days before the sale and mailed to the borrower within 30 days after recording, and the sale must be advertised in a newspaper for four weeks. Under Ark. Code § 18-50-108, once a statutory sale happens, all rights of redemption end. Selling before the sale date is the only way to keep your equity.

Divorce: one-half is the starting point

Ark. Code § 9-12-315 says marital property is divided one-half to each spouse unless the court finds that would be inequitable, and the judge must explain any unequal split. Property owned before the marriage or received by gift or inheritance generally goes back to the spouse who brought it in. Until the decree or a signed settlement, plan on both spouses signing a sale.

Owner in jail or prison

A person in custody still owns the house and can sign a deed or a power of attorney. For a sale, the title company will want the power of attorney notarized so it can be recorded with the deed. Facilities schedule notaries differently, so ask the jail or prison before mailing papers. Our guide to selling a house while incarcerated covers the usual steps.

When an Arkansas agent will get you more

If the house is in good condition, every owner agrees, and no State Lands sale or foreclosure date is near, list it with a local Arkansas real estate agent. That will usually net more than a cash or partnership sale. Our offers fit stuck situations: missing heirs, a creditor period that has not run, a certified tax delinquency, or major repairs.

Quick answers for Arkansas

Can an Arkansas small estate affidavit transfer a house?

Yes. Under Ark. Code 28-41-101 the affidavit can include real property when the estate, minus encumbrances and not counting the homestead and statutory allowances, is $100,000 or less, at least 45 days after the death, with notice published within 30 days of filing.

Does Arkansas allow beneficiary deeds?

Yes. Under Ark. Code 18-12-608 an owner can name who receives the property at death, but the beneficiary deed is valid only if it was recorded with the county recorder before the owner died.

What happens to Arkansas property with unpaid taxes?

The county collector certifies it to the Commissioner of State Lands, no later than July 1 of the year after delinquency, and title goes to the state. The sale cannot be set earlier than one year after certification, and the owner can redeem until 4:00 p.m. on the last business day before the sale.

Can I redeem my Arkansas house after a statutory foreclosure sale?

No. Under Ark. Code 18-50-108, a statutory foreclosure sale ends all rights of redemption, so selling before the sale date is how an owner keeps equity.
