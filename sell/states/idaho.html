Selling an Idaho House When Community Property Is Involved

Idaho is a community property state, and that changes who owns the house

Idaho is one of the few community property states. Under Idaho Code 32-906, property either spouse acquires after marriage is community property, and income from property is generally community property too. A house bought during the marriage is usually owned one-half by each spouse, whatever name the mortgage statement shows.

That single rule drives most of what happens when a married Idaho owner dies, divorces or goes to prison. Before you plan a sale, find out when and how the house was acquired and how the deed is worded.

When a married owner dies

If there is no will, Idaho Code 15-2-102 gives the surviving spouse the deceased spouse's half of the community property, so the survivor ends up with all of it. Separate property follows different rules: the spouse takes all of it only if the person left no children and no parents, and otherwise one-half.

Idaho also lets married couples title real estate as community property with right of survivorship under Idaho Code 15-6-401. The deed must expressly say so. When one spouse dies, the house passes automatically to the survivor. Idaho's transfer-on-death statute, by contrast, covers securities registration, so for a house, survivorship wording on the deed is the common way to avoid probate.

Problems show up when the deed is silent, a second marriage is involved, or children from an earlier marriage expect a share of separate property. That is where careful title and family research pays off.

Probate and the $100,000 affidavit

Idaho estates are handled by the magistrate division of the district court in the county where the person lived. For smaller estates, Idaho Code 15-3-1201 allows an affidavit 30 days after the death when the probate estate, less liens and encumbrances, is worth $100,000 or less. The statute speaks to collecting personal property such as accounts, stock and debts owed; it does not set out a deed process for real estate. A house usually needs a personal representative or a court order to move title, and the house's value counts toward the $100,000 figure.

If you are a child of the owner and there is a surviving stepparent, settle the community-versus-separate question before you list or sign anything. Our page on selling an inherited or probate house covers the general steps.

Divorce: dividing community property in Idaho

In an Idaho divorce, the court divides community property, and the house is usually the largest piece. Families typically choose among three outcomes: one spouse buys out the other and refinances, the couple sells and splits the net, or the house is held for a time under the decree. A sale during the case usually needs both signatures and should follow whatever the court has ordered.

If the house needs repairs neither spouse can fund, or one spouse is stalling, our divorce home sale page explains how a cash or partnership offer can close around the court schedule.

Foreclosure under an Idaho deed of trust

Most Idaho homes are financed with a deed of trust, which lets the trustee sell without a lawsuit. Idaho Code 45-1506 sets the timeline: the notice of default is recorded first, the notice of sale is mailed at least 120 days before the trustee's sale, and it is published once a week for four weeks, with the last notice at least 30 days before the sale. Those 120 days are your window to sell, refinance or reinstate. Our guide to selling before a foreclosure sale covers the general options.

Back taxes: Idaho's three-year tax deed

Idaho counties do not sell tax liens to investors. Instead, under Idaho Code 63-1005, if delinquent taxes are not redeemed within three years of the delinquency date, the county tax collector issues a tax deed to the county. Before that happens, the owner must get notice by certified mail between two and five months before the deed date, or by newspaper publication if the mail comes back. If you get that letter, the clock is short.

Co-owners, heirs and someone in custody

Partition

When co-owners who are not married disagree, Idaho's partition statutes in Title 6, Chapter 5 let a court divide the land or order it sold through court-appointed referees. That chapter does not contain the heirs-property buyout protections some states have adopted, so a contested case can end in a referee's sale.

An owner who is incarcerated

An Idaho owner in jail or prison still owns their share. They can sign a power of attorney, and under Idaho Code 15-12-105 a signature acknowledged before a notary is presumed genuine, which is what title companies want. If the house is community property, the spouse on the outside usually cannot sell it alone. See our page on selling when an owner is incarcerated.

When an Idaho agent will get you more

If the house is in good shape, both spouses or all heirs agree, and nobody is facing a trustee's sale or tax deed, a local Idaho listing agent will usually get a higher net price than an investor. Boise, Coeur d'Alene and Idaho Falls all have active retail buyers.

Quick answers for Idaho

Is a house bought during marriage community property in Idaho?

Usually yes. Idaho Code 32-906 makes property acquired after marriage by either spouse community property, so each spouse generally owns half.

What happens to community property in Idaho if a spouse dies without a will?

Under Idaho Code 15-2-102, the surviving spouse receives the deceased spouse's half of the community property. Couples can also title real estate as community property with right of survivorship under 15-6-401 so it passes automatically.

What is Idaho's small estate affidavit limit?

Idaho Code 15-3-1201 allows an affidavit 30 days after death when the probate estate, less liens and encumbrances, is $100,000 or less. It is aimed at personal property, so a house usually needs a personal representative or court order.

How much notice does Idaho require before a trustee's sale?

Under Idaho Code 45-1506, the notice of sale must be mailed at least 120 days before the sale and published once a week for four weeks, with the last notice at least 30 days before.
