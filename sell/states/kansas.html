Kansas Houses With More Than One Owner: Partition, Heirs and Sales

Kansas gives co-owners something many states do not: a built-in chance to keep the house at a fair price before a court sells it. Under K.S.A. 60-1003, when a house cannot be split up physically, any owner may elect to take it at the value set by court-appointed commissioners. And the sheriff may not sell it for less than two-thirds of that appraisal. If you share a house with siblings, an ex or a relative who will not cooperate, that rule shapes every conversation about buying out, selling or going to court.

How a Kansas partition case actually runs

  1. One owner files a partition case in district court. The judge first decides what share each person owns.
  2. The judge appoints three commissioners to divide the property. A single house usually cannot be split without serious harm, so the commissioners appraise it instead and report back.
  3. Any party can file objections to the report. The judge can approve it, change it or order more steps.
  4. Within a deadline the judge sets, one or more owners may elect to take the property at the appraised value.
  5. If nobody elects, or the elections conflict, the judge orders the sheriff to sell it, and the sale cannot be for less than two-thirds of the appraisal.

Costs, attorney fees and title expenses are divided among the owners by their shares, and the judge may refuse partition entirely if it would cause extraordinary hardship. Kansas has not adopted the Uniform Partition of Heirs Property Act. A 2024 bill to adopt it died without advancing, so the general partition statute is what families work with.

The two-thirds floor protects you from a fire-sale price, but court costs and months of waiting still come out of everyone's share. That is why most co-owners settle: one buys the others out, or all of them sell together. If you want out and the others do not, read our page on selling your share when a co-owner won't sell and the overview of partition lawsuit costs and timelines.

Married couples: no tenancy by the entirety in Kansas

Kansas abolished tenancy by the entirety in 1891. Under K.S.A. 58-501, a deed to two or more people, spouses included, creates a tenancy in common unless the deed clearly says joint tenancy. Read the deed closely. The words decide whether a surviving spouse owns the whole house or shares it with the other spouse's heirs.

Inherited Kansas houses: TOD deeds and the six-month descent route

Kansas lets owners pass a house outside probate with a transfer-on-death deed under K.S.A. 59-3501. The owner signs and records a deed naming a beneficiary, and ownership passes at death. If a parent recorded one, the beneficiary may not need an estate at all. Check with the county register of deeds before assuming probate is needed.

Without a TOD deed, Kansas has a route many states lack: a petition to determine descent under K.S.A. 59-2250. Once the person has been dead more than six months, and no will has been filed or probated and no administration opened, an heir can ask the court to decide who inherited the real estate. The resulting decree gives a title company a clear answer about who signs the deed.

Kansas probate cases are handled through the district court. When a full estate is needed, the Kansas Simplified Estates Act lets the court choose between simplified and supervised administration, weighing the estate's size, the family relationships, whether the estate can pay its debts, what the heirs want and the cost.

The small estate affidavit under K.S.A. 59-1507b covers personal property when probate assets are $75,000 or less. It does not transfer a house.

An owner serving time in Kansas

An inmate can still sell through an agent. Under K.S.A. 58-652, a durable power of attorney must be signed by the owner, dated and acknowledged before a notary. If the owner is physically unable to sign but competent, an adult designee may sign in the owner's presence, with the owner giving the direction in front of the notary. How a notary reaches someone inside depends on the jail or prison, so ask the facility first. See our page on selling when the owner is in jail or prison.

Kansas divorce: ownership shifts when the case is filed

Kansas divides property in a divorce by fairness rather than a fixed formula. One detail stands out: under K.S.A. 23-2801, property owned by either spouse becomes marital property at the moment one spouse files, whether titled to one spouse or both. From that day each spouse has a vested interest that the court sizes in the final decree, so a sale during the case usually needs both signatures or a court order.

Foreclosure and the Kansas redemption period

Kansas foreclosures go through the courts. Under K.S.A. 60-2414, the owner can usually redeem for 12 months after the sheriff's sale. If the default came before a third of the original loan was paid, the court sets three months instead, and for abandoned property the period can be shortened or eliminated. Redeeming means paying the full sale price plus costs. Selling before the sheriff's sale protects more of the equity.

Unpaid Kansas property taxes

Kansas does not sell tax certificates to investors. Delinquent land is bid off to the county. If it is still unredeemed after the redemption period, generally two years and three for a homestead, the county files a foreclosure suit and the property goes to a sheriff's auction. An owner can usually still redeem until the day before that sale by paying the taxes, interest and costs.

Quick answers for Kansas

Can a co-owner keep a Kansas house in a partition case?

Yes. Under K.S.A. 60-1003, if the house can't be divided, any owner may elect to take it at the commissioners' appraised value, and a sheriff's sale cannot go below two-thirds of that value.

Does Kansas allow transfer-on-death deeds?

Yes. K.S.A. 59-3501 lets an owner record a deed that transfers the house to a named beneficiary at death, without probate.

How can Kansas heirs get title without full probate?

If the person has been dead more than six months and no will or administration has been filed, an heir can petition to determine descent under K.S.A. 59-2250, and the decree shows who inherited the real estate.

How long is the redemption period after a Kansas foreclosure?

Usually 12 months after the sheriff's sale under K.S.A. 60-2414, or three months if the default came before a third of the original loan was paid.
