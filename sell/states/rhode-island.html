Selling a Rhode Island Home When Probate Runs Through Town Hall

Rhode Island handles estates differently from almost every other state. Probate is not run by a county or state court. Each city and town has its own probate court with its own probate judge, and the city or town clerk serves as the clerk of that court. New Shoreham, on Block Island, goes further: its town council sits as the probate court. So the first question for a family with an inherited house is simple: which town did your parent live in?

From there, Rhode Island law adds a second step that surprises people. An executor or administrator cannot just sign a deed. Under R.I. Gen. Laws § 33-19-3, the probate court that issued the letters grants authority to sell real estate on a petition that describes the property and states the reasons. This page explains how that works, along with partition, divorce, foreclosure and tax sales in Rhode Island.

Your Parent's Town Decides Where the Estate Is Filed

The probate court sits in the city or town where the person lived, not where the house is. If your mother lived in Warwick but owned a rental in Pawtucket, the estate opens in Warwick.

The $15,000 small estate rule

Rhode Island's small estate procedure, § 33-24-1, is narrow. It applies only when the estate is made up entirely of personal property worth $15,000 or less, and it is filed with the probate court of the town where the person lived. A house automatically puts the estate outside that rule, so real estate goes through a regular probate.

The Petition to Sell Real Estate

Once letters are issued, the executor files a petition under § 33-19-3 asking the probate court for authority to sell. The petition names the property and gives the facts supporting the sale. The court can approve a cash sale or a sale on credit, and related sections cover public or private sale, notice of auctions and the conveyance.

In practice, this means a buyer's contract on a Rhode Island estate house usually depends on the court's approval. We plan for that from the first conversation, and our offers are written so the executor is not stuck if the hearing takes a few extra weeks.

Heirs Who Want Different Things

When a house passes to several heirs, any co-owner can ask the court to partition it under Chapter 34-15 of the General Laws. The court can set off shares or order a sale and divide the proceeds, with a share held back for any owner who cannot be found. Costs of the partition are split among the owners and can become a lien on the property.

Partition is expensive and slow. A buyout between relatives, or a sale of one heir's share, is often a better result. Our plain-English guide to partition lawsuits and our page on selling when heirs disagree lay out the choices.

One Rhode Island detail matters for co-owners: under § 34-3-1, a deed to two or more people, including a married couple, is presumed to create a tenancy in common unless it clearly creates a joint tenancy or a right of survivorship. Read the deed's exact words before assuming a surviving owner already owns everything.

Divorce: Rhode Island Assigns Property

Rhode Island does not split property by community property rules. Under § 15-5-16.1, the divorce court may assign part of one spouse's estate to the other, looking at the length of the marriage, each spouse's contributions and any waste of assets. Property owned before the marriage and inheritances from third parties generally cannot be assigned, though growth in value from a spouse's efforts can be. Our page on selling a house during a divorce covers timing and how proceeds are held.

Foreclosure by Power of Sale

Most Rhode Island foreclosures happen outside court under the power of sale in the mortgage. Under § 34-27-4, an individual consumer borrower must be mailed written notice by certified mail at least 30 days before the first newspaper notice. The notice then runs once a week for three weeks, with the first publication at least 21 days before the sale.

That gives a homeowner roughly two months from the mailed notice to the auction. It is enough time to sell if you start right away, and selling usually keeps far more equity than letting the sale happen.

Tax Sales: One Year, Then Superior Court

When taxes go unpaid, the city or town collector can sell the property at a tax sale. The owner can still redeem by repaying the buyer, but it gets more expensive the longer it waits. Under § 44-9-19, the penalty is 10 percent of the purchase price if redeemed within six months, plus 1 percent for each month after that. Section 44-9-21 adds interest at 1 percent a month.

After one year from the tax sale, the buyer can petition the Superior Court to foreclose the right of redemption under § 44-9-25. Once that happens, the owner can lose the house entirely. If you see a tax sale notice, act inside that first year.

When an Owner or Heir Is Incarcerated

A person in custody can still sell. The usual tool is a power of attorney naming a trusted relative, signed before a notary so a title company will accept it and it can be recorded with the deed in the town's land records. Ask the facility early how residents reach a notary. In an estate, an heir in prison still has to be notified and still shares in the proceeds.

Quick answers for Rhode Island

Which court handles probate in Rhode Island?

Each Rhode Island city and town has its own probate court, and the city or town clerk serves as its clerk. In New Shoreham the town council sits as the probate court.

Does an executor need court approval to sell a house in Rhode Island?

Yes. Under R.I. Gen. Laws 33-19-3, the probate court that issued the letters grants authority to sell real estate on a petition describing the property and the reasons for the sale.

Can Rhode Island's small estate procedure transfer a house?

No. The small estate procedure in 33-24-1 applies only when the estate is entirely personal property worth $15,000 or less, so an estate with a house needs regular probate.

How long do I have after a Rhode Island tax sale?

After one year from the tax sale, the buyer can petition the Superior Court to foreclose the right of redemption. Redeeming costs a 10 percent penalty within six months plus 1 percent per month after that, and interest at 1 percent a month.
