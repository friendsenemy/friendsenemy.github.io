Selling a DC House With Tenants, Heirs or Tax Trouble

Before You Sell a Rented DC House, Check the Tenants' Right to Buy

The District has a rule most cities do not. Under the Tenant Opportunity to Purchase Act, tenants in a property being sold must be offered the first chance to buy it. A 2018 amendment exempted single-family homes, including a house with one accessory unit or a single condo unit, with one important exception: elderly tenants (62 or older) and tenants with disabilities who signed a lease by March 31, 2018 and moved in by April 15, 2018 kept a limited right to buy or to assign that right.

Even when the exemption applies, the seller has paperwork to do. Title companies typically expect:

  1. A notice delivered to each tenant or occupant by a trackable method, with copies sent the same day to the Office of the Tenant Advocate and the Department of Housing and Community Development (DHCD).
  2. Either a signed tenant acknowledgment that no TOPA rights apply, or a 20-day wait and a DHCD review letter.
  3. If a tenant claims elderly or disability status, a formal offer of sale and another response period.

Leaving this out is one of the most common reasons a DC closing gets delayed. If you are a landlord ready to sell, see our page for tired landlords.

No County and No State: One Probate Division

Washington, DC has no county courts and no state layer, so every estate goes to the Probate Division of the Superior Court of the District of Columbia. The Strengthening Probate Administration Amendment Act raised the small estate ceiling in D.C. Code § 20-351 from $40,000 to $80,000, effective March 21, 2025. Almost any DC house is worth far more than that, so selling an inherited home normally means opening a standard estate and having a personal representative appointed. When relatives disagree, our guide on whether all heirs have to agree to sell explains the options.

DC Property Tax Depends on the Class of Property

The Office of Tax and Revenue sets rates by class. An occupied house is Class 1 at $0.85 per $100 of assessed value, and the rate rises to $1.00 on Class 1B value above $2.558 million. The homestead deduction for an owner-occupied home is $95,100 for tax year 2027, and a homestead's taxable assessment cannot rise more than 10% a year. A worked example:

  • Assessed value: $650,000
  • Less homestead deduction: $650,000 − $95,100 = $554,900
  • Tax: $554,900 ÷ 100 × $0.85 = $4,716.65

Now the same house if it is left empty after a death and the District classifies it as vacant, Class 3, at $5.00 per $100 with no homestead deduction: $650,000 ÷ 100 × $5.00 = $32,500 a year. A house classified as blighted, Class 4, is taxed at $10.00 per $100. For heirs holding an empty rowhouse, that difference can decide whether to sell now.

Foreclosure Requires Mediation or a Lawsuit

A DC lender chooses between two paths. In the nonjudicial path, the owner receives a notice of default with a mediation election form, a loss mitigation application and a $50 fee to send to the Department of Insurance, Securities and Banking. The lender cannot go forward until DISB issues a mediation certificate, and the notice of intention to foreclose must go out at least 30 days before the auction. In the judicial path, the lender sues in Superior Court and the owner has 20 days to answer. Either way, there is usually time to sell. Our foreclosure help page explains the steps.
