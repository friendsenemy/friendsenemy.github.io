Selling an Arnold House Between the Severn and the Magothy

Arnold is unincorporated Anne Arundel County on the Broadneck Peninsula, the strip of land between the Severn River and the Magothy River north of Annapolis. A lot of Arnold houses sit on coves and creeks that feed those two rivers, and that one fact shapes more sales here than anything else: land within 1,000 feet of tidal water falls under Maryland's Critical Area law, which can limit clearing, additions, new decks and how much of a lot can be covered by hard surface.

What the Critical Area means for an older Arnold waterfront house

If a parent bought a small cottage on a creek decades ago and it now needs a roof, a new septic field or a full gut, the next owner will want to know what can actually be built there. Buyers using a mortgage often pause when they learn a planned addition may need a variance, and some walk away. A buyer who prices that uncertainty in up front can close without asking you to resolve it first. That does not make the land worth less as land; it just changes who is willing to take on the paperwork.

  • Expansion plans may be capped by impervious-surface limits.
  • Trees near the shoreline can carry replanting requirements if removed.
  • Piers and bulkheads have their own permits, separate from the house.

Probate goes through Annapolis

When an Arnold owner dies, the estate is opened with the Anne Arundel County Register of Wills in Annapolis, and disputes go to the Orphans' Court there. If the estate is $50,000 or less (or $100,000 when a surviving spouse is the only heir), Maryland allows a simpler small-estate process, though few Arnold houses fall under that line on their own. In a regular estate, the personal representative usually signs the deed. If two or three siblings inherit and only one wants to keep the place, our guide to what happens when Maryland heirs can't agree walks through the options short of a partition suit.

The tax bill on an Arnold house in FY2027

Because Arnold is not an incorporated town, there is no separate town tax. Owners pay the county rate of $0.968 plus the state rate of $0.112, a total of $1.080 per $100 of assessed value for July 2026 through June 2027.

Take a house assessed at $415,000: $415,000 / 100 x $1.080 = about $4,482 a year, or roughly $374 a month that keeps coming due whether anyone lives there or not.

Anne Arundel's Homestead Tax Credit caps the yearly increase in a homeowner's taxable assessment at 2%, one of the tighter caps in the state. A long-time owner may be paying tax on an assessment well below market. When that owner passes, an heir who doesn't live there generally loses the credit, so the bill can climb once the cap no longer applies.

When you should list instead

If the Arnold house is in decent shape, the heirs or co-owners agree, and nobody is up against a deadline, a local listing agent will very likely get you more money than a cash buyer. Waterfront and water-access homes on the Broadneck draw retail buyers who will pay for the setting. We would tell you that on the phone.

A cash offer or partnership offer from Pages of Purpose makes more sense when something is in the way: a house that won't pass a lender's inspection, a failing septic system, back taxes, an estate with heirs pulling in different directions, a co-owner who won't sign, a divorce, or an owner who is incarcerated. A partnership offer can split the upside of a fix-and-sell with you instead of paying a lower flat price. A simple sale can close in as few as 7 days; a complicated one takes as long as the title takes.

A few honest questions to ask any buyer

  • Will the price change after the title search? Ask in writing.
  • Who pays the transfer and recordation taxes at closing?
  • Can I take what I want from the house before closing?
