Selling a Potomac Home After a Death, a Split, or a Stalled Estate

Big Lots, Older Owners, and Estates That Run Through Rockville

Potomac is an unincorporated part of Montgomery County along the Potomac River, with the C&O Canal towpath and Great Falls at its edge. Much of the housing sits on large lots, and many of those houses were bought decades ago by people who stayed. When an owner here passes away, the estate is opened with the Montgomery County Register of Wills in Rockville, and the Orphans' Court there supervises the personal representative who eventually signs the deed.

That combination, a valuable house and a long-time owner, is where most of the harder Potomac sales come from: several adult children spread across different states, a will that names co-executors who rarely talk, or a property that needs a new roof, a new septic field, and thirty years of updates before a retail buyer would look at it.

What Carrying a Potomac House Costs Each Month

Because Potomac is not an incorporated town, there is no municipal tax layer. Owners pay the Montgomery County rate plus the state rate only. For FY2027 that is county $0.6706 + state $0.112 = $0.7826 per $100 of assessed value.

On a house assessed at $650,000, that works out to about $5,087 a year, or roughly $424 a month. Add insurance on a vacant dwelling, utilities to keep pipes from freezing, and lawn care on an acre or more, and an empty estate home can quietly drain an inheritance while siblings decide what to do.

Montgomery County's Homestead Tax Credit caps the yearly rise in the taxable assessment of an owner-occupied home at 10%. That cap belongs to the person who lives in the house. Once a parent dies and the house passes to children who live elsewhere, the credit generally falls away, and the bill can climb toward the full assessed value at the next reassessment.

Small estates are rare here

Maryland lets families use a simplified small-estate process when the probate assets are worth $50,000 or less, or $100,000 if the surviving spouse is the only heir. With Potomac values, a house held in the deceased person's name almost always pushes the estate past those limits, so expect regular administration in Rockville.

When the Family Cannot Agree

Disagreement is the most common reason a Potomac estate stalls. One heir wants to move in, one wants top dollar, one wants out this month. Options that sometimes break the deadlock:

  • One heir buys out the others at an agreed price, with the estate selling the house to that heir.
  • A buyer purchases the whole property, and the personal representative distributes the net proceeds under the will.
  • A buyer purchases only the share of an heir who wants out, leaving the rest of the family to decide later.

When a Cash Sale Is the Wrong Choice

Plenty of Potomac houses should simply be listed. If the property is updated, the heirs or owners are on the same page, and nobody faces a deadline, a local listing agent will usually put more money in your pocket than any cash buyer, because the open market pays for move-in condition. A cash or partnership offer makes more sense when the house needs major work, title or estate problems scare off lenders, or the people involved need a single decision they can all sign.

Practical Steps Before You Call Anyone

  1. Find out whether an estate has been opened and who was appointed personal representative.
  2. Pull the latest assessment from the state's SDAT records so you know the tax figure you are carrying.
  3. Confirm whether the house is on public water and sewer or on a well and septic system, since that affects inspections.
  4. List every person on the deed and every heir, with how to reach them.

For questions about wills, co-executors, or court approval, talk with a Maryland estate attorney; this page is general information, not legal advice.
